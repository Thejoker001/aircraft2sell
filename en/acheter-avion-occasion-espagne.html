The Spanish used aircraft market

Spain benefits from year-round favourable flying weather and an active network of airfields, particularly around Madrid, Barcelona, Seville and Valencia. The light aviation market there is underpinned by a solid base of flying clubs and flight schools, with a used aircraft supply made up mainly of classic touring single-engines (Cessna 172, Piper PA-28) and light sport/ULM aircraft, plus a growing segment of higher-performance touring aircraft (Cirrus, Diamond) favoured for long trips to the rest of Europe.

Listings circulate through specialist marketplaces, regional flying clubs (Real Federación Aeronáutica Española and its regional federations) and word of mouth at airfields. As elsewhere in Europe, the asking price remains a starting point for negotiation, particularly for an aircraft that has been on the market for a long time.

Average prices by aircraft type

Price ranges in Spain broadly align with those seen elsewhere in the European Union for comparable aircraft. Good overall airframe condition and high remaining engine potential weigh more heavily on the price than the year of manufacture alone.

Tip — Always compare at equivalent engine potential (airframe hours remaining before TBO): this is the factor that most influences an aircraft's actual price, far more than its year of manufacture.

Registration with AESA

Any aircraft based and operated in Spain must be registered with the Agencia Estatal de Seguridad Aérea (AESA), under the EC- prefix. For a private purchase of an aircraft already registered in Spain, the transfer consists of updating the holder in the matrícula register. For an aircraft imported from another country, prior deregistration from the register of origin is required, as with any intra-European transfer.

  • Valid certificate of airworthiness and ARC — check before signing, as the expiry date determines the first flight under your name.
  • Bill of sale — dated, signed by both parties, stating the serial number.
  • Certificate of liability insurance — required before first use under the new registration.
Good to know — Common EASA rules govern transfers between member states and simplify them compared with an import from outside the European Union. Allow generally 2 to 8 weeks depending on how complete the file submitted to AESA is.

VAT (IVA) and taxation

A private sale in Spain is generally not subject to IVA (Spanish VAT, standard rate of 21%). However, a sale made by a professional or a company may be subject to it, and an aircraft imported from a country outside the European Union remains subject to import IVA calculated on its customs value. It is advisable to have your tax situation confirmed before purchasing, particularly if the aircraft has already changed country of registration several times.

SituationTax treatment
Private sale between EU residentsGenerally outside the scope of IVA
Sale by a professionalIVA potentially applicable (21%)
Import from outside the EU (e.g. USA)Import IVA, standard rate

Pre-purchase inspection and documents

Before signing, a pre-purchase inspection by a certified mechanic independent of the seller remains the best protection against unpleasant surprises. It covers the condition of the airframe, the consistency of the engine logbook, the maintenance history and the avionics' compliance with current European requirements (transponder, 8.33 kHz radio).

  • Up-to-date airframe, engine and propeller logbooks, with no chronological gaps.
  • Maintenance invoice history for the last three years.
  • No undeclared structural damage (verifiable via the maintenance records and a thorough visual inspection).
Point of caution — Never finalise a purchase without having seen the original logbooks and had their consistency checked by a professional: it is the best indicator of how well the aircraft has actually been maintained.

Pitfalls to avoid

The most common mistakes when buying in Spain echo those seen elsewhere in Europe: neglecting to verify deregistration from the previous register for an imported aircraft, underestimating the time and costs of the AESA transfer, or signing without having the exact condition of the optional equipment listed in the spec sheet confirmed in writing.
