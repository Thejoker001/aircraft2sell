The Greek used aircraft market

Greece enjoys a climate particularly favourable to light aviation, with abundant sunshine and generally mild flying conditions for much of the year. The country has a network of airfields and flying clubs spread across the mainland and several islands, shaping a general aviation scene geared both towards air touring and inter-island travel.

Light aviation activity remains concentrated around a handful of main hubs: the Athens area, where several flying clubs operate from secondary airfields, and Thessaloniki in the north of the country. Some islands also have airfields used by recreational aviation, alongside the major commercial airports. The market nevertheless remains modest in size compared with Western European markets, with a more limited number of aircraft in circulation.

Many Greek buyers and sellers naturally look at listings from other European countries, particularly Italy or Central Europe, to widen their choice when buying or to find a buyer when selling. Local maintenance networks exist but can be less dense than in the major European aviation countries, which justifies extra vigilance over parts availability and proximity to an approved workshop.

A point to bear in mind throughout this guide: Greece is a member of the European Union and uses the euro, which simplifies price comparisons with listings from other eurozone countries compared with markets using a different currency.

Average prices by aircraft type

Prices in Greece for an aircraft of equivalent condition, airframe hours and remaining engine potential remain broadly comparable with those seen elsewhere in Southern Europe. The smaller number of aircraft available locally can sometimes reduce the room for negotiation on the most sought-after models.

Registration with the HCAA

Any aircraft based and operated in Greece must be registered with the Hellenic Civil Aviation Authority (HCAA, Greece's civil aviation authority), under the SX- prefix. For an aircraft already registered in Greece, a private purchase results in an update of the holder of the certificate of registration with this authority. For an aircraft coming from abroad, prior deregistration from the register of origin is generally required before any new Greek registration.

  • Valid, up-to-date certificate of airworthiness, recognised by the HCAA.
  • Previous certificate of registration and proof of deregistration if the aircraft comes from another country.
  • Certificate of liability insurance covering operation in Greece.

VAT and taxation within the European Union

As Greece is a member of the European Union, buying an aircraft from another member state falls in principle under the intra-Community acquisition regime rather than importation, which simplifies customs procedures compared with a purchase outside the EU. This does not, however, remove the need to clarify the VAT treatment applicable to the transaction, which depends on the seller's status (private individual or VAT-registered professional) and the intended use of the aircraft.

The standard Greek VAT rate is generally quoted at around 24% — this general figure should always be confirmed for the specific case of an aircraft, as exemption or calculation rules can vary depending on whether the transaction is between private individuals, through a professional, or involves an aircraft that has already cleared customs and been taxed within the Union. It is essential to have the exact tax treatment confirmed by the HCAA and by the Greek tax authority (Independent Authority for Public Revenue) before signing, rather than relying on unverified rates for this specific case.

Key takeaway — Always request a written quote for the applicable VAT treatment from a professional before committing, particularly if the aircraft changes country as part of the transaction.

Pre-purchase inspection and documents

As with any used aircraft purchase, a pre-purchase inspection carried out by an approved workshop independent of the seller remains the best protection against unpleasant surprises. In Greece, the hot climate and frequent exposure to sea air for aircraft based near the coast or on the islands justify particular attention to corrosion, the condition of seals and UV-exposed trim, and the cabin air-conditioning system where fitted.

Documents to gather and check systematically:

  • Complete aircraft logbook, with no gaps in the history.
  • Report of the last annual inspection (Part-M / Part-ML) and any major engine overhauls.
  • Valid certificate of airworthiness and history of airworthiness directives (ADs) applied.
  • No outstanding dispute or lien on the aircraft, to be checked with the HCAA.
  • Storage conditions (enclosed hangar or outdoors) and any signs of corrosion linked to marine exposure.

Pitfalls to avoid

The more limited number of aircraft available locally can push buyers to rush a purchase decision: nevertheless, take the time to compare with listings from other Southern European countries before committing. Be wary of incomplete logbooks, particularly for aircraft that have moved between several islands or countries, and of prolonged outdoor parking by the sea without suitable protection against corrosion.

Key takeaway — A price noticeably below market should always be questioned: reduced engine potential, corrosion linked to marine exposure, upcoming airframe work or incomplete documentation are the most frequent causes.
