Financing a business jet: leasing, finance lease and fractional ownership

An investment on a different scale

A business jet represents a budget on a completely different scale from a light aircraft: anywhere from a few million euros for a used light jet to tens of millions for a recent long-range aircraft. At this level of commitment, the choice of financing structure directly affects the aircraft's profitability, its tax treatment and its flexibility of use. Three main families of solutions coexist on the European market: the operating lease, the finance lease with a purchase option, and co-ownership (or fractional ownership for the more formally structured arrangements).

The right choice depends above all on the intended use: an operator running the aircraft for passenger transport will typically get better value from an operating lease or a finance lease, while an executive who flies occasionally for personal use will usually find more value in co-ownership.

Operating lease

An operating lease involves renting the aircraft for a fixed term, generally 3 to 10 years, with no obligation to buy at the end of the contract. The leasing company remains the owner of the aircraft and leases it to the operator against monthly or quarterly rent.

  • No significant upfront capital required, which preserves the company's cash flow.
  • Lease payments are generally treated as operating expenses, off balance sheet.
  • Flexibility at the end of the contract: return the aircraft, renew the lease, or move to a newer aircraft.
Good to know — Operating leases are particularly well suited to charter operators and large companies that want to renew their fleet regularly without carrying the resale risk.

Finance lease (lease with purchase option)

An aircraft finance lease works differently: the financing company buys the aircraft and leases it to the client with a purchase option at a price fixed at signing, generally exercisable at the end of the contract. The lease payments cover both the amortisation of the aircraft and the cost of the financing.

This structure is often favoured by dealers, operators and companies intending to become owners eventually. A finance lease allows the investment to be spread over 7 to 15 years depending on the aircraft's age, with an initial deposit generally between 10 and 20% of the jet's value.

Ownership at the end of the contractNo (aircraft returned)Yes, via the purchase option

Co-ownership and fractional ownership

Co-ownership involves sharing ownership of an aircraft between several individuals or companies, each holding a share proportional to their expected use. Fractional ownership, a more formalised arrangement, is generally managed by a specialised operator who handles the aircraft, maintenance and availability according to a guaranteed number of hours per co-owner.

  • Gives access to a recent jet for a fraction of the full purchase price.
  • Fixed costs (hangar, insurance, scheduled maintenance) are split between co-owners.
  • A detailed co-ownership agreement is essential: booking priority, resale rules, and how unexpected costs are shared.

This financing model particularly suits executives and private individuals who fly between 50 and 200 hours a year — not enough to justify sole ownership, but too much to rely solely on occasional charter.

Operating costs to anticipate

Whatever financing structure you choose, the operating costs of a business jet represent a substantial budget that must be factored in from the start:

ItemOrder of magnitude
Crew (type-qualified pilots)Salaries + recurrent training
Scheduled maintenanceSeveral hundred thousand euros a year depending on the type
Hangar and parkingVaries by airport, often several thousand euros a month
Insurance (hull + liability)Depends on the insured value and the use
FuelDepends heavily on the aircraft type and hours flown

These figures vary enormously depending on the jet model, its age and its use — they should always be quantified with an operator or a specialised fleet manager before any financing decision, and should never be underestimated in the financing plan.

Building a solid file

Organisations specialising in aviation financing (dedicated banks, specialised leasing companies, financing brokers) systematically require a detailed file: the financial position of the individual or company applying, the planned use of the aircraft (annual hours, type of mission), the complete technical history of the target aircraft (airframe, engines, avionics), and a forecast maintenance plan.

Tip — Always have the financing structure validated by a tax specialist and a lawyer experienced in business aviation before signing: the structure chosen (holding company, country of registration, VAT regime) has a direct impact on the project's profitability.
