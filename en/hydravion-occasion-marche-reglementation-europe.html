Used seaplanes: market and regulations in Europe

Used seaplanes in Europe: market, specific regulations, pilot qualification and points to watch before buying an amphibious or float-equipped aircraft.

A small but active European market

Seaplanes remain a niche segment of the European light aviation market, far from the volumes seen for conventional light aircraft or microlights. Most of the active fleet is made up of historic aircraft (Cessna 172s or 206s fitted with removable floats, Found Bush Hawks, Lake Buccaneer flying boats) alongside a more recent wave of amphibious kitplanes (Icon A5, some Searey models) which have reignited interest in the activity over the past fifteen years or so.

Scandinavia, Finland, and certain lake-rich regions (the Alps, Scotland) account for most of the active bases in Europe, thanks to a longer tradition of flying off water and more accommodating local regulations. In France, the activity remains marginal but is organised around a handful of clubs and a limited number of approved bodies of water.

Floats or hull: two distinct families

Two architectures coexist on the used market, with very different implications for buyers:

  • Floatplane — a standard land aircraft fitted with removable floats. It keeps its base certification and can often be switched back to wheel configuration depending on the season, making it the most flexible option for mixed use.
  • Flying boat — the airframe itself acts as the hull, sometimes with retractable gear to allow landing and taking off on both water and runway (amphibious configuration). The hull's structure and maintenance require specific expertise, less widely available among general-aviation mechanics.

The choice between the two depends mainly on intended use: removable floats suit occasional use on an aircraft you already own, while a flying boat is suited to dedicated, regular flying.

EASA regulations and national specifics

At the European level, EASA regulates the certification of seaplanes like any other aircraft, with specific annexes for floats and amphibious configurations. In practice, the rules that weigh most heavily on day-to-day use remain national and local: each country sets its own rules for water landings, its approved bodies of water, and notification procedures with aviation authorities and sometimes maritime or environmental ones.

Good to know — In France, landing on water outside an approved body of water generally requires prior authorisation from the local prefecture or municipality, which varies depending on the department and the environmental sensitivity of the site (Natura 2000 zones, nature reserves). Always check the list of approved bodies of water before planning a purchase tied to a specific base.

Before buying an aircraft already based on water in another European country, it's essential to check the conditions for continued operation in the destination country — some imported historic seaplanes may require a revalidated airworthiness certificate or compliance work under the applicable EASA framework. The seaplane qualification (SES)

Flying a seaplane requires a specific class rating, commonly known as SES (Single-Engine Seaplane) for single-engine aircraft. This rating is added to a pilot licence already held (PPL, LAPL or equivalent) and is obtained through dedicated training, generally lasting a few days, at one of the few seaplane training centres available in Europe.

  • The training covers the specifics of water landings and take-offs, handling wind and chop, and emergency procedures specific to the aquatic environment.
  • The limited number of approved training centres in Europe can mean travel and a non-trivial training budget, to be planned for in addition to the aircraft's purchase budget.
  • The rating must be kept current through minimum recent experience, as with any class rating — a point to check before bringing a seaplane back into service after a long period on the ground.

For those just starting out, our guide on the cost of a private pilot licence gives an idea of the initial training path, before considering the seaplane rating as an additional step.

Points to watch when buying used

Buying a used seaplane calls for greater vigilance than buying a conventional land aircraft, because the structure is exposed to a more corrosive and mechanically demanding environment:

Point to checkWhy it's critical
Corrosion of the structure and floatsWater (especially salt water) significantly accelerates corrosion; a borescope inspection of hidden areas is recommended
Float compartment sealingUndetected leaks add weight to the aircraft and can compromise its buoyancy
History of water landings and hours on waterIntensive use on salt water wears float attachments and the powerplant faster than land use
Compliance of the float/airframe installationThe float kit must be approved for the specific aircraft type (STC or EASA equivalent)
Availability of specific partsSome float or hull parts come from North American manufacturers with longer import lead times

As with any used aircraft, a pre-purchase inspection by a mechanic or maintenance centre with real seaplane experience is strongly recommended — a general-aviation workshop with no float experience may miss wear signs specific to this type of aircraft.

Bases, water landings and operational constraints

Beyond the purchase itself, operating a seaplane depends heavily on proximity to an approved body of water and a home base (launching, storage, maintenance). Before buying, it's worth:

  • Identifying the approved bodies of water closest to your home or intended base.
  • Checking seasonal conditions (water level, winter freezing in some regions) that may limit flying to part of the year.
  • Planning for storage (pontoon, launching trailer, land hangar for aircraft on removable floats).

Purchase and running budget

Prices for a used seaplane vary widely depending on age, powerplant and type (removable floats vs dedicated hull) — much like the gaps already seen in the used helicopter or microlight markets, the day-to-day running budget needs just as much attention as the initial purchase price. The extra maintenance cost tied to the aquatic environment (corrosion treatment, float overhauls, specific parts) generally represents a notable additional line item compared with an equivalent land aircraft, and should be quantified with a professional before any buying decision.

Tip — Before committing, contact a local seaplane club or training centre: they generally know the regional used market well and can point you toward well-maintained aircraft with a reliable maintenance history.
