Buying a used aircraft in the United Kingdom: the post-Brexit market

Market, prices, registration with the CAA, VAT and the process of importing from the European Union: what has changed since Brexit for a buyer of a used light aircraft.

The British used aircraft market

The United Kingdom has one of the most active light aviation fleets in Europe, supported by a dense network of private airfields and flying clubs affiliated with federations such as the Light Aircraft Association (LAA) or the Royal Aero Club. The used aircraft market there is historically rich in classic single-engine touring aircraft (Cessna 172, Piper PA-28) as well as in amateur-built aircraft, a category that is particularly developed on this side of the Channel under the supervision of the LAA.

Since Brexit, the United Kingdom no longer belongs to the European EASA system for airworthiness regulation: the competent authority is now exclusively the UK Civil Aviation Authority (CAA), with its own regulatory framework (UK Part-M, UK Part-21). This does not make a purchase from the EU impossible, but it adds an extra administrative step that needs to be anticipated before committing.

Average prices by aircraft type

The price ranges observed in the United Kingdom remain broadly comparable to those of the rest of Western Europe for similar aircraft. As elsewhere, the condition of the airframe and the engine potential remaining before overhaul (TBO) weigh much more heavily on the price than the year of manufacture alone.

Tip — Always compare at equivalent engine potential (hours remaining before TBO): this is the factor that most influences the real price of an aircraft, far more than its year of manufacture.

Registration with the CAA

Any aircraft based and operated in the United Kingdom must be registered with the Civil Aviation Authority (CAA), under the G- prefix. For an aircraft already registered in the United Kingdom, a transfer between private individuals consists of updating the holder's details in the register. For an aircraft imported from abroad, prior deregistration in the register of origin is required, followed by the submission of a complete file to the CAA.

  • Valid certificate of airworthiness (or UK Part-21 equivalent) — to be checked before signing, as its expiry date determines the first flight under your name.
  • Bill of sale — dated, signed by both parties, stating the aircraft's serial number.
  • Third-party liability insurance certificate — required before first use under the new registration.
Good to know — Since Brexit, an EASA-certified aircraft is no longer automatically recognised by the CAA: mutual recognition agreements make the most common transfers easier, but the file still has to be assessed case by case. Generally allow 4 to 10 weeks depending on how complete the submitted file is.

VAT and taxation

A sale between private individuals in the United Kingdom is generally not subject to VAT (the standard rate of 20%). However, a sale carried out by a professional may be subject to it, and an aircraft imported from the European Union is now treated as a non-EU import: import VAT may apply, calculated on the customs value of the aircraft, except in specific circumstances that should be confirmed in advance.

SituationTax treatment
Sale between UK resident private individualsGenerally outside the scope of VAT
Sale by a professionalVAT potentially applicable (20%)
Import from the EU (post-Brexit)Import VAT, standard rate

Importing an aircraft from the European Union

Importing an aircraft from the EU into the United Kingdom now requires a customs declaration, even for a light aircraft. You need to plan for deregistration in the register of the country of origin, transport or a ferry flight to the United Kingdom, then submission of the import file to the CAA and the British customs authorities. Timeframes and required documents vary depending on the aircraft's initial status (EASA registration or not): getting support from an agent specialised in aviation imports remains advisable for a first file of this kind.

Point of caution — Always check the aircraft's customs status (whether VAT has already been paid in the EU or not) before signing: an aircraft whose tax situation is not clearly established can generate an unexpected import cost in the United Kingdom.

Pitfalls to avoid

The most common mistakes when buying in the United Kingdom echo those seen elsewhere in Europe, with an extra dimension specific to Brexit: underestimating the timeframes and administrative complexity of a cross-border transfer, failing to check the aircraft's customs status, or signing without having the exact condition of the optional equipment listed in the technical sheet confirmed in writing.
