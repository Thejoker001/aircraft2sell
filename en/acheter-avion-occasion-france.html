The French used aircraft market

France has one of the densest light aviation fleets in Europe, supported by a highly active network of flying clubs and more than 450 airfields spread across the country. The most commonly listed used aircraft remain the classics of touring aviation: the Robin DR400 and HR200 (historic French manufacturers), the Cessna 172, the Piper PA-28, alongside a plentiful supply of light sport/ULM aircraft, a category that has been particularly dynamic in France for several years.

Listings circulate through regional flying clubs, specialist marketplaces and word of mouth at airfields. As elsewhere in Europe, the asking price is a starting point for negotiation, particularly for an aircraft that has been on the market for several months or is being sold by a flying club looking to renew its fleet.

Average prices by aircraft type

Price ranges seen in France broadly align with the European average for comparable aircraft, with strong availability in the light sport/ULM and light single-engine segments sometimes pulling entry-level prices down thanks to the volume of supply.

Tip — The condition of the avionics and the engine history (hours remaining before TBO) often weigh more heavily on the price than a difference of a few years in the build date. Always compare at equivalent engine potential before relying on the year alone.

Registration with the DGAC

Any aircraft based and operated in France must be registered with the Direction générale de l'Aviation civile (DGAC), under the F- prefix. For a private purchase of an aircraft already registered in France, the transfer consists of a change of ownership in the French civil aircraft register. For an aircraft imported from another country, prior deregistration from the register of origin is required before any new French registration.

  • Valid certificate of airworthiness and CDN — check before signing, as the expiry date determines first use under your name.
  • Bill of sale — dated, signed by both parties, stating the aircraft's serial number.
  • Certificate of liability insurance — required before any use under the new registration.
Good to know — Common EASA rules simplify transfers between member states compared with an import from outside the European Union. Allow generally 2 to 8 weeks depending on how complete the file submitted to the DGAC is.

VAT and taxation

A private sale in France is generally not subject to French VAT. However, a sale made by a professional or a company may be subject to it, and an aircraft imported from a country outside the European Union remains subject to import VAT, calculated on its customs value. It is advisable to have your tax situation confirmed before purchasing, particularly for an aircraft that has already changed country of registration several times.

SituationTax treatment
Private sale between EU residentsGenerally outside the scope of VAT
Sale by a professionalVAT potentially applicable
Import from outside the EU (e.g. USA)Import VAT, standard national rate

Pre-purchase inspection and documents

Before signing, a pre-purchase inspection by a certified mechanic independent of the seller remains the best protection against unpleasant surprises. It covers the condition of the airframe, the consistency of the engine logbook, the maintenance history and the avionics' compliance with current European requirements (transponder, 8.33 kHz radio).

  • Up-to-date airframe, engine and propeller logbooks, with no chronological gaps.
  • Maintenance invoice history for the last three years.
  • No undeclared structural damage, verifiable via the maintenance records and a thorough visual inspection.
Point of caution — Never finalise a purchase without having seen the original logbooks and had their consistency checked by a professional: it is the best indicator of how well the aircraft has actually been maintained.

Pitfalls to avoid

The most common mistakes when buying in France echo those seen elsewhere in Europe: neglecting to verify deregistration from the previous register for an imported aircraft, underestimating the costs and timelines of the DGAC ownership transfer, or signing without having the exact condition of the optional equipment listed in the spec sheet confirmed in writing. An aircraft sold by a flying club deserves particular attention to its training usage history, which is often more intensive than private use.
