New vs used aircraft: the complete comparison before you buy

Price, depreciation, warranty, avionics and delivery times: everything you need to compare before choosing between a new aircraft and a used one.

The price gap between new and used

Budget remains the first criterion. A new light aircraft typically sells for between €250,000 and well over €700,000 depending on the engine manufacturer, equipment and certification, while a comparable used aircraft (same model, 10 to 20 years old, with an airframe and engine in good condition) is often available for 40 to 70% less. For a business jet, the gap is even larger in absolute terms, even though the percentage of depreciation follows a different curve (see below).

CriterionNewUsed
Purchase priceHigher, list price plus optionsGenerally 40-70% cheaper depending on age
NegotiationLimited room to negotiateOften more room, especially between private individuals
FinancingSometimes preferential rates via the manufacturerConventional loan or finance lease, market rates
Total entry costPrice + options + entry into servicePrice + possible refurbishment/avionics upgrade

Depreciation: who loses the most value

A new aircraft loses most of its relative value in its first few years of use, like most durable capital goods. A used aircraft already 10-15 years old has a much flatter depreciation curve: its residual value tracks mainly the condition of the airframe and engine, the remaining potential before overhaul (TBO), and the model's reputation on the market — not its calendar age alone.

This is a common argument in favour of buying used for leisure flying or a first acquisition: the financial risk on resale is generally more limited than with a brand-new aircraft, provided you buy a sought-after, well-maintained model.

Manufacturer warranty and support

A new aircraft comes with a manufacturer warranty (airframe, engine, avionics) and direct technical support — a real advantage for a first-time owner still learning the ropes of aircraft maintenance. A used aircraft, depending on its age, may still be under partial warranty (recently overhauled engine, modern avionics) or entirely out of warranty — in which case the maintenance budget needs a wider safety margin and a rigorous check of the maintenance history.

Always check — On a used aircraft, ask for the complete flight logbook, the maintenance history (Part-M/Part-ML), and the number of hours remaining before the next engine overhaul (TBO). These factors weigh more heavily on the price than the year of manufacture alone.

Avionics and onboard equipment

A new aircraft leaves the factory with up-to-date digital avionics (glass cockpit displays, certified GPS, integrated autopilot), often a decisive point for comfort and safety in flight. On the used market, avionics vary enormously from one aircraft to another: some older models have been retrofitted with modern displays (a real plus at resale), while others retain their original analogue instrumentation.

  • Check whether the avionics have been upgraded and when — a recent retrofit can justify a higher price.
  • A GPS/transponder that doesn't meet the latest regulatory requirements (e.g. ADS-B) can mean an upgrade cost you need to plan for.
  • Ask for the manuals and certificates for every piece of equipment added after the aircraft left the factory.

Delivery times and availability

Buying new often means a production lead time of several months, sometimes more than a year depending on the manufacturer and current demand — a factor not to overlook if you have an operational need coming up soon. A used aircraft, by contrast, is available immediately: once the pre-purchase inspection is done and financing arranged, you can take delivery within a few weeks.

How to decide based on your use case

There's no universal answer: the choice depends on your profile and intended use.

  • First purchase, leisure use, tight budget: a well-inspected used aircraft generally offers the best cost-to-risk ratio.
  • Intensive professional use, need for warranty and immediate support availability: a new aircraft reduces the risk of unexpected downtime.
  • Planning to resell in the medium term: a sought-after used model with a strong resale reputation often depreciates less, in relative value, than a brand-new aircraft fresh off the production line.
