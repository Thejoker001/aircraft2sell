The Norwegian used aircraft market

Norway stands apart from most European markets in its geography: a very elongated territory from north to south, deep fjords, mountain ranges and a population spread along the coast. Light aviation holds a special place there, not only as a leisure activity but sometimes as a practical way of connecting areas poorly served by road in winter. This reality has shaped a specific aircraft fleet, with a notable proportion of floatplanes (sjøfly) operated on lakes and fjords, as well as ski-equipped aircraft for the cold season, alongside classic touring single-engines.

The club network is very active: flying clubs affiliated with the Norges Luftsportforbund (Norwegian Aero Club) play an important role in the life of regional airfields, in training and sometimes in the circulation of sale listings between members. The busiest fields for general aviation are around Oslo (notably Kjeller), as well as Sandefjord, Stavanger, Bergen and Trondheim, in addition to a network of small regional airfields. Norwegian buyers often widen their search to neighbouring Swedish and Danish listings, the three Scandinavian markets being well connected in terms of spare parts and approved maintenance workshops.

An important point to bear in mind throughout this guide: Norway is part of the European Economic Area (EEA) but is not a member of the European Union. This particularity has concrete consequences in customs and tax terms, detailed further below, that set buying in Norway apart from buying in an EU member state such as Sweden or Germany.

Average prices by aircraft type

Prices in Norway remain broadly comparable with those seen elsewhere in Northern Europe for an aircraft of equivalent condition, airframe hours and remaining engine potential. Aircraft fitted with floats or skis, more specific to the local market, can command a premium linked to the cost of this equipment and demand for this type of use.

Tip — Norwegian listings are generally quoted in Norwegian kroner (NOK). Always convert at the current exchange rate and request the engine and airframe history before comparing a listed price with listings from neighbouring countries.

Registration with the Luftfartstilsynet

Any aircraft based and operated in Norway must be registered with the Luftfartstilsynet (Norway's Civil Aviation Authority), under the LN- prefix. For an aircraft already registered in Norway, a private purchase results in an update of the holder of the certificate of registration with this authority. For an aircraft coming from abroad, prior deregistration from the register of origin is required before any new Norwegian registration.

  • Valid, up-to-date certificate of airworthiness, recognised by the Luftfartstilsynet.
  • Previous certificate of registration and proof of deregistration if the aircraft comes from another country.
  • Certificate of liability insurance covering operation in Norway.

VAT and taxation: a non-EU country

This is the point that most sets a purchase in Norway apart from a purchase in an EU member state. Norway belongs to the European Economic Area, which facilitates the free movement of many goods, but it remains outside the customs territory and the VAT territory of the European Union. In practical terms, a transaction between an EU country and Norway is, for tax and customs purposes, akin to an import or export operation rather than a simple intra-Community transfer as is the case between two EU countries.

In practice, bringing an aircraft into Norway can trigger formalities with Norwegian customs (Tolletaten) and the application of Norwegian VAT (merverdiavgift, MVA), whose standard rate is generally quoted at around 25% in Norway for goods in general — this general figure should always be confirmed for the specific case of an aircraft, as exemption or calculation rules can vary depending on the status of the seller and buyer, the use of the aircraft and its origin. Conversely, taking an aircraft out of Norway to the EU also involves export procedures. These rules evolve and depend on the precise circumstances of each transaction: it is essential to have the exact tax and customs treatment confirmed by the Luftfartstilsynet, Norwegian customs (Tolletaten) and the tax authority (Skatteetaten) before signing, rather than relying on unverified rates for this specific case.

Key takeaway — Budget the time and cost of customs clearance into your purchase timeline if the aircraft is changing country, and request a written quote from a professional before committing.

Pre-purchase inspection and documents

As with any used aircraft purchase, a pre-purchase inspection carried out by an approved workshop independent of the seller remains the best protection against unpleasant surprises. In Norway, particular attention should be paid to the condition of the airframe and systems in light of local climate conditions: freeze-thaw cycles, humidity, and above all exposure to seawater for float-equipped aircraft, which can accelerate corrosion if maintenance has not been rigorous.

Documents to gather and check systematically:

  • Complete aircraft logbook, with no gaps in the history.
  • Report of the last annual inspection (Part-M / Part-ML) and any major engine overhauls.
  • Valid certificate of airworthiness and history of airworthiness directives (ADs) applied.
  • No outstanding dispute or lien on the aircraft, to be checked with the Luftfartstilsynet.
  • For a float- or ski-equipped aircraft, the specific condition and maintenance history of this equipment.

Pitfalls to avoid

The cross-border dimension of a purchase in Norway (a non-EU country) is the main pitfall to anticipate: underestimating customs clearance timelines or costs can delay entry into service or eat into the planned budget. Take the time to have the applicable customs and tax treatment confirmed in writing before negotiating the final price. Also be wary of incomplete logbooks, particularly for aircraft that have circulated between several Nordic clubs or countries, and of prolonged outdoor storage without protection suited to the climate. For a float-equipped aircraft, poorly treated corrosion remains one of the most costly points of vigilance to overlook.

Key takeaway — A price noticeably below market should always be questioned: reduced engine potential, corrosion linked to marine exposure, upcoming airframe work or incomplete documentation are the most frequent causes.
