The used aerobatic aircraft market in Europe

Aerobatic flying attracts a passionate crowd of pilots, whether they fly for leisure, training or top-level competition. The European used market is particularly active in France, Germany, the Czech Republic and Poland, historic homes to several well-known aerobatic manufacturers and training schools.

This market stands clearly apart from the classic touring aircraft segment: these airframes are designed to withstand far higher structural loads, which directly shapes their design, maintenance needs and, above all, the points a buyer must watch closely in a used purchase. An aerobatic aircraft with an intensive competition history does not carry the same fatigue profile as one flown only occasionally for leisure.

The used supply spans a wide spectrum, from an accessible entry-level biplane to an internationally competitive aircraft optimised for the most demanding figures, with considerable differences in price and performance between these two extremes.

Composite monoplane or traditional biplane: which to choose

Two main families structure the used aerobatic market. On one side, modern composite monoplanes optimised for top-level competition, offering very sharp handling and strong roll rate and climb performance. On the other, traditional tube-and-fabric biplanes, lighter, usually cheaper to buy, and long favoured for aerobatic training or leisure flying.

Beyond these two historic families, more recent competition monoplanes push performance even further, purpose-built for the most demanding levels of international competition. The right choice depends mostly on the intended flying level and the budget available, both for purchase and for upkeep.

Reference brands and models

A handful of manufacturers have historically dominated the aerobatic market, with good parts availability and a network of specialised workshops across Europe.

  • Extra — a leading German manufacturer of composite monoplanes, notably the 300 and 330 series, widely flown in international competition.
  • Pitts — the traditional, widely produced Pitts Special biplane remains a historic reference for aerobatic training and leisure flying.
  • Zivko — maker of the Edge 540, a high-performance monoplane prominent in top-level competition and in certain air-racing disciplines.
  • CAP — a French line of aerobatic monoplanes, notably the CAP 232, valued for its flying behaviour and ruggedness.

Before targeting a specific model, clearly define your flying level and ambitions: a Pitts biplane for leisure beginners does not answer the same needs as an Extra or an Edge 540 built for international competition.

What budget should you plan for

The price of a used aerobatic aircraft depends heavily on its age, airframe and engine hours, its documented load-factor history in the G-logbook, and its equipment level. Entry-level traditional biplanes trade at an accessible price point, while recent, well-equipped competition monoplanes — especially those with a recognised competition record — can represent a considerably larger investment.

Beyond the purchase price, budget for the additional costs: a pre-purchase inspection by a mechanic specialised in the type, aerobatic-specific insurance (often pricier than a standard touring policy), and possibly an overhaul of the high-performance engine or a propeller upgrade.

The G-logbook: the single most important document

The G-logbook, which records the actual load factors the airframe has experienced across flights and aerobatic figures, is arguably the single most decisive document when buying a used aerobatic aircraft. It lets you estimate real structural fatigue far more accurately than flight hours alone ever could.

These aircraft are designed to withstand load factors typically around +/-10G depending on the model, sometimes more for top-level competition machines. The structure is reinforced accordingly, but every repeated loading close to structural limits adds to material fatigue, whether the airframe is metal, composite, or tube-and-fabric.

Good to know — A missing, incomplete or clearly outdated G-logbook should make any buyer cautious: it can hide intensive competition use whose structural impact is very difficult to assess without that traceability.

What to check before you buy

A pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic specialised in the type is essential before finalising any transaction. For an aerobatic aircraft, a few points deserve particular attention.

  • A complete G-logbook consistent with the aircraft's declared history, especially if it has a competition background.
  • Fuselage condition: look for cracks, delamination or past repairs on a composite structure, or corrosion and tears on a tube-and-fabric structure.
  • Complete engine history for the high-performance powerplant: hours since last overhaul, compliance with the manufacturer's maintenance programme.
  • Propeller condition and balance, often a constant-speed unit on recent competition models.
  • Flight controls and canopy operation, plus a fully traceable logbook with no gaps.
Good to know — Never rely solely on the seller's description of the aircraft's condition: a written independent inspection report, ideally from a workshop approved for the type, is your best protection against an unpleasant surprise after the purchase.

Qualification and regulation

Beyond the standard pilot licence, a specific aerobatic rating or training is generally required to fly this discipline, with experience minimums that vary by country and by the level of figures flown. National authorities (such as DGAC, or the relevant EASA-based rule depending on the country) set these requirements, which should be checked before any purchase, especially if you are new to the discipline.

On the registration side, the process follows the general rules applicable to any aircraft, with particular attention to the certificate of airworthiness and its ongoing validity, given the specific structural stresses this type of aircraft is designed for.

Maintenance, resale and the used market

Running an aerobatic aircraft adds, on top of the usual cost items (mandatory annual inspection, insurance, parking), close monitoring of the airframe and high-performance engine, plus specific insurance that is often pricier than a standard touring policy given the discipline's risk profile.

On the resale market, an aircraft with a complete G-logbook, documented maintenance and no apparent accident history generally resells more easily and on better terms than one with a murky past. Comparing several listings for similar models, factoring in competition record and load-factor history, remains the best way to judge a fair price, whether you're buying now or preparing a future resale.
