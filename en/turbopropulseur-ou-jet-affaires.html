Turboprop or business jet: how to choose

A comparison of costs, range, speed and use case to choose the aircraft suited to your operation.

The fundamental difference

A turboprop uses a gas turbine to drive a propeller, while a business jet produces its thrust directly through jet reaction. This technical difference drives almost every other selection criterion: cost, speed, range and operational flexibility.

Operating cost comparison

A turboprop generally burns 30 to 50% less fuel than a jet of comparable power, and its maintenance is often simpler and less expensive. At purchase, a recent used turboprop generally costs less than an equivalent light jet of the same age and flight hours.

Speed and range

The business jet has a clear edge over long distances: its higher cruise speed significantly cuts flight time beyond 800 to 1,000 km. For shorter trips, the flight-time gap between the two aircraft types often stays marginal once taxi and climb time are factored in.

Access to short runways

Thanks to their better short-field takeoff and landing performance, turboprops often reach smaller airfields that jets cannot serve. This is a decisive criterion for an operation that needs to reach remote areas or unpaved runways.

Tip — If your operation regularly involves reaching small airfields, check the minimum runway length of each aircraft you're considering before deciding.

Which aircraft for which use

A turboprop suits frequent regional trips, access to short or unprepared airfields, and a controlled operating budget well. A business jet is preferable for long distances, cabin comfort on multi-hour flights, and the image associated with this type of aircraft for intensive professional use.

Word of caution — The choice should never come down to purchase price alone: factor in the full annual operating cost (fuel, maintenance, hangar, insurance) over 5 to 10 years before comparing two aircraft.
