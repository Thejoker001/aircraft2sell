Why a first purchase is different

Buying your first aircraft is not simply a matter of the price shown in the listing. An experienced buyer already knows how to assess a logbook, estimate the real condition of an engine, or negotiate a price based on the local market. For a first purchase, every step requires more caution: fewer benchmarks to judge whether a listing is fair, fewer contacts in the industry to cross-check information, and a real risk of underestimating the ancillary costs (maintenance, insurance, parking) that come on top of the purchase price.

This guide covers the most frequent questions from first-time buyers who contact Aircraft2Sell: how much to plan for in total, what type of aircraft to start with, how to avoid costly mistakes, and how a transaction between private individuals actually works in Europe.

Setting a realistic budget

The purchase price is only the first line of the budget. For a used light single-engine aircraft in good condition, prices observed on the European market generally range from €30,000 for an older model with basic instruments, up to €150,000 and more for a newer or better-equipped aircraft (modern avionics, low remaining engine potential used).

A rule commonly used by experienced buyers: plan for at least 15 to 20% of the purchase price as a reserve for the first year, on top of the acquisition budget. This margin covers the unexpected (a small repair discovered after the inspection, an avionics update, a part that needs replacing earlier than planned).

Choosing the right type of aircraft to start with

For a first purchase, the choice of model matters at least as much as the budget. A widely produced aircraft (Cessna 172, Piper PA-28, Robin DR400) offers a real advantage for a beginner: spare parts are available, mechanics are trained at most airfields, resale liquidity is strong, and a well-documented price history makes negotiation easier.

  • Favour a widely produced model over a rare aircraft, even if the price looks attractive — rarity makes maintenance and resale harder.
  • Check the availability of parts and qualified mechanics near your home airfield before signing.
  • Compare several similar listings (same model, similar year) to gauge the asking price against the real market.

The essential checks

Before making any offer, four documents must be requested and examined: the aircraft's logbook, the engine logbook, the current Certificate of Airworthiness (CofA), and the complete maintenance history (workshop invoices, periodic inspections). The absence or inconsistency of any of these documents is a warning sign that should delay the decision, not rush it.

Good to know — A pre-purchase inspection carried out by a mechanic independent of the seller is the best protection against a bad surprise. Its cost (€300 to €1,000 depending on the aircraft) is negligible compared with the risk of a discovery after the purchase.

Common pitfalls for first-time buyers

The mistakes that cost a first-time buyer the most are rarely related to the negotiated price, but to what was not checked before signing.

  • Relying solely on the listing's photos without asking for the detailed maintenance history.
  • Skipping the pre-purchase inspection to "save time" or its cost, even though it protects a far larger investment.
  • Underestimating the remaining engine potential before the next overhaul (TBO), an item that can represent €15,000 to €40,000.
  • Overlooking the real annual budget (insurance, hangar, inspection, fuel) by focusing only on the purchase price.

The steps of the transaction

A transaction between private individuals in Europe generally follows the same pattern: first contact with the seller, questions about the history and a request for documents, a physical viewing of the aircraft, a pre-purchase inspection by an independent professional, final price negotiation based on the inspection's findings, then signing a sales contract and transferring ownership with the relevant civil aviation authority (registration in the new owner's name).

Payment usually takes place at signing, once the inspection has been validated. For a significant amount, a notarised deed or a detailed sales contract (precise identification of the aircraft, declared condition, any warranties) protects both parties.

After the purchase: the first months

Once you are the owner, a few steps still need to be completed quickly: updating the insurance in the new owner's name, checking that the registration transfer has indeed been recorded, and contacting a local maintenance workshop to schedule the next periodic inspection. This is also the time to gradually get familiar with the aircraft, ideally with a few supervised flying hours if the model is new to you.

Tip — Compare several similar listings on Aircraft2Sell before making an offer. The listed price is often a starting point for negotiation, not a fixed price.
