Renting or buying an aircraft: which to choose

Renting an aircraft by the hour or becoming an owner: a data-driven comparison based on your annual flight hours to make the right choice.

The first question to ask

Before comparing costs, only one question really matters: how many hours will you fly each year, and for what kind of mission? A pilot flying 20 hours a year for pleasure doesn't face the same calculation as one flying 150 hours a year for regular business travel. The profitability of ownership depends almost entirely on this number.

The advantages of renting

Renting an aircraft from a flying club or a school remains the most flexible solution for occasional use: no capital tied up, no fixed costs between flights, and maintenance is entirely handled by the club or operator.

  • No capital tied up — no down payment, no resale risk, no depreciation to absorb.
  • No fixed costs — you only pay for the hours actually flown, with hangar and hull insurance included in the hourly rate.
  • Fleet flexibility — the ability to fly different aircraft types depending on the mission's needs.
  • Zero maintenance hassle — the aircraft's availability and airworthiness are managed by the club.

The main drawback remains availability: a club aircraft may already be booked by another pilot right when you need it, and the hourly rental rate remains higher than the direct operating cost of an owned aircraft.

The advantages of ownership

Becoming an owner makes sense once your flight hours increase, or when immediate aircraft availability becomes an important criterion (business travel, trips planned well in advance).

  • Declining hourly cost — beyond a certain number of hours, the direct operating cost drops below the rental rate.
  • Total availability — the aircraft is always accessible, with no booking or competition from other pilots.
  • Customization — choice of avionics, layout, an aircraft perfectly suited to your use.
  • Asset value — a well-maintained aircraft retains resale value, unlike rental hours, which are pure expense.

The break-even point in flight hours

The rule of thumb generally observed in the industry places the break-even point for ownership around 50 to 80 flight hours per year for a typical light single-engine aircraft: below that, renting remains almost always more economical once all the fixed costs of ownership (hangar, insurance, annual maintenance) are factored in; above it, the hourly cost of ownership generally becomes lower.

Annual flight hoursGenerally most economical option
Under 30 hoursRenting
30 to 60 hoursGrey area — depends heavily on the aircraft type
60 to 100 hoursIndividual ownership or co-ownership
Over 100 hoursIndividual ownership
Tip — This threshold varies significantly by aircraft type: the more capable the aircraft (and therefore more expensive to buy and maintain), the more annual hours are needed to make ownership pay off compared to renting an equivalent aircraft.

Intermediate solutions

Between pure renting and individual ownership, several formulas let you enjoy the benefits of ownership without bearing the full cost alone.

  • Co-ownership (partnership) — two to four pilots share the purchase, fixed costs and a booking schedule. The most common formula for accessing a more capable aircraft on a controlled budget.
  • Flying group — an associative structure similar to co-ownership, often with more members and an aircraft managed collectively.
  • Rent-to-own — rarer in light aviation than in the automotive world, but sometimes offered by professional sellers to let you test an aircraft before a final purchase.
Word of caution — Any co-ownership arrangement must be formalized with a written contract specifying cost sharing, the terms for a co-owner to exit, and booking rules, to avoid conflicts once the aircraft is acquired.
