Used experimental / kit-built aircraft: a buying guide

What is an experimental or kit-built aircraft

An experimental, or "kit-built," aircraft is one assembled from a kit sold by a manufacturer (Van's Aircraft, Rutan designs, Alpi Aviation, Zenair and many others) by a private individual or a small workshop, then certified under a regime distinct from the standard type certification issued for series-produced aircraft. This particular status gives the amateur builder greater design and equipment freedom, in exchange for a different regulatory framework managed at national level by each civil aviation authority, in coordination with European rules.

The used market for these aircraft has grown noticeably in Europe over the past decade, driven by the rising popularity of well-established kits such as the Van's RV series, by the often very favorable performance-to-price ratio of these aircraft, and by a particularly active community of builders and owners that makes it easy to pass on technical knowledge from one generation of owners to the next.

What budget to plan for by kit type

The price of a used experimental aircraft varies considerably depending on the kit model, build quality, engine installed and avionics. A simple two-seat kit, carefully built but modestly equipped, remains noticeably more affordable than a high-performance four-seat model fitted with recent integrated avionics and a top-of-the-line certified engine.

On top of the purchase price, budget for the usual costs of a used aircraft: a pre-purchase inspection, registration transfer fees, insurance, and possibly an avionics or finish upgrade if the aircraft needs one. You should also allow time and budget to obtain the flight authorization from the competent authority if the aircraft changes national register as part of the transaction.

Legal status in Europe: restricted airworthiness and permit to fly

In Europe, amateur-built aircraft fall outside the standard EASA type-certification regime and are, in most countries, governed by a specific national status (often referred to by terms similar to "restricted certificate of airworthiness for amateur-built aircraft," with varying names and acronyms by country). This status implies a continued-airworthiness regime distinct from certified aircraft, including a conformity statement issued at the end of construction, a supervised flight-test program, and maintenance obligations adapted to amateur construction.

Each member state applies its own rules for recognizing and transferring this status, which can complicate buying an aircraft registered in another European country or imported from a third country (notably the United States, where the "Experimental Amateur-Built" category is particularly common). Before any cross-border purchase, it is essential to check with the destination country's civil aviation authority on the precise conditions for registration transfer and maintaining experimental status, as these procedures can take several months depending on the country.

Good to know — Experimental status generally restricts the aircraft to private, non-commercial use, and may include restrictions on overflying densely populated areas. Always check these limitations with the competent authority before buying.

Inspection checkpoints for a used kit aircraft

Inspecting a used experimental aircraft differs noticeably from inspecting a series-produced certified aircraft, since the aircraft's quality depends directly on the original amateur builder's skill. A few points deserve particular attention.

  • The complete builder log: assembly photos, parts invoices, and records of inspections carried out during construction. Its absence or incompleteness should raise a red flag.
  • The visible quality of structural assembly work (riveting, composite bonding, welding depending on kit type) and its consistency with the kit manufacturer's plans and instructions.
  • The history and traceability of the installed engine, whether aviation-certified or an adapted automotive unit, as well as its hours and maintenance program.
  • Consistency between the equipment advertised and the restricted airworthiness certificate or conformity statement issued by the competent authority.
  • The existence and validity of an initial flight-test report documenting the aircraft's measured performance after construction.
Good to know — Using an inspector or mechanic familiar with the specific kit model (often recommended by amateur-builder associations) is particularly valuable for an experimental aircraft, whose build quality can vary from one example to another far more than on a series-produced aircraft.

Maintaining an amateur-built aircraft

Maintenance of an experimental aircraft generally follows a program defined by the owner-builder, based on the recommendations of the kit manufacturer and engine maker, under the supervision of the competent authority according to the terms of the relevant country's experimental status. This flexibility is an advantage for a technically competent owner, but represents a risk for a buyer who cannot verify how rigorously that maintenance was actually carried out.

A detailed maintenance logbook, kept diligently since construction, remains the best indicator of how well an aircraft has been cared for. Conversely, a patchy maintenance history or frequent changes of ownership without complete transfer of documentation should prompt caution, or even a thorough technical inspection before any financial commitment.

Insurance and pilot training

Insurance for an experimental aircraft takes into account both the aircraft's value and the pilot's experience on that specific type (often new to a first-time owner), and in some cases the documented quality of the build. A transition training course with an instructor experienced on the type, or failing that on a model with similar flying characteristics, is strongly recommended before any solo flight, especially for kits known to be more demanding on takeoff and landing.

On the regulatory side, pilots should check that their license and ratings genuinely cover the aircraft type in question, as rules can vary from country to country for experimental-status aircraft.

Choosing your kit: the reference brands

Several kit families dominate the European used market. The Van's RV series (RV-6, RV-7, RV-9, RV-10...) remains the most widespread reference, prized for its flying qualities, parts availability, and a very active owner community that greatly eases technical support. Burt Rutan's designs (Long-EZ, VariEze and derivatives) appeal through their aerodynamic originality and cruise performance, but require more extensive technical familiarization. Alpi Aviation, notably with the Pioneer 300, offers more recent kits geared toward travel and comfort, while Zenair (CH series) tends to target a more versatile and economical use case.

Beyond the brand, the single most decisive factor remains the build quality of the specific example in question: two aircraft built from the same kit model can show considerable quality differences depending on the care taken by the original amateur builder.

Resale and the second-hand market

The resale market for experimental aircraft depends heavily on how well-known the kit model is: the most widespread series, such as the Van's RV, enjoy a liquid market with a broad pool of potential buyers, while rarer or older kits can take longer to find a buyer. In every case, a complete builder log, documented maintenance and full transparency about the aircraft's history remain the best assets for a quick sale at a fair price.

Comparing several listings of the same kit model, while accounting for build quality and equipment, allows you to assess a price consistent with the real market rather than relying on a single isolated reference.
