7 pitfalls to avoid when buying a used aircraft

The most common mistakes when buying a used aircraft in Europe, and how to avoid them.

1. Relying solely on the listed price

A price 20 to 30% below market is almost never a good deal: it's the most common sign of a hidden problem (engine nearing its limit, corrosion, incomplete file). Always compare the listing against several comparable aircraft (same type, year, total hours) before getting excited about a low price.

2. Not cross-checking the logbooks

The airframe logbook, engine logbook and propeller logbook must tell the same story. A number of hours that doesn't match between logbooks, or a gap of several months without explanation, should always be questioned before going any further.

3. Skipping the independent inspection

Many buyers in a hurry settle for a report provided by the seller or a quick walk-around. This is the costliest mistake: only a pre-purchase inspection carried out by your own licensed mechanic, independent from the seller, truly protects your investment.

Word of caution — The cost of an inspection (€500 to €2,000 depending on the aircraft) is negligible compared with the risk of discovering a major defect after the sale.

4. Ignoring the incident history

An aircraft that has been through an accident or a major incident can be perfectly repaired and safe, but this must be documented and factored into the price. Always ask whether the aircraft has been the subject of an insurance claim for structural damage.

5. Underestimating the refurbishment budget

Between an upcoming engine overhaul, paint, avionics upgrades or tires to replace, the real budget often exceeds the advertised purchase price. Draw up a costed list of everything that will need to be done within 12 months before committing.

6. Not vetting the seller

A private seller in a rush to sell, a professional with no verifiable business address, or an intermediary who refuses any direct contact with the actual owner are all signals to take seriously. Favor platforms that verify sellers' identity.

7. Signing without a technical contingency clause

Never pay a non-refundable deposit before receiving the inspection results. A serious sale agreement should always include a "subject to satisfactory inspection" clause, with a full refund in case of a documented major defect.

Tip — If in doubt about any technical or legal point, get support from a professional before signing anything.
