Why the Skywagon remains a utility benchmark

The Cessna 180, followed by the 185 Skywagon, was designed as a rugged aircraft capable of operating from rough strips, with generous payload for its class. Built over several decades, it earned a strong reputation among mountain pilots, bush operators and fans of grass or unprepared runways. The conventional tailwheel gear and ample power make it known for short takeoff and landing (STOL) performance, which explains its lasting popularity on the used market, including in Europe where unpaved airstrips remain common.

While the 180 and 185 share a common lineage, they aren't strictly identical: engine power, maximum authorised weight and certain equipment differ between the series and production years. Correctly identifying the version and its history is therefore an essential step before committing to a listing.

180 or 185: what are the real differences

The Cessna 180 came first, fitted with a Continental engine of more modest power than the one later installed on the 185, which arrived with more generous power and a higher maximum take-off weight. Depending on the year, the 185 also received fuel capacity upgrades and reinforced gear options for float operations. In practice, the 185 is often seen as the more versatile version for heavy-duty use, while a well-prepared 180 remains highly sought after for its relative lightness and flying qualities.

Before buying, always ask for the current weight-and-balance sheet and the applicable type certificate for the specific airframe: the supplemental type certificates (STCs) installed over the decades (propeller, more powerful engine, STOL kit, reinforced gear) can significantly change performance and value compared with another aircraft of the same nominal model.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used 180/185 depends heavily on the condition of the airframe, engine hours and remaining potential before major overhaul, whether a STOL kit is fitted, the type of gear (standard wheels, tundra tires or floats) and the quality of the installed avionics. An aircraft with a sound airframe but dated avionics usually trades for less than one fitted with a recent digital cockpit, but can be an excellent starting point for a gradual upgrade project.

Beyond the purchase price, your budget should include an independent mechanic's pre-purchase inspection, registration and transfer fees, insurance (often higher for bush or float operations), and possibly an avionics or gear upgrade depending on your intended use.

What to check before buying

As with any used aircraft, a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic qualified on the type is essential. For a 180/185, several points deserve particular attention given the often intensive and sometimes rough use these aircraft see.

  • Corrosion: carefully inspect the belly, longerons and gear attachment points, especially on aircraft that have operated on floats or near coastal areas.
  • Complete Continental engine history: hours elapsed since the last overhaul, compliance with the maintenance program, traceability of replaced parts.
  • Condition of the conventional gear: tailwheel, shock struts, and gear reinforcements if the aircraft has been used for heavy loads or on rough strips.
  • Compliance of installed STC modifications (STOL kit, propeller, engine) with the technical documentation and aircraft file.
  • Complete logbook and technical file, with no grey areas in the usage history (bush flying, towing, floats).
Good to know — A 180/185 with heavy utility use isn't necessarily a bad buy: rigorous, documented maintenance often matters more than the raw hours displayed.

Conventional gear, tundra tires and floats

The gear fitted to the aircraft has a direct impact on its use case and value. Oversized tundra tires allow operation from very rough or sandy strips, at the cost of a performance penalty and specific tire maintenance. Float-equipped versions, rarer in Europe but present on certain lakes, require additional pilot skill and dedicated maintenance (cables, shock struts, sealing).

If you plan to change configuration after purchase (for example switching from standard wheels to tundra tires), check in advance the availability of kits, installation cost and compatibility with the registration and limitations of the specific aircraft.

Maintenance and operating costs

The operating cost of a 180/185 remains reasonable overall for a single-engine aircraft of this payload class, but plan for specific items: the mandatory annual inspection, insurance (often higher for bush, towing or float use), tires and brakes stressed by rough strips, and in the longer term an engine overhaul once the hourly potential is reached.

Having the aircraft maintained at an approved shop familiar with the type, and ideally with its specific use (bush, floats), helps limit unpleasant surprises and preserve resale value. Rigorous, well-documented maintenance records remain a strong argument at a future sale.

Tip — Always ask for the last three maintenance invoices before making an offer: they give an accurate picture of how seriously the aircraft has been maintained, especially around corrosion and the gear.

Operating and registering one in Europe

The Cessna 180/185 flies in Europe under various registrations depending on the aircraft's origin and import history. When buying an example imported from the United States or Canada, budget time and money for the import process and for obtaining a Certificate of Airworthiness (CofA) from the relevant authority, plus a possible avionics upgrade to meet local requirements.

Also check the operating possibilities under your license (aeroplane, seaplane if float-equipped) and any local restrictions on operating from unprepared strips, which vary across European countries.

Resale and the used market

The European Skywagon market remains smaller than in North America, making it a rarer find locally but also one sought after by a loyal base of bush and mountain flying enthusiasts. A well-maintained aircraft, with a sound airframe and a transparent technical file, generally resells without difficulty and on good terms.

Comparing several listings of similar aircraft, taking into account remaining engine potential, installed STC modifications and gear type, remains the best way to assess a fair price, both for buying and for preparing a future resale.
