Why the ICP Savannah is a reference among STOL microlights

Designed by the Italian manufacturer ICP (Italian Construction Partnership), the Savannah has established itself over more than two decades as one of the most popular microlights in the STOL (short take-off and landing) category. Its high wing, reinforced conventional landing gear and rugged airframe make it a favourite with pilots who fly from rough strips, in mountain terrain or in the countryside, far from paved runways.

On the used market, the Savannah stands out thanks to a very large installed fleet across Europe, which makes it easier to find spare parts, technical documentation and mechanics experienced with the type. Its reputation for ruggedness and versatility - leisure flying, training, exploring remote strips - explains a steady demand among buyers, from first-time owners to experienced pilots looking for a second aircraft.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used Savannah varies greatly depending on the age of the airframe, total flight hours, the exact model (S, XL, Cub or VG XL depending on the manufacturer's evolutions), the condition of the engine and the level of avionics fitted. An aircraft built many years ago with basic avionics trades at a very accessible entry level, while a recent, well-equipped example with low hours represents a significantly larger investment.

Beyond the purchase price, budget for an independent pre-purchase inspection by a mechanic experienced with this type of kit, registration fees, microlight insurance and, if needed, a refresh of the fabric covering, paint or avionics.

Versions and engine options to know

The Savannah has evolved over several generations, with different fuselage and wing variants aimed at different priorities: more payload, more range or more versatility on rough terrain. Before buying, it's essential to identify exactly which version you're looking at, since this affects parts availability, weight and balance, and the performance figures quoted by the manufacturer.

On the engine side, the vast majority of aircraft in service are fitted with Rotax 900-series engines, valued for their reliability and the availability of a strong maintenance network across Europe. Some older aircraft may be fitted with a different engine, in which case it's worth checking parts availability and the feedback from workshops on that specific engine before committing.

Because the Savannah is typically supplied as a kit and assembled by an amateur builder or an approved workshop, two aircraft of the same version can differ noticeably in finish, equipment and build quality. This is a central point to keep in mind throughout your search.

What to check before buying

As with any kit-built microlight, an independent pre-purchase inspection by a mechanic familiar with the type is strongly recommended before finalising a deal. For a Savannah, a few points deserve particular attention.

  • Quality of the structural build: check the riveting, wing and landing gear alignment, and look for corrosion or poorly documented previous repairs.
  • Complete engine history: engine hours flown, SMOH since the last major overhaul, and traceability of replaced parts.
  • Condition of the reinforced landing gear, which takes heavy loads from rough-field flying: shock absorbers, wheels and tyres suited to this use.
  • Compliance of the amateur-build file and the microlight identification record, with no grey areas about the aircraft's origin or modifications.
  • Consistent logbook and flight log, with regular, documented maintenance since the aircraft entered service.
Good to know — On an amateur-built aircraft, the quality of the original build often matters more than the displayed age: an older Savannah built with care can be a far better buy than a more recent example assembled in a hurry.

Microlight status and formalities in Europe

The Savannah falls under microlight status in most European countries, with registration, licensing and inspection rules that vary from one country to another. Before buying, check with your national aviation federation or competent authority to understand the exact formalities that apply to this type of aircraft and to your intended use.

One point to anticipate systematically: the aircraft's administrative file (identification record, proof that the build complies with the approved kit, declaration of entry into service) must be consistent with the regulations of the country where you intend to register or operate the aircraft. If in doubt, have this file validated by a competent body or federation before signing, rather than relying solely on the seller's statements.

Maintenance and operating costs

The operating cost of a Savannah remains moderate compared to a certified light aircraft of the same category, which partly explains its popularity. The main expense items are the periodic inspection under the applicable maintenance programme, microlight insurance, hangar or airfield parking, fuel and, over the longer term, engine overhaul or replacement once its potential is reached.

Having the aircraft maintained by a mechanic or workshop experienced with amateur-built kits and the Savannah type helps limit unpleasant surprises and keep a clear history, which is valuable at a future resale. Always keep invoices and work reports in the logbook.

Tip — Ask the seller for the full technical file (logbook, invoices, build file) before the viewing: an incomplete or confusing file should raise a flag, even if the aircraft looks good visually.

Resale and the used market

The Savannah benefits from an active, liquid used market in Europe, supported by a large installed fleet and good spare parts availability. A well-maintained aircraft with a clear build file and a complete logbook generally resells faster and on better terms than one whose history has grey areas.

Comparing several listings of aircraft from a similar generation and with a similar engine remains the best way to assess a fair price, both for buying and for preparing a future resale. Price differences between two similar Savannahs are most often explained by build quality, remaining engine potential and the quality of the technical file provided.

Before deciding, take the time to meet the seller in person and, if possible, fly the aircraft in favourable weather conditions. A test flight lets you judge the aircraft's real handling, the quality of the controls and how well all onboard equipment works, beyond what the technical file alone can tell you. This step usefully complements the pre-purchase inspection and helps you make an informed decision before committing to a used Savannah.
