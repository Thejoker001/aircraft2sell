Why the Phenom 300 appeals to business jet buyers

Launched by Brazilian manufacturer Embraer in the late 2000s, the Phenom 300 quickly established itself as a reference in the light business jet segment, regularly claiming the title of best-selling model in its category. Its spacious cabin for a twin-engine jet of this size, its cruise performance and its ability to operate from short runways made it a preferred choice for both operators and owner-pilots.

On the used market, the Phenom 300 benefits from an already substantial installed fleet and a solid technical support network in Europe, which makes day-to-day operation easier compared with rarer or lower-volume jets.

The Phenom 300 generations

Since its launch, the Phenom 300 has gone through several successive updates, with improvements to avionics, cabin layout and performance. The most recent versions stand out with an even more integrated cockpit and a redesigned cabin, which creates notable value differences between the earliest examples of the line and the latest model years produced.

Identifying precisely the generation and equipment level of an aircraft offered for sale is therefore an essential step before any serious negotiation.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used Phenom 300 represents a substantial investment, considerably higher than that of light propeller aircraft or turboprops, in line with its positioning as a high-performance business jet. It depends above all on the age of the aircraft, airframe and engine hours, whether or not it is enrolled in an engine program, and the level of avionics and cabin equipment fitted.

Beyond the acquisition price, your financing plan should include substantial ancillary costs: a thorough pre-purchase inspection, registration and transfer fees, insurance suited to this type of aircraft, and possibly enrollment costs for an engine or avionics program if the aircraft is not already covered.

What to check before buying

Buying a business jet like the Phenom 300 requires a pre-purchase inspection carried out by specialists on the type, generally at a workshop approved by the manufacturer or familiar with this specific model. The aircraft's complexity makes this step even more decisive than for a conventional light aircraft.

  • Complete engine history: hours and cycles accumulated, status regarding any hourly maintenance program, traceability of major interventions.
  • Condition and updates of the integrated avionics, as well as compliance with the regulatory requirements applicable to this type of aircraft.
  • History of structural inspections and service bulletins applied by the manufacturer.
  • Overall condition of the cabin, interior and comfort equipment, the refurbishment of which can represent a significant budget item.
  • Complete logbook and technical file, with no grey areas in the aircraft's history or its previous owners.
Good to know — On a business jet, an independent inspection report from a specialist workshop remains the best protection before committing, given the amounts involved and the complexity of the onboard systems.

Engine and avionics programs

Many Phenom 300s in service are enrolled in engine maintenance programs billed by the flight hour, which smooth out maintenance costs and help secure the aircraft's resale value. An engine covered by such a program is a strong selling point at purchase, as it reduces the risk of an unpleasant financial surprise linked to a major overhaul.

Before buying, it is essential to check precisely the aircraft's status regarding these programs, the conditions for transferring them to the new owner, and any fees associated with that transfer, which can vary considerably from one contract to another.

Crew qualification and operation

Operating a Phenom 300 requires a specific type rating for the pilot(s), obtained through an approved training center. This point should be anticipated from the start of the buying project, whether the aircraft is intended for owner-pilot use or operation by a professional crew.

Many owners choose to entrust the operation of their aircraft to a specialized management company, which handles crewing, maintenance and regulatory compliance, greatly simplifying ownership of a business jet of this level of complexity.

Operating costs

The operating cost of a Phenom 300 remains considerably higher than that of a light aircraft or a turboprop, in line with its performance and equipment level. The main expense items are fuel, periodic maintenance inspections, insurance, hangar parking and, in the longer term, engine overhauls if the aircraft is not covered by an hourly program.

Having the aircraft maintained at a workshop approved by the manufacturer and rigorously following service bulletins helps preserve its resale value and avoid costly corrective work.

Tip — Always ask for a detailed hours-and-cycles statement, as well as the status of any ongoing programs, before making an offer on a used Phenom 300.

Resale and the used market

The Phenom 300 benefits from an active used market in Europe and elsewhere, supported by a large in-service fleet and strong demand for high-performance light jets. A well-maintained aircraft, enrolled in an engine program and with a transparent history, generally resells more easily and on better terms.

Comparing several listings of comparable generations remains the best way to assess a fair price, both for buying and for preparing a future resale. Price differences between two aircraft of the same model year are almost always explained by the status of engine programs and the quality of the avionics and cabin equipment.
