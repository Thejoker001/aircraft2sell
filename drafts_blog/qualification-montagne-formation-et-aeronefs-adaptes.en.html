Mountain Rating: Training and Suitable Aircraft

How to train for a mountain flying rating, on which airstrips, and which aircraft are genuinely suited to landing and taking off on mountain strips.

What a mountain rating actually is

A mountain rating (or mountain flying qualification) is additional training for light aircraft pilots who want to land and take off on mountain airstrips (altiports) or short, sloped strips located in mountainous terrain. It certifies mastery of specific techniques: sloped approaches, uphill landings, downhill take-offs, handling of mountain winds and terrain-induced turbulence, as well as knowledge of the particular constraints linked to the altitude of the airstrips concerned.

This rating is not a standalone licence but an endorsement added to the pilot's logbook, generally issued strip by strip or for a group of strips, depending on the regulation and the training organisation. It is particularly common in the Alps, the Pyrenees and in some mountain ranges of Central Europe where approved altiports still operate.

Prerequisites for training

Training organisations generally require a currently valid private pilot licence (PPL), a minimum number of flight hours (often in the range of a few dozen hours as pilot-in-command, the exact threshold varying by organisation and country), and a good command of basic visual flight procedures. Recent flying experience, with a certain number of hours flown in the months before the course, is also recommended by most mountain instructors.

  • Currently valid private pilot licence (PPL) or equivalent.
  • Recent experience as pilot-in-command, ideally on the type of aircraft used for the training.
  • Up-to-date medical fitness, suited to high-altitude flying if the target strips are elevated.
  • Comfort with visual flight procedures over terrain (navigation, visual estimation of slopes and distances).

How the training unfolds

Training generally takes place over several days with an instructor qualified and authorised on the strips concerned. It combines a theoretical briefing (mountain meteorology, reading terrain, aircraft performance at altitude) with hands-on progression strip by strip, starting with relatively easy altiports before moving on to more demanding ones.

The number of strips covered and the total hours involved vary considerably between organisations and depend on each pilot's pace of progress. Some organisations issue a rating per approved strip, others a broader rating covering a mountain range or a group of strips with similar characteristics.

Good to know — The requirements, validity period and exact scope of a mountain rating vary by country and training organisation. Always check the current requirements with the relevant authority and your chosen school before committing.

What practical training generally covers

  • Observing the strip and its surroundings before the first approach (reconnaissance overflight).
  • Sloped approach techniques and speed management on a short strip.
  • Downhill take-off and safety-margin management in case of an aborted take-off.
  • Reading local weather conditions: slope winds, rotors, terrain-induced turbulence.

Altiports and mountain airstrips

Altiports are basic strips, often grass or dirt, located at altitude and characterised by a pronounced slope, a short length and a demanding mountain environment (a confined approach, no escape route in case of a go-around). Their approval status and conditions of use (time slots, seasonal restrictions, prior authorisation) are governed by national and local regulation, which should always be checked before any flight.

Every strip has its own particularities (length, slope, orientation relative to prevailing winds, altitude) that require specific familiarisation, even for a pilot already mountain-rated on other strips. This is why progression is traditionally done strip by strip, under the supervision of a local instructor before any solo landing.

Aircraft suited to mountain flying

Not every light aircraft is suited to landing and taking off on an altiport. Aircraft sought out for this kind of flying generally combine a low stall speed, good short take-off and landing (STOL) capability, a rugged landing gear able to absorb an uneven surface, and enough power to compensate for the performance loss caused by altitude.

Types regularly used in the mountains include high-wing aircraft with effective flaps and reinforced gear, as well as some types specifically designed or modified for STOL performance. The exact choice depends on the target strips, the load carried and the habits of the local instructor or club: there is no single universally "best" model, but rather aircraft whose performance and ruggedness are better suited to mountain use than others.

  • Low stall speed and a genuine handling margin at low speed.
  • Short take-off and landing distances, with a real safety margin on the target strip.
  • Rugged landing gear, suited to unprepared surfaces.
  • Enough power and climb performance given the altitude and temperature of the target strips.

Before buying an aircraft intended for regular mountain use, a thorough pre-purchase inspection is essential, particularly of the landing gear and structure, which are subjected to greater stress on uneven terrain than on a hard-surfaced runway.

Budget and maintenance of a mountain aircraft

An aircraft used regularly in the mountains experiences greater mechanical stress than one used exclusively on hard-surfaced airfields: the landing gear, tyres and some structural components wear out faster. You should budget for slightly above-average maintenance costs for this kind of use, along with extra attention during periodic inspections on the components under the most strain.

The cost of mountain rating training itself varies depending on the number of strips targeted, the number of hours required and the organisation chosen. Ask for a detailed quote including the planned number of flights and the strips covered before committing.

Safety and risk factors

Mountain flying combines several specific risk factors: reduced performance margins due to altitude and temperature, sometimes unpredictable local weather (slope winds, rotors), confined terrain limiting go-around options, and frequent lack of rescue infrastructure close to the strip. Decision discipline — in particular the willingness to abort in case of doubt about conditions or one's own fitness on the day — is a central element taught during training.

Word of caution — Never fly to an unapproved mountain strip, or without the prior guidance of a local instructor qualified on that specific strip, even if you already hold a mountain rating earned on other sites.

Keeping up the rating and currency

A mountain rating requires regular upkeep of skills: a pilot who stops practising for a long period will lose their reference points faster than on a classic lowland flight. Many organisations recommend, or even require, a minimum number of landings per year and per strip to keep operating there autonomously, beyond any regulatory recurrency requirements.

Flying regularly with an instructor or other experienced mountain pilots, exchanging information about how strips evolve (surface condition, new obstacles, slope changes) and staying informed about local weather conditions remain the best ways to maintain a high level of safety over time.
