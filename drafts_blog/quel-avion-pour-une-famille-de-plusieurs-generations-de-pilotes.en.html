Which aircraft for a multi-generation flying family

Grandparents, parents and kids who all fly: how to choose an aircraft capable of serving training, travel and passing on the love of flying across generations.

A special need: flying together, at each generation's own pace

In some families, flying is handed down almost like a tradition: a grandparent learned on a club aircraft decades ago, the parents earned their own licence in turn, and the children or grandchildren are now taking their first lessons. Choosing an aircraft in this context is not simply about picking the fastest or cheapest model — it is about finding one aircraft that can answer very different needs depending on who is at the controls: forgiving handling for a student pilot, comfort and range for a long family trip, and proven reliability for an older pilot who flies less often than before.

This kind of purchase therefore means balancing criteria that don't always point the same way: an aircraft simple enough to stay accessible to a pilot still in training, yet capable enough to take the whole family on a trip; rugged enough for years of shared use, but not so demanding to maintain that it puts off one of the households sharing it.

The versatile single-engine: training and passing on the skill

For a family where several generations are, or will be, learning to fly, a simple fixed-gear single-engine aircraft is often the most sensible first common purchase. This kind of aircraft is widely available, well documented, backed by an easy-to-find network of qualified mechanics, and supplied by a healthy parts market. It is also the type most flight instructors are themselves trained on, which makes supervising a family member in training easier, whether they are sixteen or sixty.

The appeal of this choice for a multi-generation family is twofold: it lets the youngest members train on a simple, forgiving aircraft, while more experienced pilots in the family keep using it for local or short trips — so the aircraft never sits idle between one generation and the next.

The capable single-engine: travelling comfortably as a group

Once training is behind them, many families look for a second aircraft, or a single more capable one, to cover longer trips with several people on board. A retractable-gear single-engine aircraft with four or six seats and modern avionics covers distances the family's training aircraft never could, while remaining within reach of a private pilot holding the appropriate ratings.

This kind of aircraft often becomes the real link between generations: grandparents appreciate the cabin comfort and reliability, parents value the range for business trips or holidays, and the youngest pilots in the family see it as the goal to work towards once they have earned their licence on the family's training aircraft.

Seats and payload: why four seats is rarely enough

The number of usable seats often decides, on its own, whether an aircraft can really support extended family outings. Four seats are fine for a couple with one or two children, but quickly become limiting once several generations want to fly together, with luggage, on the same day. A six-seat aircraft, or a four-seater with strong payload capacity, opens up noticeably more possibilities, at the cost of a higher purchase and running budget.

  • Seats that are genuinely usable with full fuel and baggage on board, not just the certified seat count.
  • Access and comfort for older passengers or young children, often underestimated when choosing a model.
  • Baggage space for a weekend trip involving several households, not just a couple on a local flight.
  • Remaining weight-and-balance margin once the aircraft is loaded with several adults aboard.

Sole ownership or co-ownership: matching the structure to an extended family

When an aircraft is meant to serve several households within the same family, the question of ownership structure comes up quickly. Sole ownership by one family member, made available to the others, stays simple to manage but concentrates the financial and administrative burden on a single person. A formal co-ownership arrangement between several family members spreads the purchase and running costs, but needs clear rules covering booking priority, cost-sharing and liability in case of damage.

Good to know — Whichever structure you choose, it is worth putting the rules for use, cost-sharing and insurance in writing at the time of purchase, rather than relying solely on informal family arrangements.

This is the same thinking many unrelated co-ownership groups go through when considering shared aircraft ownership: the same governance principles apply, whether the partners are cousins, siblings, or several generations of the same family.

Budget and costs shared across generations

Splitting costs across generations changes the budget picture compared with an individual purchase. The purchase price can be pooled, opening the door to a more capable aircraft than any single household could have financed alone. In exchange, recurring costs — insurance, parking, the annual inspection, fuel, and eventually a major overhaul — need to be split according to clear rules, usually in proportion to the flight hours actually logged by each family member.

Cost itemMost common way to split it
Purchase price and acquisition costsFixed shares agreed in advance between co-owners
Insurance and parkingSplit equally or in proportion to each owner's share
Fuel and routine wearProportional to each pilot's flight hours
Major overhaul and heavy maintenanceSet aside collectively in advance, often through a shared reserve fund

Planning for these costs at the time of purchase, rather than discovering them later, avoids a lot of friction between the different branches of the family involved in the project.

Training and passing on the licence

A multi-generation family aircraft often ends up, in practice, as the main platform for training the family's younger members. It is therefore worth choosing an aircraft whose handling qualities remain suitable for a student pilot, even if the more experienced family pilots also use it for more demanding missions.

Conversely, for older pilots who fly less often than they used to, simple procedures and forgiving handling remain important criteria — sometimes more important than raw performance. An aircraft that is too demanding when things go wrong can end up sidelined by the oldest generations in the family, which defeats the original purpose of passing the skill on.

What to watch for when buying a used family aircraft

Whether it is a first club aircraft bought for training or a more capable aircraft bought for family trips, buying a used aircraft meant to be shared across generations calls for particular care over its history: engine and airframe hours, consistency of the logbook, the status of the certificate of airworthiness (CofA), and full traceability of previous owners and how they used the aircraft.

An aircraft used mainly for club training has a different wear profile from one used for personal travel. Having an independent pre-purchase inspection carried out and requesting every maintenance invoice remains the best way to make sure the aircraft you choose will genuinely be able to serve several generations of pilots in the same family, without unpleasant surprises down the line.
