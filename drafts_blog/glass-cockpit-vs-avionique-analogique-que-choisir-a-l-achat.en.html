Glass cockpit vs analog avionics: which to choose when buying

Integrated screens or round dials: how these two generations of avionics affect price, reliability, maintenance and resale on the used aircraft market.

Glass cockpit and analog avionics: what we mean

Analog avionics refers to traditional flight instruments: mechanical or electromechanical round dials (airspeed indicator, altimeter, attitude indicator, vertical speed indicator, heading indicator) paired with radios and a portable or panel-mounted GPS that work independently of one another. Each function has its own housing, its own power source, and often its own failure mode without affecting the others.

A glass cockpit replaces this set of instruments with one or more integrated digital displays (a PFD for flight parameters, an MFD for navigation and engine data), fed by digital sensors and often paired with an autopilot, a flight management system and terrain alerting. The most common suites on the used market are the Garmin G1000/G3000, the Avidyne Entegra, the Garmin G500/G600 as a retrofit, and more recently all-glass suites such as the Garmin G3X or Dynon for experimental aircraft.

Between these two extremes sits a very common middle ground: an aircraft with a basic mechanical panel fitted with one or two digital instruments (an IFR navigation GPS, an ADS-B transponder, a digital autopilot). This partial mix is frequent on the European used market.

What you'll find on the used market

Light aircraft built before the mid-2000s almost always left the factory with pure analog avionics. From the mid-2000s onward, manufacturers such as Cirrus, Diamond and Cessna progressively rolled out glass cockpits across their lineups, first as an option and then as standard equipment on most models.

The result on today's used market: the oldest aircraft remain mostly analog unless retrofitted later; intermediate-generation aircraft (late 2000s to early 2010s) show a highly variable mix depending on the options the first owner chose; more recent aircraft, and nearly all Cirrus SR20/SR22s built over the past fifteen years, come with a glass cockpit as standard.

The impact on purchase price

For comparable airframe and hours, an aircraft with a glass cockpit generally sells for more than an equivalent analog-only aircraft, for two reasons: the cost of building and installing the digital suite was baked into the original purchase price, and a full glass cockpit retrofit on an existing aircraft itself costs tens of thousands of euros, which creates a value floor for aircraft already fitted with one.

Conversely, an older aircraft that never received any digital avionics upgrade typically trades at the lower end of its segment, especially if it lacks the GPS navigation and ADS-B transponder equipment now expected in most European airspace.

Good to know — A partial avionics retrofit (adding a modern IFR GPS and an ADS-B transponder to an analog panel) generally costs far less than a full glass cockpit conversion, and is often enough to make an older aircraft fully usable in Europe.

Reliability and failure modes: two different logics

Analog avionics benefits from simplicity and natural redundancy: the failure of one instrument does not affect the others, mechanical parts are well understood by maintenance shops, and the most basic instruments (airspeed indicator, altimeter) are often independent of any electrical power. Its main drawback is the gradual wear of mechanical parts and gyros, which require periodic servicing and can develop drifts that are difficult to spot in flight.

A glass cockpit brings a different kind of redundancy: multiple digital sensors typically feed two or more screens, backed by dedicated standby batteries in case of a total electrical failure. On the other hand, a power or sensor failure can affect several functions at once, and the earliest suites (first-generation G1000, Avidyne Entegra) are starting to show their age, with certain screens or circuit boards and spare parts becoming harder to source.

  • Check the logbook for recorded avionics failures, including any screen or module replacements.
  • Ask for a full ground run of the digital suite: screens, sensors, redundancy, standby batteries.
  • On analog avionics, have the gyro instruments checked for wear and abnormal drift.

Maintenance, updates and obsolescence

Maintaining analog avionics stays fairly predictable and spread out over time: periodic overhauls of gyroscopic instruments, occasional replacement of a failed instrument, with no knock-on effect on the rest of the panel. Spare parts remain available for most common models, even older ones, through a network of specialist shops.

A glass cockpit requires a different kind of upkeep: software and navigation database updates, sensor or module replacement on failure, and above all vigilance around obsolescence. The very first generation of glass cockpits, now fifteen to twenty years old, is reaching a stage where certain components are becoming harder to source, which can mean longer downtime after a failure, or even the need to retrofit to a newer suite.

Watch out — Before buying an aircraft fitted with a first-generation glass cockpit suite, check how available spare parts and software support still are for that specific avionics model.

Pilot training and transition

Moving from analog avionics to a glass cockpit, or the other way around, represents a genuinely different cognitive workload that warrants transition training even for an experienced pilot. Reading a digital PFD, navigating MFD pages and operating a GPS-coupled autopilot all take real time to master, particularly in instrument flight conditions.

This human factor deserves as much weight in a purchase decision as the technical side: a buyer less comfortable with digital systems may prefer a simpler analog-equipped aircraft, with the option of a gradual retrofit later once they are fully at ease with the type.

Impact on resale

On today's market, an aircraft fitted with a recent, well-maintained glass cockpit generally sells faster and at a higher price than an equivalent analog-only aircraft, as buyer demand has shifted noticeably toward digital avionics over the past fifteen years. This trend is particularly strong in the travel and advanced-training segments, where glass cockpit safety features (terrain alerting, traffic display, instrument redundancy) have become a genuine purchase criterion.

Conversely, an aging first-generation glass cockpit can, somewhat paradoxically, hold back a resale if it raises concerns about significant maintenance or upgrade costs down the line, whereas simple, well-maintained analog avionics keeps a stable value in certain basic training or leisure aircraft segments.

How to choose based on your profile

For regular travel, IFR flying, or longer distances, a glass cockpit brings navigation comfort and added safety that generally justify its higher purchase price. For simple local VFR leisure flying, well-maintained analog avionics, possibly complemented by a modern GPS and ADS-B transponder, remains an economical and reliable option, without the added complexity of managing a full digital suite.

Either way, have the avionics inspected, regardless of generation, as part of the overall pre-purchase inspection: it is often as significant a hidden cost item as the airframe or engine.
