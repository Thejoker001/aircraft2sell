Buying a Used Aircraft in Germany or Italy: Which Country to Choose

Market, prices, taxation, registration and maintenance: a comparison between Germany and Italy to help you decide where to look for your used aircraft.

Two neighbouring markets, two different aviation cultures

Germany and Italy are both major light-aviation markets in Europe, but with fairly different profiles. Germany has the largest fleet of light aircraft and gliders on the continent, supported by a long-standing aviation tradition, a particularly dense network of airfields and clubs, and a historically strong industrial base in the sector. Italy, for its part, has a smaller overall fleet but a leisure-aviation scene that has grown in recent years, helped by a climate that allows flying year-round in many regions.

A buyer based in Germany can perfectly well find an interesting aircraft in Italy, and vice versa, provided they understand the administrative and practical specifics of each country before entering a cross-border transaction.

Size and dynamism of the used-aircraft market

Germany has the largest and most varied used-aircraft market in continental Europe, with an abundant supply of the most common piston single-engine types, but also of gliders and motor gliders, a discipline in which the country remains a world reference. This abundance makes it easy to compare several similar aircraft, though it can also mean stronger competition between buyers for the most sought-after models.

The Italian used-aircraft market is smaller in absolute volume but remains active, particularly in the north of the country (Lombardy, Veneto, Piedmont) where most Italian general aviation is concentrated. Supply there tends to lean a little more towards microlights and recently certified or amateur-built light aircraft, with a notable appetite for economical types to operate.

Comparing prices

Price levels between the two markets remain broadly comparable for the same model and the same maintenance condition, with observed differences owing more to local supply and demand at any given moment than to a structural difference between the two countries. A well-maintained aircraft with a complete logbook will trade at a similar level regardless of its country of origin.

In both cases, comparing several listings of similar models remains the best way to assess a fair price before committing, wherever the coveted aircraft happens to be.

Seasonality also plays a role: the Italian market often sees a peak in listings at the end of the flying season in regions where activity is concentrated in the warmer months, whereas the German market, being larger and spanning more varied regional climates, varies less sharply from month to month. Widening your search to both countries therefore often provides access to a more stable choice throughout the year.

Taxation and purchase-related costs

The taxation applicable to the purchase of a used aircraft depends above all on the buyer's status (private individual or professional), their country of tax residence and the country in which the aircraft will be registered after the transaction, more than on the single country where the aircraft is located at the time of purchase. In a cross-border transaction between Germany and Italy, it is worth checking precisely the possible intra-community VAT formalities applicable between professionals, as well as the ownership-transfer fees specific to each country, which can vary from one country to the other.

Using a professional familiar with cross-border transactions (broker, specialist lawyer) helps avoid procedural mistakes that can prove costly, particularly regarding VAT or customs documentation when the aircraft changes country of registration.

Registration and administrative procedures

In Germany, civil aircraft registration is handled by the Luftfahrt-Bundesamt (LBA), under the D- prefix. In Italy, it is the ENAC (Ente Nazionale per l'Aviazione Civile) that maintains the national register, under the I- prefix. An aircraft bought in one of the two countries can be registered in the other, depending on the buyer's choice: keeping the registration of the country of purchase is not mandatory. This change, however, involves specific procedures with the destination authority, including submitting the complete technical file, checking the validity of the certificate of airworthiness and, where applicable, a compliance inspection.

  • Check that the logbook, technical file and the aircraft's actual condition are consistent before any change of registration.
  • Plan ahead for administrative timelines, which can vary noticeably between the LBA and ENAC depending on their current workload.
  • Check for any specific onboard equipment requirements in the destination country.
  • Budget for ferry-flight costs if an inspection or compliance work is needed before the transfer.

Maintenance network and parts availability

Germany has one of the densest and most specialised networks of approved maintenance workshops in Europe, covering almost all common types, including gliders and motor gliders. Italy also has a network of competent workshops, concentrated mainly in the north, but which can be sparser in some central and southern regions for less common types.

For rarer models or specific engines, it is worth checking in advance the availability of parts and the proximity of a specialist workshop before finalising the purchase, regardless of the aircraft's country of origin. The distance between the two countries remains modest from southern Germany and northern Italy, which makes ferrying an aircraft for maintenance between the two countries relatively easy when needed.

The language barrier can also come into play: a German buyer not comfortable in Italian will often favour a German- or English-speaking workshop, and an Italian buyer will reason the same way in the other direction. In both cases, technical documentation in English is generally accepted by approved workshops in both countries.

Which country to favour depending on your profile

For a buyer who values a very wide choice and immediate proximity to their flying club or home airfield, searching primarily on the local market often remains the simplest solution, whether in Germany or in Italy. For a buyer willing to widen their search in order to find the best-maintained aircraft at the best price, considering both markets in parallel provides access to a wider offering and a better basis for comparing technical files.

Good to know — In all cases, a pre-purchase inspection by a qualified independent mechanic remains essential, whether the aircraft is located in Germany, Italy or elsewhere in Europe.

Climate can also steer the choice depending on intended use: a buyer mainly looking for a touring aircraft to fly year-round in mild conditions will often lean towards the Italian market, particularly in the south of the country, while a buyer who values proximity to a very wide choice of models, gliders and workshops will often stay loyal to the German market. The two approaches are not mutually exclusive: there is nothing stopping you from searching for an aircraft in Italy while planning to have it maintained in Germany, or vice versa, as long as ferrying distances remain reasonable.

Ultimately, the country of purchase matters less than the actual quality of the aircraft and how well it has been maintained. Comparing listings from both markets widens your options and gives you a better negotiating position, whether on price or on the terms of the transaction.
