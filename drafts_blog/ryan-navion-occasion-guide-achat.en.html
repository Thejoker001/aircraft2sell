Used Navion: buying guide

Budget to plan for, inspection points to watch, routine maintenance and resale outlook to help you buy a used Ryan Navion, a rugged American vintage aircraft with a well-earned reputation.

A vintage aircraft with a unique history

The Navion was originally designed by North American Aviation in the late 1940s, before production was taken over by Ryan Aeronautical, hence the "Ryan Navion" name commonly used for aircraft built under that banner. Conceived as a fast, solid touring aircraft, it inherits a rugged design inspired by the military standards of its era, with a hefty metal structure and a particularly resilient retractable landing gear.

On the used market today, the Navion appeals to owner-pilots looking for an aircraft with character, different from the composite single-engine aircraft of today. Its cabin, spacious for its time, good load-carrying capacity and reputation for toughness make it a popular choice among vintage aviation enthusiasts, provided buyers accept more demanding upkeep than a more recently designed aircraft.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used Navion varies widely depending on the overall condition of the airframe, the engine hours remaining before the next major overhaul, the quality of modifications carried out over the decades, and the level of interior and paint restoration. An entry-level example needing work trades at a modest purchase level, while a carefully restored, well-documented aircraft fitted with upgraded avionics represents a far larger investment.

Beyond the purchase price, a vintage aircraft like this brings costs that are often underestimated: a thorough pre-purchase inspection, a possible avionics or instrument upgrade, and a financial reserve for type-specific parts that aren't always as readily available as on a more common, modern production aircraft.

What to check before buying

An aircraft of this age calls for a particularly rigorous pre-purchase inspection, carried out by a qualified mechanic, ideally one familiar with the type. Several points deserve heightened attention.

  • Condition of the metal structure: look for any signs of corrosion, especially in hard-to-reach areas and spars, common on airframes several decades old.
  • Full operation of the retractable landing gear and its associated hydraulic or electrical systems, a known source of costly rework.
  • Complete engine history: engine hours since the last major overhaul (SMOH), traceability of parts and compliance with applicable service bulletins.
  • Validity of the certificate of airworthiness (CofA) and consistency between the logbook and the modifications physically found on the aircraft.
  • Condition of the wiring and avionics, often partly original on examples that haven't been modernised.
Good to know — On a vintage aircraft like the Navion, the quality and continuity of the logbook often matter as much as the aircraft's visible condition: an incomplete history should raise a flag, even if the airframe looks good.

Engine, propeller and type modifications

Many Navions have been modified over their careers, including re-engining with more powerful engines than those originally fitted. Properly documented and approved, these upgrades can improve performance and value, but they make it essential to carefully check the consistency between the technical documentation and the aircraft's actual configuration.

It's advisable to ask the seller for a full account of the modifications carried out since the aircraft was built, along with supporting paperwork, before committing to an offer.

Maintenance and parts availability

Maintaining a Navion requires a shop used to older aircraft and able to work around parts availability that can be more limited than for a model produced in much larger numbers. The network of type owners and clubs remains a valuable resource for finding parts, technical information and maintenance advice specific to this aircraft.

The main expense items remain the mandatory annual inspection, landing gear upkeep, corrosion monitoring and, eventually, an engine overhaul. Buyers should anticipate a maintenance budget somewhat higher than for an equivalent modern aircraft.

Tip — Joining a Navion owners' club or association before buying gives access to valuable first-hand feedback on the aircraft currently available on the market.

Checkout and training

The Navion flies differently from a modern fixed-gear single: its weight, inertia and the handling of the retractable gear require an adjustment, especially for a pilot coming from lighter, more modern aircraft. A proper checkout with an instructor experienced on this type is strongly recommended before any solo flight.

This handling specificity should also be discussed with your insurer, as some policies may require minimum training or experience on this type of aircraft.

Resale and the used market

The used Navion market remains a niche market, driven more by a community of enthusiasts than by a transaction volume comparable to modern production aircraft. A well-restored aircraft, with a transparent history and properly documented modifications, generally finds a buyer more easily than a neglected or poorly documented example.

Comparing available listings and talking to type clubs remains the best way to assess a fair price, both when buying and when preparing a future resale.
