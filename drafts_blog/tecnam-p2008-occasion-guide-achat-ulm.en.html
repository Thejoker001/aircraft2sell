Why the Tecnam P2008 appeals to microlight pilots

The Tecnam P2008 is one of the most widely flown three-axis microlights in Europe, built by the Italian manufacturer Tecnam and sold, depending on the market, as a microlight or in a certified version. Its airframe pairs a composite fuselage with an aluminium wing, a combination that gives it a reputation for toughness while keeping weight down. The cabin is roomier than most in its class, and the overall comfort makes it a common pick for flight schools as well as private owners who want a modern aircraft that is cheap to run and pleasant to fly day to day.

On the used market, the P2008 benefits from good spare parts availability and a network of mechanics familiar with the type, particularly in Europe where Tecnam has a strong commercial presence. That popularity translates into a comparatively stable resale value next to less common microlights, which many buyers also find reassuring.

The P2008 is also valued for its versatility: it suits both ab-initio training and light touring, with modest fuel burn and overall running costs that stay well controlled for the class.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used P2008 varies widely depending on the year of manufacture, airframe and engine hours, the avionics fit (classic analogue panel or a modern EFIS screen) and the overall quality of maintenance. The oldest examples, with reduced engine potential and basic avionics, trade at a far more accessible entry level than recent aircraft with a full digital cockpit and immaculate maintenance records.

On top of the purchase price, budget for the usual ancillary costs: a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic qualified on the type, microlight registration fees, insurance, and possibly an avionics or cabin interior refresh if the aircraft needs one.

Rotax engine and engine hours (SMOH)

The P2008 is overwhelmingly fitted with an engine from the Rotax 912 family, a powerplant widely used across light and microlight aviation and valued for its reliability and the good availability of parts in Europe. As with any engine of this type, the number of hours elapsed since the last major overhaul (SMOH) is one of the indicators buyers watch most closely: the more potential remaining before the next overhaul, the more value the aircraft retains.

It is essential to check that the maintenance intervals set by the engine manufacturer have been strictly observed, and that any components subject to a calendar limit — not just an hourly one — have been replaced on schedule, regardless of the actual flight hours flown.

An engine that has sat idle for long periods without proper preservation can show signs of internal corrosion even with low hours on the clock: hours alone are never enough to judge an engine's real condition.

Composite fuselage and aluminium wing: what to watch

The P2008's hybrid structure, combining a composite fuselage with a metal wing, calls for a careful inspection of both material families. On the composite side, look for impacts, gelcoat cracks or poorly executed repairs, particularly on the engine cowling and fairings, the areas most exposed to knocks during ground handling.

On the aluminium wing, check for corrosion, excessive play in the flight controls and impact marks around the spars, along with proper operation of the flaps and control surfaces through their full range of travel. An aircraft left outside for long periods without a hangar generally shows more wear on both material families than a hangared example.

What to check before buying

As with any used aircraft, a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic qualified on the type is essential before finalising a transaction. For a P2008, a few points deserve particular attention.

  • Complete logbook and technical file, with no grey areas in the aircraft's history.
  • Detailed engine history: hours since the last overhaul, compliance with the Rotax maintenance program, traceability of replaced parts.
  • Condition of the composite structure and the aluminium wing, checking for corrosion, cracks or previous repairs.
  • Operation of the onboard avionics and compliance of fitted equipment with current requirements.
  • A full test flight with a pilot qualified on the type, including a check of engine performance across different power settings.
Good to know — Never rely solely on the seller's statements about overall condition: an independent pre-purchase inspection by a mechanic qualified on the type remains the best protection before committing.

Maintenance and the logbook

The operating cost of the P2008 stays moderate for its class, helped by the relative simplicity of its Rotax engine and the good availability of parts. The main expense items are the periodic maintenance required by the manufacturer's program, insurance, parking and, further down the line, the engine overhaul once its hourly potential is reached.

A logbook kept rigorously, at a shop familiar with the type, is one of the strongest arguments for reassuring a future buyer. Asking for the most recent maintenance invoices before making an offer lets you check that the declared hours match the work actually carried out.

Tip — Always ask for the last three maintenance invoices before making an offer: they give an accurate picture of how seriously the aircraft has been maintained.

Registration and microlight regulations in Europe

Microlights fall, depending on the European country, under their own national rules, distinct from the certification that applies to conventional aircraft. Registration procedures, training requirements and the limits of the category vary from one country to another, which can affect the steps required for a cross-border purchase or a change of country of operation.

Before buying a P2008 in a country other than the one where you plan to fly it, it is advisable to check with the relevant national microlight authority or federation on the procedures for transferring registration and on whether the pilot's licence will be recognised.

Resale and the used market

The P2008 benefits from an active used market in Europe, supported by Tecnam's strong reputation and a large installed fleet, particularly among flight schools. A well-maintained aircraft, with a transparent history and up-to-date avionics, generally resells more easily and on better terms than one whose technical file has grey areas.

Comparing several listings of similar aircraft remains the best way to assess a fair price, both for buying and for preparing a future resale. Price differences between two aircraft of the same model year are most often explained by remaining engine potential and the level of avionics fitted.
