Ferry permit (special flight permit): when and how to get one

An aircraft that cannot fly under normal conditions sometimes needs to reach a workshop, a maintenance base, or a new owner. A special flight permit — commonly called a "ferry permit" — governs this exceptional flight. Here's what you need to know before requesting one.

What a special flight permit is

A special flight permit — commonly known by its English name "ferry permit" or, in some countries, "permit to fly" — authorizes an aircraft to carry out a specific flight even though it does not, or no longer, meets all the normal conditions of airworthiness to fly freely. It is a one-off, closely controlled exception, never a restoration of the aircraft's general airworthiness.

This document is issued by the civil aviation authority responsible for the aircraft in question (based on its registration), for one flight or a precisely defined series of flights, with a route, conditions and limitations set in advance.

Key point — The exact procedure, forms, timelines and fees differ depending on the competent authority (the DGAC in France, the CAA in the United Kingdom, the FAA in the United States, or any other national authority or EASA, depending on the case). This guide is deliberately kept general: always check the current procedure with the authority responsible for the aircraft's registration before starting the process.

Which situations call for one

A special flight permit typically applies when an aircraft needs to move while its normal airworthiness is not — or no longer — established.

  • Ferrying to a maintenance workshop — the aircraft has an identified fault that prevents it from flying normally, but that does not rule out a controlled flight to the repair location.
  • Expired or suspended certificate of airworthiness — the aircraft needs to reach an approved maintenance centre to renew its airworthiness, but cannot fly "normally" until that is done.
  • After returning to service following long-term storage — before every check is complete, a one-off flight to a specialist workshop may require this exception.
  • Ongoing modification or repair — the aircraft needs to be ferried to the site where the work will be carried out or completed.
  • Change of ownership — the aircraft needs to be delivered to its new owner or moved to a new home airfield, while its administrative or technical status is not yet fully settled.
  • Limited check or test flight — in some cases, a controlled check flight after major work can be covered by this type of permit rather than a full return to airworthiness.

The general principle behind the process

The logic is similar from one authority to another, even though the forms and timelines vary. The goal is always to demonstrate that, despite the lack of general airworthiness, the aircraft remains safe enough to carry out the specific flight requested, under controlled conditions.

  • Precise identification of the fault(s) preventing normal airworthiness, and their impact on the safety of the intended flight.
  • Description of the requested flight: route, departure and arrival airfields, acceptable weather conditions, required crew.
  • Inspection or statement from an approved mechanic confirming that the aircraft can safely carry out this specific flight, despite the identified fault(s).
  • Filing the request with the competent authority, generally with a processing time to anticipate — this timeline varies significantly depending on the authority and the urgency of the case, and should never be assumed without direct confirmation.
  • Issuance of a document specifying the conditions and limitations of the authorized flight, which must be carried on board for the entire flight concerned.
Word of caution — Never assume a special flight permit will be granted automatically or within a given timeframe. Each case is reviewed individually by the competent authority, which can refuse it, request additional inspections, or impose stricter conditions than expected.

What the authority typically reviews

Beyond the forms specific to each country, authorities generally look at the same core elements before issuing a special flight permit.

  • The exact nature of the airworthiness defect and how critical it is to flight safety.
  • The competence and approval of the mechanic or organization that inspected the aircraft before the flight.
  • The suitability of the proposed route (overflight of populated areas, diversion options in case of a problem).
  • The qualification of the crew planned for this particular flight.
  • Insurance specifically covering this exceptional flight — a standard policy may exclude a flight carried out under a special permit, a point that absolutely must be checked with the insurer before departure.

Limitations imposed during the flight

A special flight permit almost always comes with precise restrictions, which vary depending on the reason for the request and the authority's assessment.

  • A fixed route, with no deviation possible outside the alternate airfields explicitly authorized.
  • Weather restrictions, often stricter than for a normal flight (VMC conditions required, raised minimum ceilings and visibilities).
  • A ban on carrying passengers not essential to the mission, or sometimes a total ban on passengers depending on the reason for the permit.
  • Limited or prohibited overflight of populated areas, to reduce risk in the event of a failure.
  • A strictly bounded validity period for the permit, sometimes including a precise time window.

The special case of buying and selling

In the context of a transaction, the special flight permit comes up regularly when a buyer wants to ferry the aircraft to their home airfield or a workshop before completing all the airworthiness or registration steps in their own country. This is a point to anticipate right from the negotiation: who organizes the process, who bears the cost, and who assumes the risk if the permit is refused or delayed.

Address this question explicitly in the sales agreement if a ferry flight under this regime is being considered, with a clause spelling out each party's responsibilities in case of a delay or refusal from the authority.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Waiting until the last minute to start the process, without accounting for the processing times specific to the competent authority.
  • Not checking whether the insurance specifically covers a flight carried out under a special flight permit.
  • Underestimating the weather limitations imposed, which are often more restrictive than expected.
  • Forgetting to carry the original permit document and all the technical paperwork justifying its issuance on board.
  • Confusing the special flight permit with a full return to airworthiness: it only covers the specific flight for which it was issued, nothing more.
Tip — Contact the competent authority or an approved maintenance organization as early as possible in your project to learn the exact procedure that applies to your aircraft and your situation: it can differ significantly depending on the country of registration and the reason for the request.
