Buying a Used Aircraft in Ireland or the UK: Which Country to Choose

Market, prices, registration, post-Brexit taxation and pitfalls to avoid: here's how to weigh up Ireland against the UK before buying a used light aircraft.

Two neighbouring markets, two different dynamics

Ireland and the UK sit close together and share a long general aviation tradition, yet their used-aircraft markets now run on quite different logic. The British market, larger and longer established, lists a noticeably higher volume of aircraft, spanning everything from classic touring single-engines to business jets and helicopters. The Irish market is smaller and turns over more slowly, which can stretch out the search for a specific model.

Since the UK left the European Union, Ireland has also taken on particular importance for continental buyers who want to stay inside the EASA regulatory framework without the extra paperwork now attached to an aircraft registered in the UK. That difference in regulatory status is probably the single biggest factor in choosing between the two countries.

Prices and aircraft availability

Price ranges in Ireland and the UK broadly track the rest of Europe for aircraft of the same category and engine potential, but availability differs noticeably. The larger pool of listings in the UK generally makes it easier to find a wider spread of production years and configurations for the same model, a real advantage when it comes to negotiating.

Registration: the IAA in Ireland, the CAA in the UK

In Ireland, aircraft registration falls under the Irish Aviation Authority (IAA), using the EI- prefix, within the EASA framework shared with the rest of the European Union. In the UK, the Civil Aviation Authority (CAA) runs the national register under the G- prefix, under a British regulatory framework that has stood apart from EASA since Brexit.

  • Valid certificate of airworthiness and ARC — check this before signing in either country; the expiry date determines the first flight under your name.
  • Bill of sale — dated, signed by both parties, stating the serial number.
  • Deregistration from the previous register — mandatory whenever an aircraft changes country of registration, whether moving onto the IAA or CAA register.
Good to know — A transfer onto the Irish register, within the shared EASA framework, is generally the simpler route for an aircraft already registered in another EU member state. A transfer onto the British register means separately checking the aircraft meets the CAA's own requirements, some of which have diverged from EASA's since Brexit.

VAT, customs and the impact of Brexit

A sale between private individuals resident in the same country is generally not subject to local VAT, whether in Ireland or the UK. The real difference lies at the customs border created by Brexit: an aircraft moving today between the UK and the European Union (which includes Ireland) can face customs formalities and, in some cases, import VAT, whereas an intra-EU move onto the Irish register sits outside that framework entirely.

SituationTax and customs treatment
Sale between private individuals in Ireland (intra-EU)Generally VAT-exempt, no customs formality
Sale between private individuals in the UKGenerally VAT-exempt at national level
Aircraft moved between the UK and the EUCustoms formalities, import VAT possible depending on the asset's status

It's worth having your customs and tax position confirmed before any purchase that involves moving the aircraft between the two countries, especially if it has already changed country of registration more than once since Brexit.

Pre-purchase inspection and documents

In both countries, a pre-purchase inspection by a certified mechanic independent of the seller remains the best protection against unpleasant surprises. It covers the condition of the airframe, the consistency of the engine logbook, maintenance history and avionics compliance with the requirements of the destination country.

  • Up-to-date airframe, engine and propeller logbooks, with no chronological gaps.
  • History of maintenance invoices from the last three years.
  • Avionics compliant with the rules applicable in the intended country of registration, EASA for Ireland, UK rules for the UK.
Point of caution — Never finalise a purchase without having seen the original logbooks and had their consistency checked by a professional qualified on type, whether they're based in Ireland or the UK.

Pitfalls to avoid in each country

In the UK, the most common mistake is underestimating the impact of post-Brexit customs formalities on an aircraft you later intend to bring back onto the European continent, including into Ireland. In Ireland, the most common pitfall is the relative scarcity of certain highly sought-after models, which can push some buyers into rushing a decision without comparing enough similar listings first.

Tip — Always compare several similar listings on Aircraft2Sell across both countries before committing: it gives you an objective sense of fair price and helps you anticipate any customs steps tied to the aircraft's final destination country.

Which country suits your profile

A buyer based in the European Union who wants to stay firmly within the EASA framework, without any customs formalities, will generally find Ireland the simpler option over the long run, at the cost of a somewhat narrower pool of available aircraft at any given moment.

A buyer who prioritises volume of listings and model diversity above all, and who has no plans to bring the aircraft back onto the European continent in the near term, may find the UK the deeper market, provided the CAA registration steps specific to that country are factored in from the outset.

Either way, the method stays the same: compare several similar listings, scrutinise the engine history and logbook, and arrange an independent pre-purchase inspection before signing anything, whichever country you end up choosing.
