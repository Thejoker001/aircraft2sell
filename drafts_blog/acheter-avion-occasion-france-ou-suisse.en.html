Buying a used aircraft in France or Switzerland: which country to choose

Market, prices, tax, registration and maintenance: a comparison between France and Switzerland to help you decide where to search for your used aircraft.

Two neighbouring markets, a different regulatory framework

France and Switzerland share a long common border and a strong tradition of light aviation, but their regulatory and tax frameworks differ on one essential point: France is a member of the European Union, while Switzerland is not, even though it applies common EASA aviation safety rules under its bilateral agreement with the EU. This difference in status has concrete consequences for taxation and the formalities to expect in a cross-border purchase.

A French buyer can perfectly well find an interesting aircraft in Switzerland, and vice versa, provided they understand the customs and tax specifics of each market before entering into a cross-border transaction.

Size and activity of the used market

France has a very large fleet of light aircraft, supported by a particularly dense network of flying clubs and a long-standing tradition of popular aviation. This translates into an abundant supply on the used market, especially for the most common single-engine piston aircraft, with a substantial choice available across the whole country.

Switzerland has a smaller market in volume, proportionate to the size of the country, but known for the rigour of its maintenance and the quality of its technical documentation. The country's mountainous terrain has also fostered a specific mountain-flying tradition, with a notable proportion of aircraft equipped for this type of operation (skis, reinforced landing gear) on certain segments.

Comparing the prices charged

Price levels between the two markets remain broadly comparable for the same model and the same maintenance condition, with observed differences owing more to local supply and demand at any given time than to a structural difference between the two countries. Switzerland, with a generally higher cost of living and maintenance rates, sometimes shows aircraft listed at a slightly higher level for equivalent potential, particularly when the price is set in Swiss francs and then converted.

In both cases, comparing several listings of similar models remains the best way to assess a fair price before committing, regardless of which country the aircraft you want is located in. Factor the day's EUR/CHF exchange rate into your comparison, as it can noticeably change the apparent gap between two listings.

Tax and VAT: Switzerland outside the European Union

This is the most important difference between the two countries. France applies European Union VAT rules: a sale between private individuals is generally not subject to French VAT, while a sale by a professional may be subject to it. Switzerland, not being an EU member, applies its own national VAT at a rate distinct from that charged within the European Union, and any aircraft brought into Switzerland from abroad may be subject to customs formalities and Swiss import VAT, independent of the intra-EU VAT rules applicable between EU member states.

Conversely, an aircraft bought in Switzerland and then imported into France (or any other EU country) is treated as an import from a third country, with specific customs and tax formalities to check before the transaction — quite different from those applicable between two EU member states.

SituationTax treatment
Sale between resident individuals, aircraft staying in the same countryGenerally VAT-exempt
Purchase in France, import into SwitzerlandCustoms formalities and Swiss import VAT to check

Point of caution — For a cross-border transaction between France and Switzerland, have your tax and customs situation confirmed in advance by a professional familiar with this type of deal: the consequences of a procedural mistake can be costly, since Switzerland is not subject to the EU's intra-community VAT rules.

Registration and administrative steps

An aircraft bought in Switzerland can be registered in France, and vice versa, depending on the buyer's choice: keeping the registration of the purchase country is not mandatory. Switzerland applies common EASA airworthiness standards thanks to its bilateral agreement with the European Union, which makes mutual recognition of technical documents between the two countries simpler than a transfer from a country entirely outside the EASA system. The change of registration, however, involves specific steps with the civil aviation authority of the destination country, including submitting the complete technical file and checking compliance of the Certificate of Airworthiness (CofA).

  • Check the consistency between the logbook, the technical file and the aircraft's actual condition before any change of registration.
  • Plan for administrative lead times, which can vary significantly between the French authority (DGAC) and the Swiss authority (FOCA).
  • Check the customs formalities applicable when crossing the border, since Switzerland is not part of the EU customs union.
  • Budget for ferry-flight costs if an inspection or a compliance fix is needed before the registration transfer.

Maintenance network and parts availability

Both countries have a network of approved maintenance workshops for the most common models, which makes routine maintenance straightforward regardless of the country of purchase or final registration. The geographic proximity between France and Switzerland also makes it easy to have an aircraft maintained in either country without major constraints, as long as you check that the chosen workshop is indeed approved for the type of aircraft concerned.

Hourly labour rates at maintenance workshops are generally higher in Switzerland than in France, which can weigh on the routine maintenance budget of an aircraft based in Switzerland. For rarer models or specific engines, it can be useful to check in advance the availability of parts and the proximity of a specialised workshop before finalising the purchase, regardless of the aircraft's country of origin.

Which country to favour depending on your profile

For a buyer who favours a wide choice and more affordable routine maintenance costs, searching primarily on the French market often remains the simplest solution. For a buyer based in Switzerland, or looking for an aircraft with a reputedly particularly rigorous maintenance record, the Swiss market remains an option worth considering, bearing in mind the customs and tax formalities specific to this non-EU country.

Good to know — In all cases, a pre-purchase inspection by a qualified independent mechanic remains essential, whether the aircraft is located in France, Switzerland or elsewhere in Europe.

Ultimately, the country of purchase matters less than the actual condition of the aircraft and the quality of its maintenance record. Comparing listings from both markets widens your options, provided you correctly anticipate the customs and tax formalities specific to a transaction involving Switzerland.
