Used Beechcraft King Air 350: buying guide

Budget to plan for, inspection points to watch, maintenance and resale outlook to help you buy a used Beechcraft King Air 350, the flagship twin-turboprop in the Beechcraft lineup.

Why the King Air 350 appeals to demanding operators

The Beechcraft King Air 350 is the largest and most capable variant of the renowned King Air family, a twin-turboprop designed for business transport and demanding missions. With a longer cabin than the 200 series and a greater payload capability, the 350 targets corporate operators, on-demand charter companies and government agencies looking for the versatility of a twin turboprop without the operating costs of an equivalently sized business jet.

On the used market, the King Air 350 comes in several successive generations, with notable developments in cockpit avionics (a gradual move toward integrated digital suites), winglets and engine fit. This diversity of model years explains a wide price range, which makes it essential to target your budget and intended use carefully before starting a search.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used King Air 350 depends above all on the age of the aircraft, total airframe and engine hours, remaining potential before the next major engine overhaul, the level of avionics fitted and the cabin configuration (seat count, VIP or utility layout). The oldest examples, with reduced engine potential and analogue avionics, trade at a noticeably more accessible entry level than recent integrated-avionics versions, which represent a far larger investment.

Beyond the purchase price, your budget should include ancillary costs: a pre-purchase inspection at a shop approved on the type, registration and transfer fees, insurance (noticeably higher than for a light single-engine aircraft) and, if needed, an avionics or cabin interior refresh.

What to check before buying

As with any used aircraft, and even more so for an aircraft in this category, a pre-purchase inspection carried out by an approved maintenance centre experienced on the type is essential before finalising a transaction. For a King Air 350, a few points deserve particular attention.

  • Complete engine history: engine hours, SMOH (hours elapsed since the last major overhaul), compliance with the manufacturer's maintenance program, traceability of replaced parts.
  • Condition of the structure and cabin pressurisation, particularly for aircraft that have flown extensively at altitude over many years.
  • Operation and condition of the landing gear, hydraulic systems and de-icing equipment, which are worked harder than on a light single-engine aircraft.
  • Condition and currency of the avionics, as well as compliance with current regulatory requirements for the intended commercial or private operation.
  • Complete logbook and technical file, with no grey areas in the aircraft's history, particularly if it has had several owners or operators.
Good to know — Never rely solely on the seller's statements about overall condition: a written independent inspection report from a shop approved on the type remains the best protection against a bad surprise after the purchase.

PT6A engines and remaining potential

The King Air 350 is powered by two Pratt & Whitney Canada PT6A-family turbines, well known for their reliability and longevity in business and utility aviation. The single most decisive factor at purchase remains the remaining potential before the next major overhaul: the higher this potential, the closer the aircraft's value sits to the top of the market range, since a major overhaul on an engine of this category represents a significant expense to plan for.

Before buying, it is essential to request the complete history of each engine from the seller, the number of SMOH hours, and to check whether the engines are covered by an hourly maintenance program through the engine manufacturer or a third-party provider. An aircraft covered by such a program secures the future maintenance budget and often eases resale.

Maintenance and operating costs

The operating cost of a King Air 350 is noticeably higher than that of a light single-engine aircraft, consistent with its category as a twin-turboprop business aircraft: fuel, scheduled maintenance, insurance premiums and parking are significant expense items to factor into the ownership plan. In return, the aircraft offers availability and versatility (short missions, unprepared strips, all-weather flying) that justify this cost level for many professional operators.

Having the aircraft maintained at an approved shop specialised in the King Air range helps limit unpleasant surprises and preserve its resale value. Rigorous, well-documented maintenance records, in particular through the logbook and manufacturer programs, are a strong argument at a future resale.

Tip — Always request the complete maintenance history and the status of engine programs before making an offer: they give an accurate picture of how seriously the aircraft has been operated.

Flying, crew and type training

The King Air 350 requires a type rating and specific training, generally delivered by approved training organisations specialised in the Beechcraft King Air range. Depending on the regulatory framework and intended use (private or commercial operation), a single qualified pilot may be sufficient, but many operators prefer a two-pilot crew for the most demanding missions.

Before buying, it is worth anticipating the cost and lead time for type training for the pilot(s) who will operate the aircraft, as well as the availability of dedicated simulators, a point that is often underestimated in the overall project budget.

Resale and the used market

The King Air 350 benefits from an active used market worldwide, supported by a large installed fleet in business aviation, government agencies and certain air ambulance or utility operators. A well-maintained aircraft, with a transparent history, engines under program and up-to-date avionics, generally resells more easily and on better terms than one whose technical file has grey areas.

Comparing several listings of similar models remains the best way to assess a fair price, both for buying and for preparing a future resale. Price differences between two aircraft of the same model year are almost always explained by remaining engine potential, cabin configuration and the quality of the avionics fitted.
