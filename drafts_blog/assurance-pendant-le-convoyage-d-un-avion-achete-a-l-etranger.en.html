Insurance During the Ferry Flight of an Aircraft Bought Abroad

Between signing the sale and completing final registration, an aircraft bought abroad passes through a grey zone for insurance. Here is how to cover the ferry flight correctly.

Why ferry flight insurance is a topic of its own

Buying an aircraft abroad almost always means flying it yourself to its destination airfield, or to a workshop for compliance work. That ferry flight, sometimes several hundred or thousand kilometres long, happens in a peculiar administrative situation: the aircraft has just changed ownership, is not always registered yet under the new owner's name, and may even temporarily keep its country-of-origin registration.

This transitional period raises a simple but often overlooked question: who is actually covered, for what, and until when? A standard insurance policy, designed for routine operation from a stable home base, does not automatically cover this one-off flight, sometimes international, carried out while the paperwork is still incomplete.

The coverage gap between purchase and registration

Once the bill of sale is signed, the seller's existing policy usually stops applying, or drops to the strict legal minimum of the country of origin. The buyer, meanwhile, often cannot yet take out a definitive policy: that typically requires a finalised registration, a known home airfield, and sometimes a prior inspection of the aircraft by the insurer.

Between these two moments, the aircraft can therefore end up flying without proper cover if nobody thought about it explicitly. It is precisely this window — often a few days, sometimes a few weeks if customs or certification formalities are added — that ferry flight insurance is designed to cover.

Caution — Never assume the seller's insurance stays valid "just for the ferry flight," even with their verbal agreement. Get written confirmation from the insurer itself of the exact terms of cover up to the precise moment ownership transfers.

Ferry flight insurance options

Several solutions exist to close this coverage gap, depending on flight duration, distance and the profile of the pilot flying the aircraft home.

  • Ferry flight policy — taken out specifically for the transfer flight(s), valid for a limited number of days.
  • Rider on the definitive policy — some insurers agree to activate the final coverage early, with a specific clause for the ferry leg.
  • Specialist ferry broker policy — some aviation brokers run dedicated master contracts for international transfer flights, useful when the route crosses several countries.
  • Temporary extension of the seller's policy — in some cases, negotiated contractually before the sale, to cover the transfer before ownership fully switches over.

What ferry flight insurance must cover

A proper ferry flight policy should cover, at a minimum, third-party liability (required in almost every airspace crossed) and hull damage to the aircraft itself, based on its actual purchase price rather than a rough guess.

Also check the exact geographic scope: a policy limited to a single country does not protect a route that crosses several borders. If the ferry flight involves an extended stopover, several days parked at an intermediate airfield, or a change of pilot en route, make sure each of those situations is explicitly covered by the contract and not simply "tolerated" by default.

Documents and conditions to gather before the flight

Before departure, gather every document that could be requested in the event of a check or a claim during the ferry flight.

  • Signed, dated bill of sale, stating the effective date of ownership transfer.
  • Ferry flight insurance certificate, in English or the language of the countries overflown if required.
  • Up-to-date logbook and engine logbook, to confirm the aircraft's airworthiness status.
  • Overflight and transit permits, where the route crosses countries outside the pilot's usual airspace.
  • Ferry pilot's licence and ratings, matching the requirements for the aircraft type and the countries overflown.

Professional ferry pilot or the buyer flying it home

Two options exist for the ferry flight itself: hiring a professional pilot specialised in this kind of mission, or flying the aircraft home yourself if you hold the necessary ratings. Each option has a direct bearing on insurance.

A specialist ferry pilot usually carries their own cover, or works with insurers familiar with this kind of flight, which simplifies things for the buyer. Conversely, a buyer who ferries their own aircraft needs to check that their experience on type, recent flying hours and ratings (instrument rating where applicable) exactly match what the insurer requires — a gap on this point alone can be enough to invalidate cover in the event of a claim.

The special case of intercontinental ferry flights

Ferrying an aircraft bought outside Europe — in the United States, for example — adds further layers of complexity: a sea crossing (by container ship, or flown across depending on the aircraft's range), stopovers in countries with different aviation regulations, and customs formalities at every border.

In this context, ferry flight insurance must explicitly cover every leg of the journey, including any extended stopovers while waiting for a weather window or an administrative clearance. A broker specialised in aircraft importation, familiar with this kind of route, is usually better placed than a generalist insurer to offer cover genuinely suited to a complex international journey.

Pre-departure checklist

  • The ferry flight policy is confirmed in writing, with validity dates covering the entire planned route, plus a margin.
  • Geographic coverage includes every country and airspace crossed, including stopovers.
  • The insured value of the aircraft matches the actual purchase price, not an arbitrary estimate.
  • The ferry pilot(s) are named individually in the contract, with their ratings verified.
  • A copy of the insurance certificate is carried on board, alongside the usual airworthiness documents.
Tip — Arrange ferry flight insurance as early as the purchase price negotiation, not the night before departure. A specialist broker often needs several days to issue a policy suited to an international route, especially if the aircraft or the pilot fall outside a standard profile.

It is also worth planning for delay: weather windows, customs clearance or a required maintenance item discovered en route can push a ferry flight back by days. Confirm with the insurer, before departure, exactly how an extended stopover or a change of plan mid-route is handled, rather than assuming the original policy quietly stretches to cover it.

Finally, keep in mind that a poorly documented ferry flight can also complicate the aircraft's paper trail for years afterwards. A clean, written record of exactly who was insured, for what, and on which exact dates during the transfer is something a future buyer's insurer — or your own, at the next renewal — may eventually ask to see.
