Negotiating the Price of a Used Aircraft: Concrete Methods

Preparing your case, using the pre-purchase inspection report, and picking the right moment: concrete methods for negotiating the price of a used aircraft effectively.

Why negotiating an aircraft differs from negotiating a car

The used aircraft market is far smaller and less standardised than the car market: every airframe has its own maintenance history, logbook and equipment fit, and two examples of the same model can be worth very different amounts. This lack of standardisation works in favour of a well-prepared buyer: a seller cannot point to a single "blue book" value the way a car seller can, which leaves real room for discussion once the buyer brings factual evidence to the table.

On the other hand, the limited number of serious buyers for any given model can work in the seller's favour, especially for a rare or highly sought-after type. Effective negotiation therefore depends less on raw leverage than on the quality of the preparation and information gathered before the conversation even starts.

Preparing your negotiation before the first viewing

Before even contacting the seller, build a comparison file: several listings of identical or similar models, with engine hours, hours since major overhaul (SMOH) and equipment level, let you establish a realistic price range for the aircraft you are targeting. Also note how long the listing has been live: an aircraft that has sat on the market for a while, or whose asking price has already been reduced, points to a seller who may be more open to negotiation.

  • Compare at least 3 to 5 listings of comparable models to position the asking price against the real market, not just the seller's pitch.
  • Ask for the logbook and engine logbook in advance to spot any gaps before travelling to view the aircraft.
  • Identify the reason for the sale (upgrading to another aircraft, leaving aviation, an estate sale): a motivated seller is often more open to a quick negotiation.
  • Set your personal ceiling price in advance, independent of any enthusiasm generated by the viewing itself.

Using the pre-purchase inspection report

An independent mechanic's report from the pre-purchase inspection remains the strongest negotiating lever, because it rests on documented technical findings rather than on an impression. Every defect noted, however minor, has a quantifiable repair cost: worn tyres, a battery nearing the end of its life, paint that needs redoing, an airworthiness directive to address before the next major overhaul.

The most effective approach is to get each item quoted by a workshop before going back to the seller, rather than throwing out an approximate percentage discount. A price reduction request backed by a precise quote is far harder to dispute than a vague general impression of the aircraft's condition.

Point of caution — Never confuse a minor defect, which justifies a reasonable discount, with a structural issue or an unresolved major airworthiness directive, which may justify simply walking away rather than negotiating.

The arguments that actually lower a price

Some arguments carry far more weight in negotiation than others. Engine hours approaching the SMOH limit represent a certain, quantifiable future expense, often tens of thousands of euros, which makes this one of the strongest arguments available. An incomplete or poorly kept maintenance history reduces a buyer's confidence and justifies an additional margin of caution, even in the absence of any confirmed technical defect.

  • Approaching the engine's major overhaul — a certain future cost, easy to quote through an approved workshop.
  • Outdated or non-compliant equipment — old avionics, missing Mode S transponder or ADS-B required under your applicable airspace rules.
  • Incomplete logbook — gaps in the history that justify a precautionary discount.
  • Defects found during inspection — quoted by a workshop, not estimated verbally.
  • Extended time on the market — a signal the initial asking price likely exceeds the real market value.

Conversely, purely subjective arguments ("I think it's expensive", "that's not in my budget") carry far less weight against an informed seller, because they rest on nothing verifiable.

Choosing the right moment to negotiate

Timing matters almost as much as the arguments themselves. Opening a price discussion before you have seen the aircraft, or before receiving the inspection report, weakens your position, because you end up negotiating on assumptions rather than confirmed facts. The most effective sequence is generally: viewing and test flight, then pre-purchase inspection, then final negotiation based on the resulting report.

A firm offer presented promptly after the inspection, with a short validity window, also signals seriousness and can push the seller to close rather than wait for another hypothetical buyer. Conversely, a negotiation that drags on for weeks without progress gives another buyer time to step in.

Mistakes that derail a negotiation

A few mistakes come up repeatedly and damage a buyer's credibility. Opening with a very large discount request with no technical justification creates distrust that complicates the rest of the discussion. Stacking up small successive demands (price, then included equipment, then ferry costs) after a price has already been agreed in principle gives an impression of bad faith and can derail a negotiation that was almost closed.

  • Negotiating without comparing the real market, relying on intuition alone.
  • Mixing several demands together (price, equipment, ferry costs) instead of presenting one clear position.
  • Verbally talking down the aircraft in front of the seller instead of relying on a written report.
  • Walking back a price already agreed in principle with no new information, which destroys the trust already built.

Closing: a written offer and conditional clauses

Once a price is agreed, formalise it in writing promptly, even as a simple dated summary email, before a change of mind or another buyer's interest puts everything back in question. The final sale agreement should state the agreed price, the list of included equipment, and any conditions still outstanding (financing, a final documentation check).

Tip — Include a "subject to satisfactory inspection" clause if the inspection has not yet taken place when the price is agreed in principle, with the deposit refundable if a major defect is later discovered.

A successful negotiation is not the one that secures the largest discount, but the one that ends at a fair price for both parties, based on shared factual evidence. It is also the best way to preserve a relationship of trust that stays useful afterwards: access to the full logbook, sharing of technical information, or even a referral to other sellers in the same network.
