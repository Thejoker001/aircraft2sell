Why the RV-10 appeals to so many owner-pilots

The Vans RV-10 is the only four-seat model in the RV range from American manufacturer Vans Aircraft, whose two-seat RV-7 and RV-8 have already proven themselves with amateur builders worldwide. Designed to carry a family of four with luggage while keeping high cruise performance, the RV-10 occupies a particular place on the market: it combines the structural simplicity and flying pleasure of the two-seat RVs with a cabin that is genuinely usable for trips with four people on board.

On the used market, available examples almost always come from an individual build assembled from a kit over several years of amateur construction. This origin explains both the model's appeal — often highly personalised in avionics and finish — and the particular care it requires when buying: every example is, to some extent, unique.

An experimental kit aircraft, not a microlight: what this means

The RV-10 is often mentioned in light-aviation discussions alongside microlights or light two-seaters, but that comparison is misleading. The RV-10 is not a microlight in the regulatory sense: it is an aircraft in the experimental / amateur-built (kit-built) category, built from a kit sold by Vans Aircraft and assembled by a private individual under the supervision of an amateur-build program. Its weight, performance and typical use (four seats, fast cruise, often IFR-capable) place it in the category of certified or equivalent light aircraft, well beyond the weight and speed limits that define a microlight in Europe.

This distinction has real consequences for a buyer: the applicable regulatory framework (registration, maintenance, ownership transfer) is not that of a microlight, and varies noticeably from one European country to another depending on how each national authority regulates experimental or amateur-built aircraft. Before any purchase, it is essential to check with the civil aviation authority of the intended country of registration on the exact status that will apply to the aircraft and the conditions for maintaining its airworthiness.

Key point — The RV-10 is a four-seat experimental kit aircraft, not a microlight. Never rely on a listing that wrongly describes it as a microlight to infer operating or licensing rules: always check the exact status with the competent authority.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used RV-10 varies considerably depending on the quality of the original amateur build, the level of avionics fitted (a full glass cockpit versus simpler instrumentation), total engine and airframe hours, and the reputation of the original builder when documented. Price differences between two aircraft of the same model year can be significant, far more so than for a series-produced certified aircraft, precisely because each example is the result of individual workmanship.

Assessing the quality of the amateur build

For an aircraft in the experimental kit-built category, the quality of the original builder's work is the single most decisive factor, even more so than for a certified aircraft whose manufacture is standardised in a factory. A well-built RV-10, with materials conforming to the kit specifications and careful assembly, behaves like any reliable light aircraft. A poorly assembled example, on the other hand, can hide defects that are hard to detect without a targeted expert inspection.

  • Complete build log: assembly photos, parts invoices, stages signed off by a qualified inspector during construction.
  • Identity of the original builder and, if possible, their previous experience on other amateur-build projects.
  • History of the initial test flights and adjustments made after the aircraft first entered service.
  • Consistency between the kit originally stated and any modifications made during construction.

What to check before buying

A pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic qualified on experimental kit-built aircraft, ideally familiar with Vans Aircraft designs, remains essential. It should cover both the usual points for any used aircraft and the specifics of an amateur-built example.

  • Condition of the structure and riveted assemblies, checking for corrosion or undocumented repairs.
  • Up-to-date logbook and engine logbook, with no chronological gaps since the aircraft entered service.
  • Complete engine history: total hours, SMOH, compliance with the engine manufacturer's recommended maintenance program.
  • Validity of the certificate of airworthiness (or equivalent document for the experimental category) and its associated conditions.
  • Compliance of the installed avionics with current regulatory requirements (transponder, traffic-monitoring systems).
Point of caution — On an amateur-built aircraft, never skip checking the original build log: its absence or incompleteness is a red flag that should significantly lower your offer, or even lead you to walk away from the purchase.

Maintenance and operating costs

The operating cost of an RV-10 remains broadly comparable to that of a certified four-seat light single-engine aircraft of equivalent power, with the advantage that maintenance can often be carried out by the owner or under the owner's supervision, depending on the rules applicable to experimental aircraft in the country of registration. This flexibility can reduce certain costs, provided the owner has the necessary skills and discipline.

The main expense items remain the annual inspection, insurance, parking and, in the longer term, the engine's major overhaul once the hourly potential is reached. Using a mechanic experienced with Vans kits for the more technical work helps preserve the aircraft's resale value.

Tip — Always ask for the complete history of work carried out since construction, including anything done by the owner: traceability is a strong argument at resale.

Registration in Europe

Transferring the registration of an experimental kit-built aircraft between European countries follows rules specific to each national civil aviation authority, distinct from those applicable to series-produced certified aircraft or microlights. Before finalising a cross-border purchase, it is essential to check with the competent authority of the destination country on the precise conditions for recognising or re-registering an aircraft in this category, as well as any operating limitations that might result.

This point deserves particular attention if you are buying an RV-10 built and initially registered in a country other than the one where you plan to base it: timelines and documentation requirements can differ noticeably from those for a certified aircraft.

Resale and the used market

The RV-10 benefits from an active community of owners and builders, which makes it easier to source parts, technical advice and first-hand experience on specific examples. A well-built aircraft, properly documented and rigorously maintained, generally resells without particular difficulty, often to pilot-owners already familiar with the Vans range.

As with any used aircraft, the transparency of the technical file and the quality of maintenance records remain the best arguments for achieving a fair price, whether buying or preparing a future resale.
