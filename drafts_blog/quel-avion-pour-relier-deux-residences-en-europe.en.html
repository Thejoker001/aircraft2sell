What aircraft for commuting between two homes in Europe

Piston single, light twin or turboprop: how to choose the aircraft best suited to flying regularly back and forth between two homes in Europe.

A recurring commute between two homes

Owning two homes in Europe — for example a primary residence and a second home in another country — changes the nature of the flying need compared with occasional leisure flying. The trip becomes recurring, often weekly or monthly, over a fixed and known distance. That regularity completely changes the criteria for choosing an aircraft: the question is no longer just "which aircraft is the most enjoyable to fly," but "which aircraft lets me make this specific trip reliably, at an operating cost I can sustain over time."

A 300 km trip does not call for the same aircraft as a 1,200 km trip, and the frequency of rotations weighs just as much as distance in the real annual budget.

The criteria that determine the right choice

Before comparing specific models, several parameters specific to your situation need clarifying: the exact distance between the two homes, the length and surface of the runways available at both ends, the number of passengers and bags usually carried, how often the trip is made during the year, and your tolerance for the higher operating cost of a twin compared with a single.

  • Actual distance between the two homes and the corresponding flight time for each aircraft type.
  • Length, surface and elevation of the runways available at both ends of the route.
  • Number of passengers and volume of luggage carried on each leg.
  • Annual frequency of the trip, which determines how much weight fixed costs carry in the budget.

Piston single: simplicity and a controlled budget

For moderate distances (roughly up to 600–800 km depending on the engine), a modern piston single such as a Cirrus SR22, a Piper PA28, a Socata TB or a Mooney is often the most rational choice. These aircraft offer a good cruise speed, a controlled operating cost, and easier maintenance than a twin, since only one engine needs following. This is the preferred option for an owner who makes the trip regularly but has no absolute need for engine redundancy on the route.

Light twin: safety and versatility

When the route crosses terrain that would be hard to deal with in the event of an engine failure — mountains, open sea, large sparsely populated areas — or when more payload is needed, a light twin such as a Diamond DA42 brings welcome engine redundancy and greater payload capacity. That extra safety margin comes at a higher operating cost, due to maintaining two engines and two propellers, plus generally higher insurance.

Single-engine turboprop: speed for long distances

For longer trips flown frequently, a fast single-engine turboprop can meaningfully cut flight time and makes it easier to fly above much of the weather, while remaining a single-engine aircraft that is simpler to maintain than a twin. This type of aircraft represents a significantly higher investment to buy and operate, but can be justified once distance or trip frequency make the time saved decisive.

Budget comparison by distance

The right choice depends above all on the combination of distance and frequency: a piston single remains the most economical option for short, regular trips, a twin is justified when the safety profile of the route calls for it, and a turboprop is justified when distance and the value of time saved outweigh operating cost.

Choosing based on flight frequency and runway profile

The more often the trip is flown, the more the fixed cost of ownership (insurance, hangar, periodic maintenance) spreads across a larger number of rotations and becomes secondary to the variable hourly cost. Conversely, for infrequent trips, a high fixed cost weighs more heavily on the overall budget, which can point toward a simpler aircraft, or even toward renting or co-ownership rather than outright ownership.

The runway profile available at both ends of the route also matters a great deal: a short, grass or high-elevation runway may rule out aircraft that cruise well but are less suited to short take-off and landing distances.

  • High frequency: prioritise a controlled variable cost over the lowest possible initial investment.
  • Low frequency: consider renting, co-ownership or a simpler aircraft before buying outright.
  • Always check the aircraft you are considering against the most demanding runway on the route.

Points to check when buying used

Whether it is a single, a twin or a turboprop, an aircraft intended for recurring use on the same route often accumulates a high number of hours and cycles. Carefully check the logbook, the engine hours since major overhaul (SMOH), and whether the maintenance history is consistent with the use declared by the seller.

Tip — Always have a pre-purchase inspection carried out by an independent mechanic before committing, and request the full logbook rather than a summary provided by the seller.

Comparing several listings of equivalent models remains the best way to assess a fair price and spot a well-maintained aircraft suited to your regular trip between the two homes.
