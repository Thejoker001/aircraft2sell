Impact of Fuel Prices and SAF on the Used Aircraft Market

How changing aviation fuel prices and the rise of Sustainable Aviation Fuel (SAF) are reshaping running costs, buying decisions and resale values for used aircraft.

Fuel: a central cost item

For a light aircraft owner, fuel has historically been one of the most visible operating cost items, alongside maintenance, insurance and hangar fees. Unlike these other items, which tend to move relatively gradually, aviation fuel prices can swing quite sharply from one season to the next or from one airfield to another, driven by geopolitical and logistical factors largely outside the owner's control.

This volatility weighs directly on a buyer's forecast budget: an aircraft that sips fuel becomes more attractive when prices are high, while a thirstier but cheaper-to-buy model can remain competitive when fuel stays affordable. The used-aircraft market reacts to these signals, sometimes with a lag of several months.

What SAF is and where things stand

Sustainable Aviation Fuel (SAF) is a family of fuels produced from renewable sources or waste streams, usable as a partial or full replacement for fossil kerosene in existing engines without major modification. Its development is driven by the aviation sector's emissions-reduction targets, with blending mandates that are gradually stepping up in several European countries over the coming years.

SAF primarily concerns commercial aviation and, increasingly, turbine-powered business aviation. Its adoption in piston-engine light aviation remains marginal for now, as this segment mostly runs on avgas, whose environmental challenges and substitution pathways follow a separate timeline from that of SAF. We deliberately stay cautious about price gaps between SAF and conventional jet fuel: these gaps vary significantly by country, supplier and period, and any precise range quoted here would risk being out of date by the time you read it.

Good to know — SAF remains more expensive to produce than fossil kerosene today. Its price depends on the production technology, the country and the incentives in place: always verify any pricing figure against an up-to-date source before using it in a calculation.

Impact on the running cost of a used aircraft

The hourly operating cost of a used aircraft depends heavily on fuel burn, which is itself linked to the powerplant, the age of the airframe, and the type of flying done. A modern light single-engine aircraft, often more efficient than an equivalent older model, can offer a meaningful running-cost advantage as fuel prices rise, even if its purchase price is higher.

  • Hourly fuel burn — a criterion worth comparing systematically between two candidate models, just like the purchase price.
  • Planned type of flying — intensive use (flight schools, long trips) amplifies the impact of fuel prices on the annual budget far more than occasional use.
  • Powerplant — some newer or optimised engines deliver noticeably lower fuel burn at equivalent displacement or power.
Tip — Before buying, ask the seller or the flying club for real fuel-burn data from several typical flights, rather than relying solely on the manufacturer's figures, which reflect optimal test conditions.

Which models are most exposed

The aircraft most sensitive to a sustained rise in fuel prices are those with high hourly fuel burn relative to their actual use: some older twins, turboprops flown on short missions that don't justify their cruise speed, or first-generation business jets that are less fuel-efficient than newer models. Conversely, modern, fuel-optimised light singles and diesel or economy powerplants tend to hold up better, with their relative advantage strengthening as prices climb.

In the turbine segment, the fuel-burn gap between two engine generations can be substantial, which partly explains why the oldest aircraft within a given family often see a sharper price discount during periods of high fuel prices, regardless of their mechanical condition.

Effect on demand and buying decisions

When fuel prices rise on a sustained basis, demand in the used market tends to shift towards the most fuel-efficient aircraft in their category, while slowing for models seen as thirsty. This is not a new phenomenon: it has already been observed during previous episodes of rising fuel prices, and it generally shows up as a longer time-to-sell for less efficient aircraft, rather than a sudden collapse in asking prices.

The gradual tightening of SAF blending mandates in some countries could, over time, add a further variable for the business aviation and turbine segments, though it is not currently possible to quantify this effect precisely on the light used-aircraft market, which largely remains outside the direct scope of these mandates.

Effect on resale value

A buyer planning to hold an aircraft for several years should factor its fuel efficiency into their expected resale value, not just into their immediate running budget. A model known for being fuel-efficient generally holds its value better during periods of high fuel prices, as it remains sought after by future buyers facing the same budget constraints.

Word of caution — Don't underestimate the psychological effect of fuel prices on how potential buyers perceive an aircraft at resale time: even if the real cost over the holding period remains reasonable, a model perceived as thirsty can see its time-to-sell lengthen.

Strategies for buyers

Given the uncertainty around future fuel and SAF prices, a few simple habits make for calmer buying decisions. Always compare the real fuel burn of several candidate models before focusing on purchase price alone. Build fuel cost into a full annual budget alongside maintenance and insurance, rather than looking at it in isolation. Finally, keep in mind that an efficient aircraft remains a solid resale argument, whatever direction the fuel market takes next.
