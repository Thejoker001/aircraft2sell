Used Aerospatiale Alouette II (SA318): buying guide

Budget to plan for, Astazou engine specifics, routine maintenance, parts availability and the resale market when buying a used Alouette II.

A pioneering helicopter still flying today

The Aerospatiale Alouette II (SA318 family, descended from the original SE3130) holds a special place in light helicopter history. Designed in the 1950s, this two-to-four-seat helicopter was one of the first mass-produced turbine helicopters, thanks to the adoption of the Turbomeca Astazou engine (and earlier the Artouste, depending on the variant). Decades after production ended, a meaningful number of airframes remain airworthy across Europe, kept flying by specialised workshops and valued for their relative mechanical simplicity and lower acquisition cost compared with more recent turbine helicopters.

On the used market, available Alouette IIs come with very varied histories: some have had a continuous civilian career (flying school, aerial work, leisure), while others have a second life after a military or institutional career. This diversity of backgrounds calls for particular care over documentary traceability before any purchase.

What budget to plan for

The price of a used Alouette II depends above all on the condition of the airframe, the remaining potential of the engine and transmission, and how complete and consistent the technical records are. An airframe with reduced engine potential and an incomplete history will trade at a noticeably more accessible entry level than a well-documented example, recently overhauled and close to full potential.

Beyond the purchase price, budget for a thorough pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic specialised on the type, registration and transfer costs, insurance — often higher for a helicopter than for an equivalent light aircraft — and a possible avionics upgrade to meet the minimum requirements of the airspace you intend to fly in.

The Astazou engine: the core of the inspection

The Turbomeca Astazou turbine engine fitted to most airworthy Alouette IIs is both an asset — relative simplicity, proven long-term reliability — and the single heaviest cost item to anticipate. Its remaining potential decreases with flight hours and cycles, and its overhaul represents a major investment, proportionally comparable to a major overhaul on a high-end piston aircraft engine.

  • Always ask for the hours flown since the last engine overhaul and the potential remaining before the next due date.
  • Check the full traceability of the engine: serial number, history of interventions, strict compliance with the engine manufacturer's maintenance programme.
  • Have the transmission and main rotor condition checked by a mechanic specialised in helicopters, distinct from a generalist fixed-wing mechanic.
Watch out — On an aircraft this age, the gap between an engine close to exhausted potential and one recently overhauled can represent a very significant share of the asking price. Never negotiate without this figure in black and white.

What to check before buying

As with any used aircraft, but with added importance given the age of this type, a pre-purchase inspection by a qualified independent mechanic on the type is essential before finalising a transaction.

  • Condition of the airframe and tail boom: look for corrosion, cracks or undocumented prior repairs.
  • Complete logbook, engine logbook and technical file, with no chronological gaps in the aircraft's history.
  • Compliance with applicable airworthiness directives (AD) for the type, particularly on the rotor, transmission and engine.
  • Valid Certificate of Airworthiness (CofA) and consistency between the registration and the presented records.
  • Condition of any advertised optional equipment (reinforced skids, floats, external cargo hook) and compliance with the certified configuration.
Good to know — For an aircraft this old, favour a mechanic or workshop with proven, recent experience on the Alouette II specifically: deep knowledge of the type makes a real difference to the quality of the inspection.

Maintenance, parts and availability

Production of the Alouette II ended long ago, which raises the central question of spare parts availability for any prospective buyer. A network of specialised workshops and parts suppliers still exists in Europe, but lead times can be longer than for a modern helicopter still in production. Before buying, it is worth checking which sources of parts are available for the most critical components (transmission, rotor, engine) and the average lead times reported by current owners.

Having the aircraft maintained by a workshop recognised for its specific experience on this type, even if that means travelling further than your home airfield, is usually preferable to generic, less specialised maintenance. A rigorous, well-documented maintenance record is also a strong argument for a future resale.

Tip — Joining an Alouette II owners' network or association often gives access to valuable first-hand feedback on reliable workshops and parts sources, far more useful than generic information found online.

Current uses and operating constraints

Today, the Alouette II is mainly operated by enthusiast private owners, aviation heritage preservation associations, and occasionally for light aerial work where its simplicity and operating cost remain competitive against more recent helicopters. Its limited payload and generally basic avionics make it primarily a leisure or training aircraft rather than a modern intensive aerial-work tool.

Before buying, check that the aircraft's avionics configuration is compatible with the airspace you plan to operate in: an upgrade may be necessary for certain controlled airspace, depending on the regulations in force in the country of registration.

Resale and the used market

The used Alouette II market remains narrow compared with modern light helicopters such as the Robinson R44 or Airbus H125, with fewer airframes available for sale and a fairly targeted buyer base, often as passionate about the type's historical value as about its practical use. A well-maintained example, with a transparent technical record and comfortable remaining engine potential, generally sells more easily than one whose history leaves grey areas.

Comparing several listings of similar Alouette IIs remains the best way to assess a fair price, both when buying and when preparing a future resale. Price differences between two airframes of a similar generation almost always come down to remaining engine and transmission potential, plus the quality of the documentation.
