Crowdfunding the Purchase of a Flying Club Aircraft

Principles, campaign models, platforms, planning and governance: how a flying club can crowdfund the purchase of an aircraft.

What crowdfunding means for a flying club aircraft

Crowdfunding means raising money from a large number of contributors rather than relying on a single lender or a small group of investors. Applied to buying a flying club aircraft, this approach lets a club gather some or all of the required budget by reaching out to current members, former members still attached to the club, local supporters, and sometimes a wider public interested in general aviation.

This form of financing differs from a standard bank loan in its collective, community-driven nature: each backer contributes whatever amount they choose, often in exchange for a symbolic reward, flight hours, or a share in a structure set up to own the aircraft. The goal is not just to raise a sum of money, but also to rally a community of pilots and enthusiasts around the purchase project.

Why flying clubs turn to crowdfunding

A club aircraft is a significant investment, often hard to fund purely from the association's cash reserves or through a conventional loan, especially for a newer or well-equipped aircraft. Crowdfunding offers a complementary way to raise funds without overly increasing the club's debt load, while directly involving members in the success of the project.

Beyond the financial side, a crowdfunding campaign also has communication value: it raises the club's local profile, strengthens existing members' sense of belonging, and can attract new members drawn to a concrete collective project. A club that runs its campaign transparently, with regular updates, often gets a benefit that goes well beyond the amount actually raised.

The different campaign models

Several crowdfunding models can be adapted to buying a flying club aircraft, each with its own legal and financial implications.

  • Donation with a symbolic reward — backers receive a token of thanks (a discovery flight, club merchandise, a name on a plaque) with no expectation of financial return.
  • Peer-to-peer loan — members advance a repayable sum over several years, with or without interest, depending on the club's bylaws and the applicable regulations.
  • Equity share in a dedicated structure — a company or specific association is set up to own the aircraft, and each backer becomes a co-owner in proportion to their contribution.
  • Hybrid approach — a collective donation covers part of the budget while a conventional bank loan covers the balance, with crowdfunding acting as the initial deposit.

The choice of model largely depends on the club's legal status (non-profit association or equivalent, company), the amount to be raised, and how willing members are to commit financially beyond a simple donation.

Choosing a platform and staying within the legal framework

Many general-purpose crowdfunding platforms (donation or loan based) can host a campaign for a club aircraft, provided their terms accept association-led projects and the amounts targeted by the campaign. It is worth comparing the fees charged by the platform, how quickly raised funds are released, and the tracking tools offered to project organisers before committing.

Loan-based or equity-based crowdfunding is governed by regulations specific to each country and, for cross-border campaigns within the European Union, by a harmonised European framework for crowdfunding service providers. A club considering a campaign beyond simple donations should consult a professional (lawyer, non-profit accountant) to make sure the chosen structure complies with the rules applicable to its organisation and the target amount, since the exact thresholds and obligations vary according to the rules in force and the club's country.

Word of caution — Never present a collection as a financial investment with a return if it does not have that regulatory status. Reclassifying a donation as an unauthorised financial product can expose the club to penalties.

Running a campaign: the key steps

A successful campaign starts with a precise definition of the need: the type of aircraft targeted, the total budget, the share that crowdfunding is meant to cover, and the planned purchase timeline. Presenting a costed, credible project, ideally backed by a technical opinion on the targeted aircraft, reassures potential backers.

  • Set a realistic fundraising target, distinguishing between the minimum amount required and an optimal goal with a margin for ancillary costs.
  • Plan regular communication (the club's social media, newsletter, member meetings) before and during the campaign.
  • Offer rewards suited to each contribution tier, consistent with what the club can realistically deliver.
  • Set a reasonable campaign length, neither so short that it limits reach nor so long that momentum fades.
  • Plan for the scenario where the target is not met: refunding backers or redirecting funds to another club use, depending on the chosen platform's rules.

Governance and ownership of a crowdfunded aircraft

The question of who owns the aircraft should be settled before the campaign launches, not after. If the aircraft remains the property of the club (an existing association or company) and the campaign simply funds its purchase, governance does not fundamentally change: the aircraft is managed under the club's usual rules, made available to members under the standard scheme.

If, however, backers become co-owners through equity shares or a dedicated structure, the club needs to put in place suitable governance: a co-ownership agreement setting out usage rights, how maintenance and insurance costs are shared, and exit terms for a backer who wishes to sell their stake. This situation resembles a conventional private aircraft co-ownership, with the added feature of a potentially larger and more varied group of participants.

Risks and points to watch

The main risk of a crowdfunding campaign is failing to reach the set target, leaving the club without an alternative financing solution if the purchase was already committed with the seller. It is therefore wise to finalise the purchase only once funding is secured, or to negotiate a conditional clause with the seller while the campaign runs its course.

Another risk concerns managing the relationship with backers over time: a one-off donation creates little ongoing obligation, but a repayable loan or an equity share creates a lasting relationship that must be managed with the same rigour as a conventional financial partner, including if the club runs into difficulty honouring its commitments.

Good to know — Once the aircraft has been acquired, a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic remains essential, whatever financing method was used to raise the funds.

After the campaign: managing the aircraft and informing backers

Once the purchase is finalised, it is advisable to share a clear summary of the campaign with all backers: the final amount raised, how it breaks down across the different funding sources, and photos or information about the aircraft now part of the club's fleet. This transparency maintains trust and makes a future campaign easier to run, for example to fund maintenance or a future replacement aircraft.

For backers who opted for a repayable loan or an equity share, regular updates (a repayment schedule, an annual note on the aircraft's status) prevent misunderstandings and strengthen trust throughout the commitment. A club that handles this follow-up phase carefully gives itself a better chance of running a successful campaign again if the need arises.
