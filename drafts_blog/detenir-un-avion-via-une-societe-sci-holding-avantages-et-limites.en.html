Owning an aircraft through a company (SCI/holding): advantages and limits

French SCI, holding or commercial company: what you need to understand before structuring aircraft ownership through a company, weighing real estate-planning benefits against practical constraints.

Why consider a corporate structure for an aircraft

Buying a used aircraft as an individual remains the simplest solution for a strictly personal use. But as soon as a project involves several co-owners, early estate planning, or a wish to legally separate the aviation asset from personal wealth, the question of the ownership structure comes up quickly. Having a company hold title to the aircraft rather than one or several individuals makes it possible to organize governance, financing and capital exit rules much more clearly.

This approach is not reserved for professional fleets or business jets: it also applies to touring single-engine aircraft owned by a small group of friends or family members who prefer formalizing their relationship through bylaws rather than a simple joint-ownership arrangement.

The SCI: a tool built for real estate, not aviation

The French SCI (société civile immobilière) is historically designed to hold real estate, and its corporate purpose is legally centered on that activity. Housing an aircraft inside a standard SCI therefore raises a fundamental issue: the corporate purpose must be explicitly drafted or amended to cover the holding and management of a movable asset such as an aircraft, which is a departure from the usual purpose of this legal form.

Some owners have, for the sake of simplicity or out of unfamiliarity, tried to house an aircraft inside an existing SCI originally created for a property. This practice carries a risk of recharacterization or challenge of the corporate purpose, particularly in the event of a tax audit or a dispute between shareholders. A civil company can in theory be adapted to hold a movable asset, but it must be designed and drafted for that purpose from the outset, with dedicated bylaws — not simply reused as-is.

Holding or commercial company: a better-suited alternative

For aircraft ownership, many estate-planning setups favor a commercial company instead (a properly drafted civil company with a movable-asset purpose, a simplified joint-stock company, or a patrimonial holding) whose corporate purpose explicitly covers the acquisition, holding and operation of aircraft. A holding can also make it easier to link the aircraft with other professional or personal assets held by the same family group, and organize governance among several shareholders more clearly than a plain joint-ownership arrangement.

This type of structure also makes it easier to consider making the aircraft available to third parties (leasing, use by other shareholders, management entrusted to an operator), with clear internal contractual rules between the owning company and the aircraft's users.

Estate-planning and organizational advantages

  • Asset separation — the aircraft becomes a company asset, separate from the shareholders' personal wealth, which can limit certain risks if a shareholder runs into personal difficulties.
  • Formalized governance — bylaws and a shareholders' agreement define usage rules, cost sharing and decision-making far more precisely than an informal joint-ownership arrangement.
  • Easier transfer — transferring shares can, in some cases, be smoother than transferring a share of an asset held under direct joint ownership.
  • New co-investors joining — a new shareholder can join the capital without needing to change the aircraft's own title of ownership.
  • Clear accounting — aircraft-related costs (maintenance, insurance, major overhaul, fuel) are tracked in dedicated accounts, separate from the shareholders' personal finances.

These advantages are real, but they are not automatic: they depend entirely on the quality of the bylaws, the shareholders' agreement and the aircraft usage agreement, as well as on strict compliance with the company's operating rules over time.

Limits and pitfalls to anticipate

The first pitfall is the cost of setting up the structure: creating and running a company (drafting bylaws, annual accounting, filing formalities) represents a recurring cost that only makes economic sense above a certain investment level or a sufficient number of shareholders. For a lower-value single-engine aircraft owned by two people, a well-drafted joint-ownership agreement can remain simpler and cheaper than a dedicated company.

The second pitfall concerns the applicable tax regime, which varies significantly depending on the country, the type of company chosen and the actual use of the aircraft (personal, rental, professional). VAT rules, capital gains taxation on the transfer of shares, or expense deductibility differ considerably from one jurisdiction to another and change regularly. It would be unwise to state a precise rate or threshold here: each situation must be validated with a tax lawyer or accountant familiar with both company law and local aviation regulation.

Watch out — Never copy a structure designed for real estate (an SCI-type entity) onto an aircraft without having the corporate purpose and applicable tax regime checked by a professional specialized in aviation law. A poorly adapted structure can be recharacterized and lead to costly legal and tax consequences.

Finally, an owning company does not remove the obligations attached to the aircraft itself: a valid certificate of airworthiness (CofA), compliance with airworthiness directives, and an insurance policy suited to the aircraft's actual use. The legal entity changes who holds title, not the safety and airworthiness rules that apply to the aircraft.

Registration, insurance and aircraft-specific formalities

When a company becomes the owner of an aircraft, it must generally appear as the titleholder on the registration documents filed with the relevant national authority, along with evidence of its legal existence (company registration extract, bylaws, and where applicable proof of financial capacity). The administrative file is therefore a bit denser than for a purchase made by an individual, but remains manageable with the help of a broker or specialized advisor.

On the insurance side, policies must be taken out in the name of the owning company, specifying the authorized pilots (shareholders, employees, professional pilots where applicable) and the aircraft's intended use. A company that leases the aircraft to third parties or authorizes several pilots must verify that its policy actually covers this real usage, or risk having a claim disputed by the insurer.

  • Check that the corporate purpose explicitly covers holding and operating an aircraft before any registration.
  • Keep the logbook and all maintenance records in the company's name, not under a specific shareholder.
  • Have the shareholders' agreement and the usage agreement reviewed by a lawyer before signing, especially exit and share buy-back clauses.
  • Agree in advance on how engine hours are split between shareholders if the aircraft is shared, to avoid disputes at resale.

How to choose the right structure for your project

The choice of structure depends above all on the number of shareholders, the aircraft's value, the intended use (strictly private, rental, professional) and the medium-term estate-planning goals (transfer, resale, integration into an existing family group). A solo purchase for personal use rarely justifies setting up a dedicated company. Conversely, a project with several shareholders, a larger budget, or a wish to prepare an estate transfer often makes a corporate structure worthwhile, provided it is correctly sized from the start.

In all cases, the most important step remains having the setup — type of company, drafting of bylaws, applicable tax regime — validated by professionals who understand both company law and the specifics of used aircraft, before signing the aircraft purchase agreement.

Key takeaway — A well-structured company simplifies governance and transfer for an aircraft held by several people; a poorly adapted structure (a recycled SCI-type entity) can instead create more legal and tax risk than a well-organized direct ownership.
