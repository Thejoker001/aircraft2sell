Which aircraft to get started in seaplane flying

Classic floatplane, amphibious microlight or dedicated seaplane: a comparison of the options for getting started in seaplane flying, based on your budget, licence and flying project.

Seaplane flying: a distinct branch of piloting

Flying from and to water fundamentally changes how you fly: reading water surface conditions, handling crosswind on water, specific landing and take-off techniques, and particular attention to the floats and their upkeep. Getting started in seaplane flying therefore means, beyond choosing an aircraft, planning additional training on top of the pilot licence you already hold.

The choice of first aircraft to get started depends heavily on available budget, the licence already held, and the type of flying planned: occasional discovery flights, regular club flying, or a medium-term personal ownership project.

Contrary to a common assumption, getting started in seaplane flying does not necessarily mean buying a seaplane in the strict sense: several families of aircraft give access to this type of flying, with very different budgets and regulatory requirements. Understanding these options before committing avoids a good number of disappointments, both financially and in terms of training.

The classic floatplane: the most common route

Many well-known light single-engine aircraft on the used market exist in a float-equipped version, which often makes this the most accessible entry route for anyone already holding a private pilot licence on aeroplanes. The aircraft stays familiar in its general handling; only the water take-off and landing phase requires dedicated training with a qualified instructor.

This option has the advantage of relying on an existing used-aircraft market, with parts and mechanics available for the engine and airframe, even though the floats themselves require specific maintenance follow-up, which should be budgeted for in operating costs.

From a resale perspective, these floatplanes also benefit from a more liquid market than dedicated seaplanes: the airframe remains sought after in both wheel and float configuration, which makes a future resale easier should seaplane flying ultimately not suit your expectations.

The amphibious microlight: lower cost, simpler training

For a tighter budget, the amphibious microlight is an appealing alternative. Its training and regulatory framework are generally lighter than for a certified aircraft, and both acquisition and operating costs stay clearly below those of a classic floatplane.

In exchange, versatility is more limited: reduced payload, shorter range and greater sensitivity to wind and water-surface conditions. It remains, however, an excellent entry point to discover seaplane flying without committing a large budget, and to assess whether this type of flying truly matches your expectations before investing further.

Choosing an amphibious microlight also often lets you keep the option of landing on both land and water with the same aircraft, making it a particularly flexible solution for mixed flying between conventional airfields and bodies of water.

The dedicated seaplane: comfort and versatility, higher budget

At the other end of the spectrum, seaplanes designed from the outset for water — a central hull rather than bolted-on floats — often offer more reassuring behaviour on water, greater payload and more versatility for varied missions. These aircraft, rarer on the European market, come with a noticeably higher acquisition and maintenance budget.

This choice suits a pilot already convinced by seaplane flying, who wants to invest in a versatile aircraft for the long term rather than starting their first introduction to the activity.

Comparison: budget, licence and rating

Beyond the aircraft itself, the licence already held and the rating still to be obtained strongly shape the choice of first aircraft for getting started in seaplane flying.

In every case, obtaining a rating specific to seaplane flying, through a recognised instructor or school, remains an essential prerequisite before flying solo, whichever aircraft is chosen.

Which aircraft for which flying project

  • Occasional discovery flying on a tight budget: the amphibious microlight offers the most accessible entry point.
  • Already hold a private pilot licence and plan regular flying: the classic floatplane remains the most common choice.
  • Planning intensive flying, varied missions or touring: the dedicated seaplane becomes relevant despite its higher budget.
  • In every case, plan for dedicated supervised training before flying solo.

It is also worth distinguishing leisure flying, centred on enjoyment and exploring new bodies of water, from more structured projects such as light transport to remote areas or extended flights along coastlines and lakes, which point more towards a versatile aircraft and more extensive training.

Access to a suitable body of water nearby, as well as access to a school or club that practises seaplane flying, also weighs heavily on the final choice, sometimes more than the aircraft itself.

Buying used to get started in seaplane flying

Whatever aircraft you're considering, buying a used aircraft equipped for water demands a particularly thorough pre-purchase inspection: condition and watertightness of the floats or hull, corrosion, engine hours and SMOH, logbook consistency and a valid certificate of airworthiness (CofA). Metal floats, in particular, require careful checking for both internal and external corrosion.

Good to know — Always have the pre-purchase inspection carried out by a mechanic specialised in water-equipped aircraft, and ask for the complete maintenance history of the floats or hull, not just that of the airframe and engine.

Comparing several listings of similar aircraft, taking into account their real maintenance condition and water-use history, remains the best way to start your seaplane project on solid ground.
