Piper Comanche vs Beechcraft Bonanza: which one for family travel

Two family-travel classics, two different approaches

The Piper Comanche and the Beechcraft Bonanza are both all-metal piston singles designed around the same era, the late 1950s, to answer the same need: give a family or a professional a fast, comfortable travelling aircraft capable of covering medium to long distances without a stop. They share a classic retractable-gear layout and a fairly similar buyer profile, which makes them natural rivals on the used market whenever the goal is family travel.

Their design philosophies still differ in meaningful ways. The Comanche was designed by Piper as an efficient, aerodynamically clean traveller for its category, while the Bonanza built its reputation for toughness and longevity over an uninterrupted production run spanning several decades, with a far larger worldwide fleet.

Cabin, comfort and payload

For family use, cabin space and payload are often the deciding factors. The Comanche offers a comfortable four-seat cabin in its most common versions, with some six-seat variants adding real extra margin for luggage or additional passengers. The Bonanza, produced across several distinct families over its long career, also comes in widened-cabin or higher-payload versions that can be a clear advantage for trips requiring more luggage capacity.

  • Comanche: comfortable four-seat cabin, with six-seat versions available on some model years for extra payload.
  • Bonanza: several cabin families depending on generation, with some versions offering higher payload than the base model.
  • Both aircraft are available with a more powerful engine on certain versions, which improves the useful-load margin for family trips.
  • The actual cabin equipment level depends heavily on the model year and on upgrades made by successive owners.

Cruise performance and range

Both aircraft deliver similar cruise performance, well above that of a basic trainer, which makes them sound choices for covering long distances with a family on board without multiple stops. The Comanche, known for its clean aerodynamics, often performs well in cruise speed relative to its engine power, while the Bonanza, on its more powerful versions, offers comparable performance with a proven track record on long trips.

In practice, the real performance gap between a well-maintained Comanche and a Bonanza of comparable generation is often marginal on a family trip of several hundred kilometres — total flight time depends far more on the chosen cruise altitude and wind conditions than on a few knots of theoretical difference between the two types.

Budget comparison: purchase and operating costs

Purchase budget depends above all on the aircraft's age, the remaining engine potential and the avionics fitted, far more than on the model itself. The Bonanza, built over a much longer period and in larger numbers, generally offers a wider range of model years with more accessible entry points. The Comanche, built over a shorter period, offers a smaller used market, which can affect price depending on how rare the specific version sought after is.

Beyond the sticker price, both aircraft involve comparable extra costs: insurance, parking, the mandatory annual inspection and, in the longer run, an engine overhaul once the time potential is reached. The retractable gear shared by both types adds a specific maintenance line item to plan for in the annual budget.

Maintenance and parts availability

The Bonanza has a clear edge in terms of parts availability and specialised workshop network, thanks to an uninterrupted production run spanning several decades and a very large worldwide fleet. This generally makes maintenance planning easier and limits downtime when a part needs replacing.

The Comanche, built over a shorter period before its production line closed, can require more patience for certain specific parts, particularly on the earliest versions. Its owner and specialist network nonetheless remains active and well organised, which significantly eases this constraint for those who know where to look.

Good to know — Before buying, identify an approved workshop familiar with the exact type you are after: the quality of ongoing maintenance often depends more on the local network available than on the model itself.

Buying either one used: points to watch

For both models, the retractable landing gear calls for a thorough check of its operation and maintenance history during the pre-purchase inspection: extension and retraction cycles, condition of the actuators and associated mechanism, and the absence of any undocumented prior gear incident. For the Bonanza, also check that airworthiness directives specific to the version concerned have been complied with, particularly on the oldest examples. For the Comanche, the overall condition of the structure and a complete, traceable logbook deserve particular attention, given the generally higher average age of the fleet still flying.

Tip — Always have the pre-purchase inspection carried out by an independent mechanic qualified on the exact type you are considering, Comanche or Bonanza, and request the complete flight logbook and maintenance invoices before committing.

Comparing several listings of similar models and model years remains the best way to judge a fair price, whether you are after a well-maintained Comanche or a Bonanza of a generation that fits your family budget.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Piper Comanche big enough for a family of four?

Yes, most four-seat Comanches offer a comfortable cabin for a family of four with light luggage, and the six-seat versions available on some model years add a real margin in payload.

Is the Bonanza more expensive to maintain than the Comanche?

The Bonanza benefits from a wider parts and workshop network thanks to its much longer production run, which can help contain some costs. The Comanche, built over a shorter period, sometimes requires more patience for certain specific parts, particularly on the older retractable-gear versions.

Which aircraft should I choose for regular family trips in Europe?

Both aircraft are well suited to family trips of several hundred kilometres. The choice mainly comes down to your available budget, the availability of parts and an approved workshop near your base, and your personal preferences regarding cabin and equipment.
