At this budget, the used market opens the door to turboprops and the first entry-level business jets: a comparison to help you choose based on your mission profile and operating budget.

A transition budget into the turboprop and jet world

Under 500,000 euros, the used aircraft market changes nature compared to a tighter budget focused on classic piston single-engines. At this price point, depending on the year of manufacture, remaining engine hours and equipment, it becomes possible to access older single-engine or twin turboprops, and occasionally even a handful of the oldest and most heavily used entry-level business jets on the market.

This budget often marks a transition step for an owner moving from a piston aircraft to turbine power, with the reliability and performance benefits that implies, but also noticeably higher operating costs that need to be anticipated before signing.

Used single-engine turboprops within this budget

The oldest single-engine turboprops, with high total engine hours or approaching their major overhaul, can occasionally be negotiated below this budget threshold depending on the model year and overall condition of the aircraft. Aircraft like the earliest generations of the Socata TBM 700 illustrate this segment: fast, pressurised and capable of operating over long distances, they approach the performance of a small jet while, on the oldest and most heavily used examples, sometimes falling into a price range comparable to that of a high-end piston twin.

Remaining engine potential before the major overhaul (SMOH) is the central negotiating point here: an aircraft close to that milestone should see its price adjusted accordingly, since a turbine overhaul represents a substantial investment.

Entry-level twin turboprops and King Airs

On the twin-turbine side, the earliest generations of the Beechcraft King Air, notably the oldest C90 models and derivatives, can occasionally be found at or near this budget when they carry significant engine hours. The King Air remains a benchmark for ruggedness, versatility and the availability of its maintenance network, making it a reassuring choice for a first step into twin-turbine ownership, despite higher operating costs than a single-engine aircraft.

Other older twin turboprops, such as certain Piper Cheyenne models, can also appear in this price range, with particular attention needed on the history of each engine and the consistency of maintenance between the two powerplants.

Used business jets: where the €500,000 threshold really sits

A budget of 500,000 euros generally sits at, or slightly below, the bottom end of the used business jet market. The oldest examples of entry-level light jets, when they have accumulated a large number of flight hours and are approaching major maintenance milestones, can occasionally appear at this price level, but these configurations remain a minority and require careful analysis of the maintenance records.

In most cases, a buyer targeting this budget for a jet will need to accept an older aircraft, with reduced remaining engine potential and approaching major maintenance events, which means budgeting a significant refurbishment reserve beyond the purchase price. At this budget level, it is often more realistic to focus on turboprops rather than a jet whose catch-up maintenance cost would quickly exceed the price paid.

Word of caution — A jet listed "under €500,000" should always prompt a close look at remaining engine potential: request the time-since-overhaul figures for each engine before any visit.

High-end piston twins: a relevant alternative

For a buyer who prioritises versatility and more moderate operating costs than a turbine, high-end piston twins like the Beechcraft Baron or well-equipped versions of the Beechcraft Duke remain a credible alternative within this budget. They do not offer the performance or reliability of a turbine, but provide a spacious cabin, good payload and engine redundancy, for a maintenance cost per flight hour well below that of a turboprop.

This choice is particularly well suited to regular personal or family use over medium-distance trips, without the constraint of a specialised turbine maintenance network.

Operating costs: the real line item to plan for

At this budget level, the purchase price is often just the visible part of the investment. The operating costs of a turboprop or jet — fuel, insurance, hangarage, scheduled maintenance and the reserve for the major overhaul — represent a far larger annual financial commitment than for a piston single-engine, and must be factored into the calculation before any purchase.

  • Always set aside a reserve for the upcoming engine overhaul, based on remaining hours to the milestone.
  • Compare insurance premiums, which are notably higher for a turboprop or jet than for a piston aircraft.
  • Check the availability of an approved maintenance network for the exact aircraft type under consideration.
  • Evaluate hangarage and parking costs, often higher for an aircraft in this category.

Inspection points to watch for these categories

The pre-purchase inspection of a used turboprop or jet should be entrusted to an approved mechanic specialised in the exact type of the aircraft, not to a generalist used to piston aircraft. Particular attention should be paid to the complete logbook, the consistency of declared engine hours, the status of the certificate of airworthiness (CofA), and the history of airworthiness directives applicable to the turbine.

Tip — Always request an engine trend monitoring report when one exists, in addition to the standard logbook: it can detect a performance drift before it turns into a costly failure.

Comparing several listings of similar models, with comparable engine hours and years, remains the best way to assess whether a price listed under 500,000 euros reflects a genuine opportunity or an aircraft whose refurbishment cost would quickly erase the apparent saving on purchase.

Financing this type of aircraft also deserves close attention: specialised aviation finance providers tend to weigh the consistency between price, age and remaining engine potential more heavily than the headline purchase value alone, which can limit the deposit or the rate offered on an aircraft judged close to a major overhaul milestone. At resale, a complete maintenance record and a recent overhaul are often the single most decisive selling argument, even more so than for a simple piston single-engine.
