Used Piper Warrior: buying guide (PA-28-161)

Budget to plan for, inspection points to watch, routine maintenance and resale outlook for a used Piper Warrior PA-28-161, one of the most common training single-engine aircraft in Europe.

Why the Warrior remains a training classic

The Piper Warrior, designated PA-28-161 by the manufacturer, is a direct evolution of the Piper Cherokee with an improved wing profile that gives better low-speed handling. A four-seat, fixed-gear single-engine aircraft, it was produced in very large numbers and has for decades been one of the go-to mounts for flight schools and aero clubs across Europe and the United States.

Its mechanical robustness, simple airframe and wide parts availability make it a particularly accessible used aircraft for a first purchase, whether bought individually, in a co-ownership arrangement, or for the activity of a flying club.

Warrior, Cherokee and PA-28: telling the versions apart

The PA-28 family covers many variants that it's important not to confuse when searching listings. The Warrior differs from the original Cherokee by its semi-laminar wing profile, which improves efficiency and stall behaviour. Other family members, such as the Archer or the Arrow, carry a more powerful engine or retractable gear, and should not be confused with the Warrior when comparing prices.

ModelDistinguishing feature
Original CherokeeClassic wing profile, entry-level engine
Warrior (PA-28-161)Semi-laminar wing, better stall behaviour, 160 hp engine
Archer / ArrowMore powerful engine, and retractable gear on the Arrow

Before comparing several listings, it's therefore essential to check the exact model designation on the aircraft's documents, since a Warrior and an Archer do not share the same operating budget or resale value.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used Warrior depends above all on the age of the aircraft, accumulated airframe and engine hours, engine status (remaining potential before the major overhaul) and the level of avionics installed. Older examples with analog avionics trade at a very accessible entry level, which explains the model's popularity among clubs and flight schools working with tight budgets.

Beyond the purchase price, your budget should include ancillary costs: a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic, registration and transfer fees, insurance, and possibly an avionics or cabin interior refresh.

What to check before buying

As with any used aircraft, a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic qualified on the type is essential before finalising a transaction. For a Warrior, a few points deserve particular attention, especially if it has been used intensively by a flight school.

  • Corrosion and general airframe wear, especially on aircraft that have accumulated large amounts of instruction hours with many landings.
  • Condition of the fixed landing gear and tires, which are often under greater stress on a flight-school aircraft.
  • Complete engine history: hours elapsed since the last major overhaul (SMOH), compliance with the manufacturer's maintenance program, traceability of replaced parts.
  • Condition and updates of the avionics, as well as compliance with current regulatory requirements (transponder, traffic-monitoring systems).
  • Complete logbook and technical file, with no grey areas in the aircraft's history, and a valid certificate of airworthiness (CofA).
Good to know — Never rely solely on the seller's statements about overall condition: a written independent inspection report remains the best protection against a bad surprise after the purchase.

Engine, hours and major overhaul

The Warrior is fitted with a 160 hp flat engine known for its reliability and simple maintenance. As with any piston engine, SMOH — the hours elapsed since the last major overhaul — should be compared against the total potential published by the engine manufacturer to estimate the hours remaining before the next major intervention.

An engine close to its potential limit means budgeting for a future major overhaul. Conversely, a recently overhauled engine with low SMOH and good traceability of the work performed is a meaningful value argument both for the buyer and for a future resale.

Maintenance and operating costs

The operating cost of a Warrior remains among the most accessible in light aviation, thanks to its mechanical simplicity, fixed gear and wide parts availability. The main expense items remain the mandatory annual inspection, insurance, parking and, in the longer term, the engine's major overhaul once its hourly potential is reached.

Having the aircraft maintained at an approved shop familiar with the type helps limit unpleasant surprises and preserve its resale value. Rigorous, well-documented maintenance records in the logbook are a strong argument at a future resale.

Tip — Always ask for the last three maintenance invoices before making an offer: they give an accurate picture of how seriously the aircraft has been maintained.

Typical use: training and four-seat travel

The Warrior was first and foremost designed for initial flight training and regular club flying, thanks to its stability, straightforward handling and controlled operating cost. It also suits light travel for two to four people over short to medium distances, within its useful-load limits.

Owner-pilots looking for an aircraft that is simple to maintain, economical to operate and whose resale value stays stable over time find in the Warrior a particularly reassuring option for a first purchase.

Resale and the used market

The Warrior benefits from a very active used market, supported by a huge installed fleet and excellent parts availability. A well-maintained aircraft, with a transparent logbook and up-to-date avionics, generally resells more easily and on better terms than one whose technical file has grey areas.

Comparing several listings of similar models remains the best way to assess a fair price, both for buying and for preparing a future resale. Price differences between two aircraft of the same model year are almost always explained by remaining engine potential (SMOH) and prior use history, intensive training versus moderate private use.
