Why the Learjet 60 appeals to business jet operators

The Learjet 60 is a mid-size business jet known for its high cruise speed and a cabin more spacious than the earliest Learjet generations. It suits companies and operators looking for an aircraft able to quickly connect European destinations or short transatlantic routes, with a good balance between performance and operating costs compared to larger jets.

On the used market, the Learjet 60 comes in several successive variants, with avionics and cabin updates depending on the production year. As with any used business jet, this diversity means you need to clearly define your mission requirements and budget before comparing available listings.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used Learjet 60 depends above all on the age of the aircraft, total airframe and engine hours, engine status (remaining potential before overhaul), and the level of avionics and cabin equipment. The oldest examples, with reduced engine potential, trade at a noticeably more accessible entry level than well-equipped, more recent versions.

Beyond the purchase price, your budget should include ancillary costs specific to a business jet: a pre-purchase inspection at an approved shop qualified on the type, registration and transfer fees, insurance, hiring and training a qualified crew, and possibly an avionics or cabin refresh.

What to check before buying

As with any used aircraft, a pre-purchase inspection by an approved shop qualified on the Learjet type is essential before finalising a transaction. For a Learjet 60, a few points deserve particular attention.

  • Complete history of both engines: hours elapsed since the last overhaul, compliance with the manufacturer's maintenance program, traceability of replaced parts.
  • Condition of the structure and flight controls, with particular attention to airworthiness directives (AD) applicable to the type.
  • Condition and updates of the installed avionics, as well as compliance with current regulatory requirements for operation in Europe.
  • Condition of the cabin interior and associated equipment, an expense item often underestimated when an upgrade is needed.
  • Complete logbook and technical file, with no grey areas in the aircraft's history, and a current certificate of airworthiness (CofA).
Good to know — Never rely solely on the seller's statements about overall condition: a written independent inspection report carried out by an approved shop qualified on the type remains the best protection against a bad surprise after the purchase.

Maintenance program and engine overhaul

A business jet like the Learjet 60 is subject to a structured maintenance program, with scheduled inspections at regular intervals and a major engine overhaul to plan for once the hourly potential is reached. This expense must be anticipated right from the purchase, by asking the seller for the precise status of each engine and the expected date of the next major intervention.

Strict adherence to the logbook and traceability of work carried out by approved shops are decisive factors, both for operational safety and for the aircraft's future resale value.

It is also recommended to check the history of airworthiness directives (AD) applied to the aircraft, to make sure no mandatory modification or inspection has been overlooked.

Operating costs and crew

Operating a business jet like the Learjet 60 involves higher fixed and variable costs than a light aircraft: fuel, handling fees, hangarage, insurance, and above all the salary and recurrent training of a crew qualified on the type, generally made up of at least two pilots.

These costs should be factored in right from the project planning stage, comparing different operating models (direct ownership, fractional ownership, or using a management company) depending on the anticipated annual flight hours.

Tip — Always ask for the maintenance records of the last two years before making an offer: they give an accurate picture of how seriously the aircraft has been maintained and of upcoming costs.

Financing and insuring a business jet

Acquiring a used business jet like the Learjet 60 is rarely paid for entirely in cash: finance lease, leasing or fractional ownership are solutions commonly used by companies and high-net-worth individuals to spread the initial investment over time.

Insuring a business jet depends on many factors, including crew experience, the intended use of the aircraft (private or commercial) and the agreed value set for the aircraft. It is advisable to compare several offers before finalising the purchase, as this item represents a significant annual budget for this type of aircraft.

Resale and the used market

The Learjet 60 benefits from an active used market, supported by a large installed fleet among mid-size business jets. A well-maintained aircraft, with a transparent history, up-to-date avionics and a cabin in good condition, generally resells more easily and on better terms than one whose technical file has grey areas.

Comparing several listings of similar models remains the best way to assess a fair price, both for buying and for preparing a future resale. Price differences between two aircraft of the same model year are almost always explained by remaining engine potential and the quality of the avionics and cabin fit-out.

Buyers of mid-size business jets like the Learjet 60 are most often companies, specialised brokers or operators that already have a maintenance structure and a qualified crew in place. This reality of the market directly shapes how a listing should be presented and negotiated: a complete technical file, accessible maintenance records and full transparency about the aircraft's history generally speed up the sales process.

As with other aircraft categories, the value of a Learjet 60 moves with the cycles of the used business jet market. Tracking price trends over several months, comparing aircraft with similar engine status and equipment, remains the best approach for deciding when to buy or sell.
