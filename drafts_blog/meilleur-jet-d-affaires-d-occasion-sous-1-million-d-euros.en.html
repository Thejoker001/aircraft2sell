Buying a business jet under €1 million: is it realistic?

Under one million euros, buying a used business jet remains possible, but only in a very specific corner of the market: first-generation aircraft designed in the 1970s and 1980s, whose airframe has already logged a large number of flight hours and whose engines are often approaching their overhaul limit. This is a niche market, very different from the market for recent light jets, where the purchase price represents only a fraction of the real cost of ownership over several years.

This budget effectively rules out almost every jet still in production or modernised versions, but it opens the door to historic aircraft that are still perfectly capable of flying, provided you accept a demanding maintenance regime and operating costs that can quickly catch up with, or even exceed, the initial purchase price.

Cessna Citation I and Citation II: the most sought-after entry-level jets

The Cessna Citation I and its stretched sibling, the Citation II, are among the most accessible used business jets on the market. Designed to offer great operating simplicity, they long served as the entry point into business aviation for many private owners and small operators. That long-standing popularity translates today into a relatively abundant used supply, with older examples listed at prices that can fall below the million-euro mark.

These aircraft are valued for their reputation for ruggedness and for parts availability, backed by a worldwide fleet still in active service. In return, the most affordable examples are also the oldest: high engine hours, sometimes dated original avionics, and a cabin that frequently needs upgrading to meet current comfort and connectivity standards.

Learjet 20 and Learjet 30: speed at an attractive used price

The Learjet 20 and Learjet 30 series represent the other major family accessible within this budget. Designed from the outset for speed, these aircraft retain high cruise performance that remains attractive for fast business travel, even decades after their original design. Their image as a compact, high-performance jet continues to attract buyers seeking maximum speed on a contained acquisition budget.

That performance comes at an operating cost, however: higher fuel burn than more recent light jets, and avionics that, on the oldest examples on the market, often require an upgrade to remain operational in today's airspace. As with entry-level Citations, the examples accessible under €1 million are almost always very high-time aircraft, whose remaining engine potential must be examined with the greatest care.

Budget comparison: purchase price and real operating costs

In this segment, the listed price gives only a very partial picture of the real budget. The gap between an aircraft close to its major overhaul and one that has just come out of it can represent several hundred thousand euros of value difference, for a similar catalogue price.

Beyond the purchase price, the budget must include insurance (noticeably higher than for a light aircraft), hangarage, pilot training or currency, and a financial reserve for a possible major overhaul if the aircraft is approaching one.

The pitfalls of a very old or high-time jet

In this market segment, the main risk is not the purchase price but the bill that can follow in the months after acquisition. An aircraft listed at an attractive price may hide very limited remaining engine potential, whose restoration sometimes costs more than the price paid for the aircraft itself.

  • Engine hours and hours remaining before the next major overhaul (SMOH, meaning hours elapsed since the last major overhaul).
  • Real condition of the airframe: structural fatigue, corrosion, history of any previous incidents on an aircraft several decades old.
  • Compliance of the avionics with current navigation and surveillance requirements, sometimes a significant upgrade item on the oldest examples.
  • Real availability of technical support and parts for the specific type, to be verified with specialist workshops before any commitment.
Good to know — A low purchase price on a very old jet must always be weighed against the remaining engine potential and the real condition of the airframe: that, rather than the catalogue price, is usually where the real cost of the deal lies.

Logbook and certificate of airworthiness: the documents to demand

Before any commitment, the entire logbook should be reviewed, ideally by an independent mechanic qualified on the specific type. This document records the full history of interventions, parts replacements and any structural repairs, and is the only reliable source for assessing the real condition of such an old aircraft.

The valid certificate of airworthiness (CofA), as well as the consistency between the hours recorded in the logbook and those shown on the aircraft's own counters, should also be checked systematically. Any inconsistency or missing document is a warning sign that, at the very least, justifies a thorough pre-purchase inspection before any negotiation.

Which jet to choose based on your use and maintenance budget

The choice between an entry-level Citation and an older Learjet depends above all on the intended mission and, more importantly, on the financial capacity to absorb operating costs over time. For occasional use favouring comfort and simplicity, a Citation I or II is often cheaper to operate. For a profile that prioritises speed on regular trips, a Learjet 20 or 30 retains a clear performance advantage, at the cost of higher fuel burn and a more demanding maintenance regime.

In every case, this market segment requires thinking in terms of total cost of ownership rather than purchase price alone: it is often better to pay a little more for an aircraft with recent engine potential and a flawless technical file than to chase the lowest price listed in an ad.
