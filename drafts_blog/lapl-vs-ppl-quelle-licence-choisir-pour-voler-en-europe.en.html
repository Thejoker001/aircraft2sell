LAPL vs PPL: which licence should you choose to fly in Europe

LAPL or PPL: a complete comparison of the two EASA private pilot licences to help you choose the right one for flying in Europe.

Two EASA licences, two ways of flying

Since private pilot licences were harmonised across Europe under the Part-FCL regulation, two main credentials coexist for flying light aircraft privately: the LAPL (Light Aircraft Pilot Licence) and the PPL (Private Pilot Licence). Both are issued by the civil aviation authorities of EASA member states and recognised throughout the European aviation area, but they don't serve exactly the same purpose. The LAPL was designed as a simplified route into aviation, aimed at local, recreational flying, while the PPL remains the reference licence for pilots planning more ambitious flying, including outside Europe.

The choice between the two mostly comes down to your flying project: flying regularly around your home airfield on a tighter training budget, or holding a licence with broad international recognition that opens the door to further ratings (night flying, IR, multi-engine, or even a later professional career).

The LAPL: a simplified entry-level licence

The LAPL was introduced to lower the barrier to entry into recreational light aviation. Its theoretical and practical training is lighter than the PPL's, which usually translates into a lower total cost and a shorter training time. It comes in several variants depending on the type of aircraft: LAPL(A) for aeroplanes, LAPL(H) for helicopters, LAPL(S) for gliders and LAPL(B) for balloons.

In exchange for this easier access, the LAPL carries structural restrictions: it only allows flying light aircraft below a limited certified maximum mass, with a restricted number of passengers, and it is not a standard stepping stone towards a professional licence (CPL or ATPL) without additional steps. It was designed to keep pilots within recreational aviation, not as a bridge out of it.

The PPL: the classic licence with wider privileges

The PPL is the historic private pilot licence, recognised beyond the EASA area thanks to its alignment with ICAO standards. Its training is more thorough than the LAPL's: more minimum flight hours, a broader theoretical syllabus, and a practical exam covering a wider range of flying scenarios.

This extra rigour has a positive counterpart: the PPL opens access to almost every additional rating that exists (night rating, instrument rating IR, multi-engine, towing, aerobatics, seaplane, and more), and it remains the foundation on which many pathways towards a professional licence are built, even though that licence then requires hours and modules far beyond the base PPL.

Training, cost and duration: a practical comparison

In practice, the most visible difference between the two licences lies in the minimum flight hours required under EASA (Part-FCL) regulations and in the scope of the theoretical syllabus to be completed. The LAPL requires a minimum number of flight hours generally lower than the PPL's, which usually means shorter and less costly training, all else being equal on the type of aircraft and the pace of training chosen.

Beyond the regulatory minimums, the real cost mostly depends on factors specific to each student pilot: the number of hours actually needed to reach the required standard, the hourly rate charged by the school and the aircraft used, and how regularly training sessions are flown. For an accurate budget, always request a detailed quote from several clubs or schools rather than relying on a general average.

What each licence allows (and restricts)

The LAPL(A) allows flying light aeroplanes within defined mass and seating limits, generally for non-commercial use, with fewer passengers than under the PPL. It still permits, like the PPL, day VFR flight on most of the aircraft it covers, and can be extended with a handful of LAPL-specific additional ratings.

  • LAPL(A) — flying light aeroplanes within their category, recreational use, mainly within the European framework.
  • PPL(A) — flying a wider range of single-engine aeroplanes and, with an additional rating, multi-engine types; access to night, IR, towing, aerobatics ratings and more.
  • Both licences remain "private" licences: neither allows paid passenger transport or commercial activity without an additional professional licence.
  • The PPL makes mutual recognition easier if you plan to fly or train outside the EASA area.
Good to know — Exact mass limits, seating limits and eligible ratings evolve with the regulation: always check the current Part-FCL texts and the conditions applied by your national authority before making your final choice.

Medical fitness and renewal

Both licences require a medical certificate, but of a different class: the LAPL is usually paired with an LAPL medical certificate, issued through a simplified procedure by an approved general practitioner in several countries, while the PPL requires a Class 2 medical certificate, issued by an approved aeromedical centre under more thorough criteria. In both cases, the certificate's validity is time-limited and renewal requires a new periodic medical examination, with a frequency that depends on the pilot's age.

This point is worth anticipating before committing to training: a pilot with certain medical history may find it easier to obtain an LAPL medical than a Class 2 certificate, or the reverse depending on the condition involved. Getting an initial medical opinion before starting training avoids unpleasant surprises partway through.

Which licence fits your flying project

For a pilot who mostly wants to fly around their home airfield, for pleasure, on a controlled training budget and without ambitions for advanced ratings, the LAPL is often an excellent entry point into light aviation. It gets most recreational pilots to their desired flying independence faster.

Conversely, a future aircraft buyer planning longer trips, additional ratings (night flying, IR), the purchase of a multi-engine aircraft, or who hasn't ruled out moving towards a professional activity, is better off aiming directly for the PPL: this avoids having to redo part of the training later to gain the corresponding privileges.

Moving from LAPL to PPL: a realistic path

A pilot holding an LAPL can, in most cases, have part of their flying experience recognised to progress towards the PPL, completing the missing hours and modules rather than starting training entirely from scratch. This bridge reassures many hesitant pilots: starting with an LAPL isn't a dead end if your flying ambitions grow over time.

In all cases, the best approach remains to clearly explain your project — intended flying frequency, target aircraft type, travel horizon — to several flight schools before committing, so you choose the licence that genuinely fits your needs rather than the most common default.
