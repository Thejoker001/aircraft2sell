TBM vs Pilatus PC-12: which single-engine turboprop to choose

Two benchmarks of the single-engine turboprop

The TBM (Socata, now Daher) and the Pilatus PC-12 have dominated the high-end single-engine turboprop segment for decades. Both aircraft share a Pratt & Whitney Canada PT6 engine, modern avionics and a reputation for reliability that makes them safe bets on the used market. Yet their design philosophies differ sharply: the TBM was built as a fast, agile aircraft, close to a light business jet in performance, while the PC-12 favours cabin volume, mission flexibility and the ability to operate from short or unprepared strips.

Choosing between the two first means defining your mission profile: usual number of passengers, type of airfields used, how much weight you give to cruise speed versus comfort and available space, and the operating budget you can accept over time.

The TBM: speed and cruise performance

The TBM has built a reputation as the fastest aircraft in its category, with a cruise speed that places it among the best single-engine turboprops on the market, sometimes close to certain light business jets on medium-length routes. Its airframe, slimmer and more streamlined than the PC-12's, saves meaningful travel time on long distances while keeping fuel consumption reasonable for its class.

Several generations have succeeded one another over the decades, with notable developments in avionics (integrated Garmin cockpits on recent versions), engine power and cabin pressurisation. On the used market, this variety of model years makes it possible to find aircraft at very different price levels depending on age and remaining engine potential.

The Pilatus PC-12: cabin volume and versatility

The Pilatus PC-12 was designed from the outset as a versatile aircraft, able to carry a large volume of passengers and baggage, to be easily reconfigured (club seating, partial cargo layout) and to operate from short or unprepared runways thanks to its reinforced gear. This versatility has made it a popular working tool for both passenger transport and specific missions (medevac, light cargo, government operations).

Its cabin, noticeably wider and taller than the TBM's, lets passengers stand up and offers comfort close to that of a light business jet, at the cost of a cruise speed lower than the TBM's. The PC-12 also benefits from a large network of approved maintenance centres in Europe, which makes technical support easier to arrange.

Budget comparison: purchase and operation

For comparable equipment and year, both aircraft sit in fairly similar price ranges on the used market, with exact positioning depending heavily on model year, installed avionics and remaining engine potential before overhaul. The gap widens mostly on operating costs: the PC-12, heavier and with a larger airframe, generally shows a slightly higher fuel burn per hour than the TBM, while still remaining an economical aircraft given its payload capacity.

Which aircraft for which use

The choice between the TBM and the PC-12 should first be based on the aircraft's intended real use. For an owner-pilot or a small operator who favours fast travel on business or family trips with a limited number of passengers, the TBM offers an excellent balance between performance and operating cost.

  • TBM: ideal for fast trips, performance-focused owner-pilots, medium to long routes with few passengers.
  • PC-12: relevant for carrying several passengers, operating from short or unprepared strips, light cargo or specific missions.
  • Need for cabin comfort and room to move around in flight: a strong point in favour of the PC-12.
  • Operating budget available over time: a criterion often more decisive than the initial purchase price.

Conversely, an operator that regularly needs to carry six to eight passengers, operate from short or poorly prepared airfields, or reconfigure its cabin by mission, will find in the PC-12 a versatility argument that justifies a more modest cruise speed.

Buying either one used: points to watch

Whether it's a TBM or a PC-12, the number one point to watch remains the complete history of the PT6 engine: hours elapsed since the last overhaul, strict compliance with the manufacturer's maintenance program, and traceability of interventions. An engine overhaul on this type of turboprop represents a significant expense, which must be factored into the real price of any aircraft advertised with a low acquisition cost.

Good to know — Always have the pre-purchase inspection carried out by a mechanic or an approved centre familiar with the type, and ask for the complete logbook, applied service bulletins and maintenance invoices before committing.

Also check the installed avionics (Garmin upgrade or equivalent), compliance with current regulatory requirements in your operating area, and the consistency between advertised airframe hours and engine hours. Comparing several listings of similar model years remains the best way to assess a fair price, whether for a TBM or a PC-12.

Frequently asked questions

Does the TBM or the PC-12 have the bigger cabin?

The Pilatus PC-12 offers a noticeably wider and taller cabin than the TBM, with modularity that allows more passengers or cargo to be carried. The TBM favours a slimmer, faster airframe at the expense of cabin volume.

Which one costs more to buy and to run?

Both aircraft sit in similar price ranges depending on year and equipment, but the PC-12 generally shows slightly higher fuel costs per hour due to its larger airframe, while the TBM stands out for its superior cruise speed.

Is a specific type rating required to fly a TBM or a PC-12?

Yes, both aircraft require manufacturer-specific training and recurrent checks, and the PC-12 is also frequently operated under single-pilot commercial IFR rules depending on the applicable regulations.
