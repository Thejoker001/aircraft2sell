Aviation Medical Certificates: Classes and Renewal

Class 1, Class 2, LAPL: what each medical certificate covers, who can issue it, how the examination works and how often it needs renewing.

Why a medical certificate is mandatory

Across Europe, exercising the privileges attached to a pilot licence is conditional on holding a valid medical certificate. This principle applies equally to light aviation, touring aviation and commercial aviation, and serves a simple purpose: making sure a pilot does not have a medical condition that could prevent them from carrying out their duties safely, for themselves, their passengers and third parties on the ground.

In most European countries, the applicable framework is the EASA Part-MED regulation, which harmonises medical criteria across member states. Each national civil aviation authority nonetheless remains responsible for the practical application of these rules on its territory, through a network of approved Aeromedical Examiners.

Flying without a valid medical certificate, or with an undeclared medical condition likely to affect flight safety, is an infringement that can also affect insurance cover in the event of an incident, regardless of any administrative penalties.

The medical certificate classes

The medical certificate required depends directly on the pilot licence held and the type of operation intended, not only on the type of aircraft flown.

  • Class 1 medical certificate — required for holders of a commercial pilot licence (CPL) or an airline transport licence (ATPL) operating in commercial air transport. Medical requirements are the strictest of the three, with a thorough cardiovascular, ophthalmological and biological assessment.
  • Class 2 medical certificate — required for holders of a private pilot licence (PPL) operating in non-commercial aviation. The examination is less extensive than for Class 1, but still thorough.
  • LAPL medical certificate (Light Aircraft Pilot Licence) — matches the privileges of the LAPL licence, intended for leisure flying on a light aircraft, motor glider or microlight depending on national equivalences. It is the lightest of the three medical requirement levels.

In some countries, a PPL holder can choose to fly solely under LAPL privileges with an LAPL medical certificate, which is less demanding to obtain and renew, as long as they stay within the limitations of that licence (number of passengers, aircraft type, flight conditions).

Who issues the certificate: the Aeromedical Examiner

An aviation medical certificate cannot be issued by an ordinary general practitioner: it must be issued by an approved Aeromedical Examiner (AME), specifically authorised by the national civil aviation authority to carry out the examination required for each class. For an LAPL certificate, some countries allow a general practitioner who has completed additional specific training to issue the certificate, under conditions set by the national authority.

The list of approved Aeromedical Examiners is generally published by each country's civil aviation authority. It is advisable to check that the chosen practitioner is approved for the class of certificate sought before booking an appointment, as waiting times can be significant depending on the region.

What happens during the medical examination

The examination takes place at the Aeromedical Examiner's practice and generally includes a review of the candidate's medical history, a general clinical examination, a vision check (visual acuity, colour vision), a hearing check, an electrocardiogram (either routine or periodic depending on class and age) and a screening urine test. For Class 1, the assessment is more extensive and can include additional biological and cardiological tests, particularly at the first renewals after a certain age.

Candidates must declare their medical history, current treatments and any recent hospitalisations fully and honestly. A deliberate omission can lead to the cancellation of the certificate issued, or even consequences for the validity of the licence itself.

Good to know — Wearing corrective glasses or contact lenses does not prevent obtaining a medical certificate, as long as corrected visual acuity remains within the required limits; a restriction (corrective lenses must be worn while flying) is then simply noted on the certificate.

Validity period and renewal by age

How long a medical certificate remains valid depends on the class held and, for Class 2 and LAPL, on the pilot's age. As a general guide, under the framework harmonised by the Part-MED regulation, Class 1 is typically valid for 12 months, with more frequent renewal from a certain age or for certain commercial passenger-carrying operations. Class 2 follows a decreasing validity principle with age: longer before age 40, then reduced in successive steps past 40 and again past 50. The LAPL certificate follows a similar logic, with a longer validity before age 40 and a reduced one afterwards.

Because these exact thresholds can be subject to regulatory adjustment and may be interpreted slightly differently by national authorities, it is essential to check the exact period applicable to your situation directly with your approved Aeromedical Examiner or the relevant civil aviation authority, rather than relying on a remembered rule that may have changed.

  • The expiry date is always printed explicitly on the certificate issued: it is the only reliable reference.
  • Booking the renewal several weeks ahead of the deadline avoids any unintended interruption of flying privileges.
  • An expired certificate immediately suspends the privileges attached to the licence, even if the licence itself remains valid.

Restrictions, unfitness and appeals

At the end of the examination, the Aeromedical Examiner can issue the certificate without restriction, issue it with one or more restrictions (corrective lenses required, limitation to certain types of operation, requirement to carry a qualified co-pilot), or refuse it if medical unfitness is found.

If there is doubt about a medical condition, the file can be referred to the national aeromedical authority for a further opinion, or even for a specific flexibility provision when the candidate's situation justifies it and sufficient compensating measures can be put in place. An initial refusal is therefore not always final: an appeal or further specialist assessment can sometimes lead to a reassessment.

A pilot who develops a medical condition during the validity period that could affect their fitness to fly (medication, hospitalisation, surgery) must stop exercising their privileges and consult their Aeromedical Examiner before resuming flying, regardless of the expiry date printed on the certificate.

Special case of microlights and gliders

Medical requirements for flying microlights and gliders vary considerably from one European country to another, as these activities remain partly governed by national regulation rather than the harmonised Part-MED framework that applies to standard EASA licences. Depending on the country and aircraft type, the requirement can range from a standard LAPL medical certificate to a simple self-declaration of good health with recommendations, for certain categories of microlight or glider flying.

Before starting microlight or glider training, it is therefore essential to check precisely with the national authority or the affiliated club which medical requirement applies in that country, as this can differ noticeably from what is known for standard light aircraft.

Preparing well for your medical examination

To reduce the risk of a restriction or a delayed issue, it is advisable to attend the examination with full documentation of your medical history (discharge summaries, recent test results, list of current treatments), as well as your usual corrective lenses if applicable. Flagging any particular medical situation to the practice beforehand often allows the examiner to anticipate any additional tests that may be needed and avoid extending the time to issue the certificate.

Good to know — Booking an appointment well ahead of your current certificate's expiry date, especially for a Class 1 or if you have an ongoing medical condition, remains the best way to avoid any interruption of your flying privileges.

Ultimately, the aviation medical examination is not a simple administrative formality: it is a pillar of flight safety, on a par with aircraft maintenance or keeping flying skills current. Preparing it well and planning renewal ahead of time is part of responsible piloting practice.
