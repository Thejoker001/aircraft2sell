Used Cessna 170: buying guide

Budget, variants, tailwheel handling and the checklist you need before buying a used Cessna 170, the taildragger ancestor of the 172.

The Cessna 170 in brief

The Cessna 170 is the tailwheel single-engine that preceded the Cessna 172 in the manufacturer's lineup, produced mainly in the late 1940s and through the 1950s. With a largely metal airframe and some fabric-covered control surfaces on certain examples, it carries the rugged Cessna DNA while handling very differently from the tricycle-geared 172, owing to its main landing gear mounted behind the centre of gravity.

On the used market, the 170 attracts a different crowd from the 172: pilots looking for a vintage aircraft with real character, fans of rough-field and light bush flying, and owners who want to build tailwheel skills on a mechanically simple airframe that's well known to classic-aircraft shops.

With several decades of operation across Europe and the United States, the existing fleet remains large enough to find listings regularly, even though choice is naturally more limited than for the 172, which was produced over a much longer period and in far greater numbers.

Budget to plan for

The price of a used Cessna 170 depends above all on the variant (170, 170A or 170B), airframe condition, engine hours since the last overhaul, and the quality of any restoration work carried out. An original, unrestored example will sit at a noticeably more accessible entry price than one that has already been restored with a recently overhauled engine and updated avionics.

Beyond the purchase price, factor in the usual extra costs: a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic familiar with classic types, registration transfer, insurance (often higher for pilots with little tailwheel experience), and possibly fabric or paint refurbishment if the aircraft needs it.

The main variants: 170, 170A, 170B

The original 170 went through several production changes that have a direct effect on value and usability:

  • The original 170, the oldest, appeals to purists but comes with the most limited factory equipment.
  • The 170A brought structural and finish improvements that make day-to-day operation easier.
  • The 170B, with its larger flaps and reworked wing, is generally regarded as the most refined and most sought-after version.

Before buying, it's worth confirming the exact variant against the logbook and the manufacturer's data plate: some aircraft have undergone significant modifications over the decades (including non-factory conversions) that must be documented and properly approved.

Tailwheel handling: a trait to embrace

The Cessna 170's conventional gear calls for specific piloting technique, particularly on takeoff, landing and taxiing in a crosswind. A pilot coming from tricycle-gear aircraft should complete a tailwheel familiarisation course with a qualified instructor before flying the type solo.

This trait also affects maintenance: the tyres and tailwheel assembly wear differently, and particular attention should be paid to the condition of the tail reinforcement and main-gear alignment, two items a thorough pre-purchase inspection should always cover.

Checklist before buying

As with any used aircraft of this generation, a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic qualified on classic types is essential. For a Cessna 170, a few points deserve extra attention.

  • Airframe condition and corrosion check, particularly on the wing spars and fuselage skins most exposed to moisture.
  • Complete engine history: hours since major overhaul (SMOH), traceability of replaced parts, and compliance with the maintenance programme.
  • Condition of the main landing gear and tailwheel assembly, and any sign of poorly documented hard landings.
  • Quality of any structural repairs or modifications, with confirmation of proper regulatory approval in the logbook.
  • Up-to-date certificate of airworthiness (CofA) and consistency between the paperwork and the aircraft's actual condition.
Good to know — On an aircraft of this age, a well-documented history often matters more than cosmetic appearance: a complete, consistent logbook remains the best protection against an unpleasant surprise after purchase.

Upkeep and running costs

Running costs for a Cessna 170 are broadly comparable to those of a classic light single of similar power, with a few quirks tied to its age and structure. The main expense categories remain the mandatory annual inspection, insurance, hangarage or tie-down fees, and, further down the line, the major overhaul once the engine reaches its service limit.

Having the aircraft maintained by a shop experienced with classic types limits unpleasant surprises and helps protect resale value. A rigorous, well-documented maintenance record, including any fabric or paint refurbishment, is a strong selling point when the time comes to resell.

Tip — Always ask for the latest maintenance invoices and annual inspection reports before making an offer: they give an accurate picture of how well the aircraft has really been looked after.

Typical uses: light bush flying, hobby, restoration

The Cessna 170 is particularly valued for rough-field and light bush flying, thanks to its ruggedness and its ability to operate from short, lightly prepared strips. It also attracts many vintage-aviation enthusiasts drawn to its character and demanding handling, as well as restoration hobbyists who find in this model a mechanically simple base to bring back to life.

It's also a common choice for building tailwheel experience before stepping up to a higher-performance bush aircraft, or for pursuing a long-term recreational flying project on a manageable maintenance budget.

Some owners also choose the 170 for mixed use: weekend leisure flying combined with attending classic-aircraft fly-ins, where the model still enjoys strong standing among enthusiasts. This dual appeal partly explains why the best-restored examples hold their value well on the European market.

Alternatives to consider

Other vintage tailwheel singles occupy a similar market niche and are worth comparing before deciding, particularly depending on parts availability in your region, the local network of mechanics familiar with the type, and your intended use (hobby flying, light bush work or restoration). The final choice will also hinge on your overall budget once ongoing maintenance costs are factored in.

Tip — Compare several Cessna 170 listings of similar variant and condition before negotiating. The price gap between two seemingly similar aircraft almost always reflects real differences in remaining engine life or restoration quality.
