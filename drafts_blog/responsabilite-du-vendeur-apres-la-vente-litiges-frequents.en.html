Seller Liability After the Sale: Common Disputes

Hidden defects, undisclosed issues, disputes over the aircraft's real condition: what keeps a seller liable after the sale of a used aircraft, and how to protect against it.

The principle of seller liability

In a private sale of a used aircraft, the seller remains bound, after signing, by two essential obligations stemming from general sales law: an obligation to deliver an aircraft matching its description (the aircraft delivered must correspond to what was described in the listing and the bill of sale) and a warranty against hidden defects. This liability does not end on the day the keys are handed over: a defect discovered several months after the sale can still hold the seller liable if that defect existed before the sale and was not disclosed.

The exact regime varies according to the national law applicable to the contract (generally the law of the country where the sale is concluded, or one explicitly chosen in the bill of sale), but the general principle of a hidden-defect warranty is found in most European legal systems, many of which draw on continental civil law.

The hidden-defect warranty

A hidden defect is one that meets three cumulative conditions: it existed before the sale, it was not apparent during a normal examination of the aircraft (including a standard pre-purchase inspection), and it renders the aircraft unfit for its intended use or significantly reduces its value. Internal corrosion not detectable without disassembly, a concealed or undisclosed structural repair, or an incident history erased from the flight logbook are typical examples of hidden defects in light aviation.

  • Pre-existence of the defect — the defect must be proven to have existed before the sale, not appeared afterwards.
  • Hidden nature — a defect visible during a normal inspection cannot be invoked as a hidden defect.
  • Severity — the defect must significantly affect the aircraft's use or value.
Good to know — An "as is" sale clause never removes the hidden-defect warranty if the seller knew about the defect and concealed it: bad faith on the seller's part neutralises this type of clause in most European legal systems.

The most common disputes

The most common post-sale disputes between private individuals rarely concern a disagreement over price, but rather a divergence over the aircraft's actual condition at the time of the transaction.

Non-matching engine history

The most frequent dispute concerns the stated engine hours: an incomplete logbook, flight hours not recorded, or an engine logbook that does not exactly match the aircraft's flight logbook. The buyer often discovers the discrepancy at the first major overhaul following the purchase, sometimes months after the transaction.

Undisclosed structural damage

A hard landing, a propeller strike, or a damage repair not mentioned in the maintenance logbook is one of the most costly disputes to settle, since bringing the aircraft back into compliance can represent a significant share of its value.

Advertised equipment that is missing or non-functional

Avionics presented as installed and working in the listing but faulty or removed before delivery: this type of dispute, while financially smaller, remains one of the most frequent in the absence of a precise written condition report before the sale.

Point of caution — Most of these disputes could have been avoided with an independent pre-purchase inspection and a bill of sale precisely detailing the condition of the equipment at the time of the transaction.

The role of the bill of sale and limiting clauses

The bill of sale is the reference document in the event of a dispute: the more precisely it describes the aircraft's condition, its equipment and the engine hours at the date of sale, the better it protects both parties. A vague bill of sale that is limited to identifying the aircraft and the price leaves room for interpretation that favours later disputes.

Clauses limiting the warranty (an "as is" sale, exclusion of certain warranties) can reduce the seller's exposure, but their validity depends on the applicable law and they never protect against the deliberate concealment of a known defect. In all cases, they must be drafted explicitly and accepted in writing by the buyer to have any chance of being enforceable.

How the buyer protects themselves

  • Have a pre-purchase inspection carried out by a mechanic independent of the seller, never one recommended by them.
  • Require the full copy of the logbook and engine logbook before committing, not just a verbal summary.
  • Have the precise condition of the advertised optional equipment confirmed in writing, as an annex to the bill of sale.
  • Keep all written communications with the seller (listing, email exchanges): they serve as evidence in the event of a later dispute.

How the seller protects themselves

  • Disclose the aircraft's entire known history, including repaired minor incidents, rather than letting the buyer discover them later.
  • Have the aircraft's condition assessed by a professional before listing it, to have a dated report available in case of a future dispute.
  • Draft a detailed bill of sale, explicitly stating known facts and the warranty limitations accepted by the buyer.
  • Never alter or have a logbook corrected to mask an inconsistency: this greatly worsens liability in the event of a dispute.
Tip — Documented transparency remains the seller's best protection: a defect disclosed before the sale, even an unfavourable one, can no longer later be classified as a hidden defect.

Possible remedies in case of a dispute

In the event of a disagreement after the sale, the first step remains direct amicable negotiation, often the fastest and least costly route. Failing agreement, the buyer may bring a hidden-defect warranty claim before the court having jurisdiction as determined by the bill of sale or, failing that, by the applicable private international law rules. Mediation or arbitration, where provided for in the bill of sale, often resolves the dispute faster than a conventional court procedure.

In every case, a dated pre-purchase inspection report, a detailed bill of sale and written exchanges with the seller form the core evidence of any case, whether handled amicably or before a court.

Tip — Before starting proceedings, weigh the real cost of the dispute (legal fees, delays) against the amount at stake: for a minor defect, an amicable negotiation is often more cost-effective than legal action.
