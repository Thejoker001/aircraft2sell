Hydraulic Hoses: Lifespan and Replacement

Brakes, retractable landing gear, hydraulic flaps: understand the lifespan of hydraulic hoses, learn to spot wear, and plan for the cost of replacing them.

The role of hydraulic hoses and flexible lines

On a light aircraft, hydraulic hoses and flexible lines carry pressurised fluid to the systems that depend on it: brakes, retractable landing gear, hydraulic flaps, or variable-pitch propeller controls on some single- and twin-engine types. Unlike rigid metal lines, flexible hoses are designed to absorb movement, vibration and mechanical travel at junction points, particularly around the landing gear.

A hose typically consists of an inner core made of synthetic rubber or PTFE (Teflon), a reinforcing braid (textile or metal fibre), and an outer protective sleeve. This layered construction explains why hose degradation is not always visible from the outside before a leak appears.

Lifespan: what manufacturers say

There is no single, universal lifespan that applies to every hydraulic hose on every light aircraft: each aircraft or hydraulic system manufacturer sets its own replacement recommendations, expressed in calendar years, flight hours, or whichever limit is reached first. These intervals are found in the aircraft maintenance manual (AMM) and in approved maintenance programmes, and must be followed in the same way as any other scheduled maintenance task.

In practice, many brake and hydraulic hoses on light aircraft are replaced on a calendar basis, since UV exposure, temperature swings and rubber ageing are often more decisive for this type of component than flight hours alone. Rather than relying on a single figure that varies by model and equipment manufacturer, it is essential to refer to the aircraft's specific maintenance programme and to any applicable airworthiness directives (ADs) for the type.

Tip — Before any purchase, ask the seller for the date or hours of the last brake and landing gear hose replacement, as well as the part reference of the hoses installed (PMA-certified or original manufacturer parts).

Wear and degradation signs to watch for

An ageing hydraulic hose usually shows visible signs before an outright leak develops, provided you know where to look during a careful visual inspection.

  • Cracking and hardening of the outer sleeve — a hose that is supple to the touch and free of visible cracks is a good sign; a stiff or cracked sleeve should raise a flag.
  • Seepage or oil traces at fittings — even minor seepage at a crimped end fitting indicates a loss of seal integrity that can develop into a full leak.
  • Localised bulging — a sign of internal degradation of the hose core under pressure, requiring immediate replacement.
  • Abrasion or rubbing against the structure — a poorly secured hose, or one whose retaining clamp has loosened, can wear prematurely through friction.
  • Discoloration or a cracked appearance from UV exposure — common on aircraft stored outdoors for long periods without a hangar.
Point of caution — A hose that looks fine visually can still have reached the end of its calendar life with no outward sign. A visual inspection supplements the scheduled replacement limits; it does not replace them.

How to inspect hoses before buying

During a pre-purchase inspection, systematically ask the mechanic to gain visual access to all reachable hoses: the landing gear bay, the brake circuit at the wheels, and, where applicable, the hydraulic flap circuit. A ground test with the circuit under pressure can reveal leaks that are not visible on an unpressurised circuit.

  • Compare the replacement dates or hours recorded in the maintenance logbook with the visual condition observed.
  • Check for dried oil traces under the gear bay or near the brake calipers, a sign of a past leak that was not addressed.
  • Check that retaining clamps are in place and properly tightened, with no abnormal rubbing of the hose against the structure.

The cost of a thorough inspection of the hydraulic circuit remains marginal compared with the risk of a brake or landing gear failure in flight or while taxiing, which fully justifies making it a systematic part of any pre-purchase inspection.

Replacement: procedure and costs

Hydraulic hose replacement must be carried out by an approved workshop, using parts certified to the manufacturer's specifications (original parts or approved PMA parts). After replacement, a full bleed of the circuit and a pressure leak check are essential before the aircraft is returned to service.

Replacement cost varies significantly depending on how many hoses are involved, their accessibility and the type of aircraft: a single brake hose is quick and inexpensive to change, while a full replacement of the landing gear hydraulic circuit on a twin-engine aircraft is a much larger job, in both parts and labour. This item should be planned for in the routine maintenance budget, in the same way as other components with a limited lifespan (tyres, brakes, battery).

Tip — Ask your workshop for a detailed quote before any work, separating parts cost (which often varies by supplier) from labour cost, which depends heavily on hose accessibility on the model in question.

Pitfalls to avoid

When buying a used aircraft, the most common mistakes regarding hydraulic hoses are relying solely on outward appearance without checking replacement dates in the logbook, overlooking the history of an aircraft left outdoors for long periods (accelerated degradation from UV and freezing), or accepting untraceable replacement parts whose origin and certification cannot be confirmed.

Another pitfall is underestimating the impact of a poorly maintained hydraulic circuit on the aircraft's resale value: a savvy buyer will always request the full hose history before making an offer, and a seller who cannot provide it will generally have to accept a lower negotiated price.

Key takeaway — Always require documented traceability for installed hoses: part reference, installation date or hours, and a conformity certificate (EASA Form 1 or equivalent) from the workshop that carried out the replacement.
