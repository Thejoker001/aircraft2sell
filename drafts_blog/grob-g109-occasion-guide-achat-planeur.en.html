Why the Grob G109 appeals to owners and clubs

The Grob G109 is a two-seat touring motor glider (TMG) with an all-composite airframe, designed by the German manufacturer Grob Aircraft to combine the feel of soaring with the self-sufficiency of an onboard engine. Unlike a pure glider, it takes off and climbs entirely on its own, with no tow plane or winch required, making it a particularly versatile hybrid for an owner or a small club wanting to fly without depending on launch infrastructure.

This self-sufficiency makes it a popular platform for soaring training, regular ridge or local flying, as well as longer cross-country trips between airfields — something a pure glider cannot do. Its glide performance, well above that of a typical light aircraft, allows long power-off descents and fuel consumption well below that of a comparable touring aircraft.

On the European used market, the G109 has a sizeable installed base, particularly in Germany, France and the UK, with reasonable parts availability through workshops specialising in motor gliders and composite structures. This long production run means a fairly steady supply of listings, with prices varying significantly depending on age, airframe and engine hours, and overall condition.

The G109 also remains relatively straightforward to maintain for a workshop familiar with motor gliders, which helps keep fixed costs lower than for a more avionics- or systems-heavy powered light aircraft.

G109, G109A and G109B: what's the difference

The original G109 was produced in the 1980s and forms the entry level of the used market, with more accessible prices but often higher airframe hours on the oldest examples.

The later G109B brought engine and aerodynamic improvements over the original model, with better range and slightly improved glide performance. It is now the most sought-after version on the used market, generally commanding a higher price than the original G109.

Before targeting a specific version, it's worth comparing remaining engine hours before overhaul (TBO) and composite airframe condition rather than focusing purely on the model year, since two examples of the same generation can show a significant value gap depending on their usage and maintenance history.

What budget should you plan for

The price of a used Grob G109 mainly depends on the version (G109 or G109B), age, airframe and engine hours, as well as the condition of the composite skin and the fitted avionics. The oldest examples, with heavy club use, trade at an accessible entry level, while recent or well-maintained aircraft owned by a single private owner command a considerably higher price.

Beyond the purchase price, budget for the additional costs: a pre-purchase inspection by an independent, type-qualified mechanic, registration and transfer fees, insurance, plus hangarage or a parking spot depending on how you plan to operate the aircraft.

What to check before you buy

As with any used aircraft, a pre-purchase inspection by an independent, type-qualified mechanic remains essential before finalising a transaction. For a Grob G109, a few points deserve particular attention.

  • Composite skin condition: look for impacts, delamination or past repairs, particularly on the leading edges and fuselage.
  • Engine hours and overhaul history, with a complete logbook and no gaps.
  • Total airframe hours and the history of periodic airworthiness checks, with a valid Certificate of Airworthiness (CofA).
  • Condition of flight controls, cables and airbrakes, plus proper operation of the propeller and its pitch control system where fitted.
  • Past storage conditions (hangar or outdoors): an example regularly kept under cover keeps its composite skin in noticeably better shape over time.
Good to know — Never rely solely on the seller's description of the aircraft's condition: a written independent inspection report, ideally from a workshop approved for the type, remains your best protection against an unpleasant surprise after the purchase.

Licensing and operations: what to know

Operating a motor glider like the Grob G109 generally falls under a specific regulatory framework for touring motor gliders (TMG), distinct from that of a standard light aircraft and from that of a pure glider. The exact licensing and qualification requirements vary by the civil aviation authority of the country of registration and change over time: it is essential to check directly with the relevant authority or a qualified instructor before any purchase project, rather than relying on general information that could be outdated.

In every case, the aircraft's logbook should precisely document its past use (soaring, powered flight, training), a factor that directly affects resale value and how an independent inspector reads its mechanical condition.

Maintenance and running costs

A Grob G109's running costs sit between those of a pure glider and those of a typical powered light aircraft, thanks to reduced fuel consumption and a composite structure that's relatively straightforward to maintain for a workshop familiar with the type. The main cost items remain the periodic airworthiness check, engine maintenance, insurance and storage (preferably hangared).

Having the aircraft maintained by a workshop approved for the type and familiar with motor gliders limits unpleasant surprises and preserves resale value. Rigorous, well-documented maintenance of both engine and airframe is a strong selling point at any future resale.

Tip — Always ask for the complete logbook, engine history and the latest periodic inspection reports before making an offer: they give a faithful picture of how seriously the aircraft has been maintained.

Resale and the used market

The Grob G109 benefits from an active used market in Europe, backed by a sizeable installed base and reasonable parts availability through workshops specialising in motor gliders. A well-maintained aircraft, with a transparent history, skin in good condition and reasonable engine hours before overhaul, generally resells more easily and on better terms than one with gaps in its technical file.

Comparing several listings for similar examples remains the best way to judge a fair price, whether you're buying now or preparing a future resale. Price differences between two examples of the same version almost always come down to remaining engine hours before TBO, skin condition and documented maintenance quality.
