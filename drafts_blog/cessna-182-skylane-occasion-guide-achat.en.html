The Skylane in a nutshell

The Cessna 182 Skylane is a four-seat fixed-gear single, produced since the 1950s and still in production today. Designed as a more powerful, higher-payload sibling of the Cessna 172, it stands out for its load-carrying capacity, ruggedness and versatility: family travel, mountain flying, glider towing or skydiving operations on some dedicated fleets.

On the used market, the Skylane benefits from a very large installed fleet and excellent parts availability, making it a reassuring choice for a first four-seat aircraft purchase. Its reputation for ruggedness and its fixed gear, simpler to maintain than a retractable gear system, explain its enduring popularity among private owners, flying clubs and training schools alike.

Main generations and variants

The Skylane has gone through numerous revisions since launch: redesigned engine cowling and tail in the 1970s, a switch to fuel injection on some series, and from the early 2000s a relaunch of production with modern integrated avionics fitted as standard. The most recent examples stand out clearly from older versions through their digital cockpit and standard equipment.

Turbocharged variants (Turbo Skylane) also exist, offering better high-altitude performance but a more demanding engine to maintain and higher running costs. Before targeting a listing, it is worth identifying precisely the year, engine type (naturally aspirated or turbocharged) and avionics level of the aircraft in question, since these factors explain most of the price gap between two seemingly comparable Skylanes.

What budget should you plan for

The price of a used Skylane mainly depends on the year of manufacture, total airframe and engine hours, the remaining potential before major overhaul (SMOH) and the avionics fit. Older examples with reduced engine potential trade at a notably more accessible entry point than recent, well-equipped versions, which represent a considerably larger investment.

Beyond the purchase price, factor in the costs specific to a used aircraft: a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic qualified on the type, registration transfer fees, insurance, and possibly an avionics or interior upgrade if the aircraft needs one.

Good to know — Always compare Skylanes of the same generation and similar engine potential: a large price gap between two seemingly similar listings almost always comes down to remaining hours before overhaul or the condition of the avionics.

What to check before buying

As with any used aircraft, a pre-purchase inspection carried out by an independent mechanic qualified on the type is essential before closing a deal. On a Skylane, a few points deserve particular attention, especially given its frequent use at high payload and on rough strips.

  • Complete engine history: time since the last major overhaul, compliance with the manufacturer's programme, traceability of replaced parts.
  • Condition of the structure and landing gear, especially on examples used on unpaved strips or for glider towing, which may have experienced repeated stress.
  • Any corrosion on the airframe, particularly for aircraft parked for long periods in coastal or humid areas.
  • Condition and currency of the installed avionics, as well as compliance with current regulatory requirements (transponder, traffic surveillance).
  • Complete logbook and technical file, with no gaps in the history, especially if the aircraft was previously used intensively by a flying school or club.
Good to know — Never rely on the seller's verbal statements about overall condition: a written independent inspection report remains the best protection against an unpleasant surprise after purchase.

Typical uses: travel, mountain flying, payload

The Skylane stands out for its generous payload compared to aircraft of similar size, making it a popular choice for family travel with luggage, mountain flying thanks to its solid performance at weight, and specific niches such as glider towing or skydiving operations on dedicated fleets. This versatility explains part of its enduring popularity on the used market.

Before buying, it is worth checking that the aircraft's past use matches your own project: a Skylane that has only seen moderate private use will not have experienced the same stresses as an example flown intensively for towing or flight training.

Maintenance and running costs

The running cost of a Skylane remains reasonable for its category, with fixed gear that simplifies maintenance compared to a retractable-gear aircraft. The main cost items remain the mandatory annual inspection, insurance, parking and, eventually, the major engine overhaul once the time potential is reached. Turbocharged variants require closer engine monitoring and generally higher maintenance costs.

Having the aircraft maintained by an approved shop familiar with the type helps avoid unpleasant surprises and preserves resale value. Rigorous, well-documented maintenance records are a strong argument at resale time.

Tip — Always ask for the last three maintenance invoices before making an offer: they give a faithful picture of how seriously the aircraft has been looked after.

Resale and the used market

The Skylane benefits from a particularly active used market in Europe and elsewhere, backed by a very large installed fleet and excellent parts availability. A well-maintained aircraft, with a transparent history and up-to-date avionics, generally resells more easily and on better terms than one with gaps in its technical file.

Comparing several similar listings remains the best way to assess a fair price, both for buying and for preparing a future resale. Price differences between two aircraft of the same vintage almost always come down to remaining engine potential and the quality of the avionics fit.
