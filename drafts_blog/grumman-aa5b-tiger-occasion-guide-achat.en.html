The Grumman AA-5B Tiger: a sleek four-seater rarely seen in Europe

The Grumman AA-5B Tiger stands out on the ramp at first glance: a cantilever low wing with no external struts, an extensively riveted airframe, and a tight, sporty fuselage shape that breaks from the more conventional silhouettes of its contemporaries. Originally designed by American Aviation and later developed by Grumman American, the Tiger was built as the fastest and most powerful member of the AA-5 family, carrying a 180-horsepower engine that gives it cruise performance ahead of most light four-seaters of its generation.

On the European market, the Tiger remains a relatively uncommon type compared to the ubiquitous Cessna or Piper models, which makes it an appealing choice for owner-pilots after something fast, distinctive and often more affordable to buy than its performance would suggest. That relative rarity, however, can make finding parts and mechanics familiar with the type a bit more demanding than for a more common model.

Versions and production history worth knowing

The AA-5 family went through several iterations before the Tiger emerged: the original, more modestly powered AA-5 Traveler, followed by the intermediate AA-5A Cheetah, before the Tiger AA-5B launched in 1975 with the Lycoming O-360 180-horsepower engine that remains its defining feature. Production paused in the 1980s before American General Aircraft, and later Tiger Aircraft, relaunched a modernised version under the AG-5B and Tiger AG-5B names, with some avionics and cabin finish updates.

For a buyer in Europe, it is the original 1975-1979 AA-5B that turns up most often on the used market, while the later relaunched versions are considerably rarer and generally of North American origin.

What budget to plan for

The purchase budget for an AA-5B Tiger varies significantly depending on the year of manufacture, airframe and engine hours remaining before the next major overhaul, installed avionics, and the overall condition of the riveted airframe. A 1970s example with an engine close to its overhaul limit and original avionics will trade for noticeably less than a well-maintained aircraft fitted with upgraded avionics and a healthy amount of remaining engine life.

  • Total airframe hours and hours since the last major engine overhaul.
  • Engine hours since overhaul (SMOH) against the applicable Lycoming TBO for the O-360.
  • Avionics fit level: original instrumentation or a partial or full panel upgrade.
  • Condition of the paint and riveted airframe, which is sensitive to corrosion after long periods of outdoor storage.

Technical quirks worth checking

The Tiger has a few distinctive technical traits worth paying close attention to during a purchase. Its fixed landing gear is simple and robust, requiring less maintenance than a retractable system, but the gear legs and attachment fittings should be inspected for cracking or premature wear. The sliding canopy, one of the model's strongest visual signatures, should slide smoothly with no excessive play, and its seals must provide proper weatherproofing — otherwise water ingress can damage the instrument panel and avionics over time.

The fully riveted, all-metal airframe avoids the moisture-related issues typical of fabric-covered aircraft, but in exchange it demands close attention to internal corrosion, particularly on aircraft that have flown or been based in a coastal or humid environment. A check by a workshop approved on the type, including a probe of the known corrosion-prone areas where possible, remains the best safeguard before buying.

What to check before buying

Beyond the standard pre-purchase inspection covered in our pre-purchase inspection guide, a few points are specific to the Grumman AA-5B Tiger:

  • History of corrosion on the riveted structure, particularly under the paint and around the wing spars.
  • Operation and weatherproofing of the sliding canopy, plus the condition of its guide rails.
  • Compliance of any modifications and service bulletins applied, and the availability of type-specific parts from specialist suppliers.
  • Compatibility of the installed avionics with current requirements (Mode S transponder, 8.33 kHz radio).
Good to know — Before buying, ask for the complete airframe and engine logbook along with the latest inspection report. Have the inspection carried out by a mechanic familiar with the type — the Tiger's relative rarity in Europe makes finding that specific expertise harder than for a common model.

Maintenance and running costs

Like most light aircraft registered in Europe, the Tiger is maintained under a continuing airworthiness programme with a mandatory periodic inspection. The Lycoming O-360 engine benefits from a solid parts and mechanics network across Europe, even though some airframe-specific Grumman parts can require longer import lead times from North America, where the largest fleet of the type remains based.

For a 180-horsepower aircraft, the Tiger's fuel burn stays reasonable thanks to its clean aerodynamic shape, making it a relatively economical aircraft per flight hour for such a capable four-seater.

Who the Tiger suits best

The AA-5B Tiger suits particularly well an owner-pilot looking for a fast four-seater for personal or family travel, often at a more accessible purchase budget than a Cirrus or a Mooney with comparable performance, largely because of its lower profile on the European market. Its handling and cabin visibility, helped by the sliding canopy, also make it a popular choice for leisure flying and sightseeing.

Resale on the European market

The Tiger's relative rarity in Europe can cut both ways at resale time: a smaller pool of potential buyers than for a Cessna 172 or a Piper PA-28, but also a particular appeal among enthusiasts of the model who are willing to wait for a well-maintained example. A complete and transparent maintenance file remains, as with any used aircraft, the best argument for a quick resale at a fair price.
