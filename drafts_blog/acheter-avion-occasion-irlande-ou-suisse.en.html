Buying a Used Aircraft in Ireland or Switzerland: Which Country to Choose

Market, prices, registration, VAT/taxation and pitfalls to avoid: a complete comparison to help you decide where to buy your used light aircraft between Ireland and Switzerland.

Two very different market profiles

Ireland and Switzerland are two small European light aviation markets, but their dynamics have almost nothing in common. Ireland has an active general aviation community centred around a handful of well-organised airfields and clubs, with a used-aircraft supply focused on classic touring types (Cessna 172, Piper PA-28) and a growing number of microlights. The Atlantic climate, wetter and windier than on the continent, directly affects the number of flyable days and, as a result, sometimes the general condition of aircraft based outdoors.

Switzerland offers an even smaller market in volume, but one driven by very high purchasing power and a rigorous maintenance culture. The Alpine terrain limits the number of usable airfields, which concentrates supply around a handful of well-known platforms. A notable proportion of high-end aircraft (high-performance single-engines, turboprops, business jets) is found there, linked to the country's economic profile.

In both cases, EASA airworthiness standards apply, which makes it straightforward to compare the technical condition of an Irish aircraft with a Swiss one despite Switzerland's different status relative to the European Union.

Price comparison by aircraft type

Price ranges depend above all on engine history (hours remaining before TBO), airframe condition and installed avionics, far more than on the country of sale alone. There is, however, a tendency in Switzerland towards aircraft that are on average better equipped and newer, linked to local purchasing power, while Ireland often offers slightly more accessible entry-level prices on classic touring single-engines, with a smaller market that can lengthen the search for a specific aircraft.

Tip — Always compare at equivalent engine potential and avionics level: in markets as small as Ireland or Switzerland, the price gap between two aircraft of the same generation almost always comes down to these two factors rather than the country itself.

Registration: IAA versus FOCA

In Ireland, the competent authority is the Irish Aviation Authority (IAA), which assigns the EI- prefix. In Switzerland, the Federal Office of Civil Aviation (FOCA / OFAC) registers aircraft under the HB- prefix. In both countries, a purchase between private individuals where the aircraft is already registered locally simply requires updating the registered owner, while an aircraft imported from another country must first be deregistered from its register of origin before being newly registered.

  • Valid certificate of airworthiness and ARC — check this before signing, in both countries.
  • Bill of sale — dated, signed by both parties, stating the serial number, required by both the IAA and FOCA.
  • Liability insurance certificate — required before first use under the new registration, in both Ireland and Switzerland.
Good to know — Ireland, as an EU member, directly applies the common EASA rules for transfers between EU member states, which are generally simple and fast. Switzerland, although part of the EASA system through a bilateral agreement, is not part of the EU customs union: importing or exporting between the two countries remains possible but requires specifically checking the applicable customs formalities with FOCA.

VAT and taxation: Switzerland outside the EU

This is the most important point of difference between the two countries. Ireland, as an EU member, applies the common EU VAT regime: a sale between EU resident private individuals is generally VAT-exempt, while a sale by a professional or an import from outside the EU remains subject to import VAT.

Switzerland is not a member of the European Union or its VAT regime. An aircraft brought into Switzerland is in principle subject to Swiss VAT, calculated on the value of the aircraft, except in specific situations that need to be confirmed case by case. Conversely, a Swiss aircraft sold and exported to the EU (including Ireland) may require specific customs formalities on entry into EU territory. In both directions, it is essential to have your tax situation confirmed before acquisition, particularly if the aircraft has already changed country of registration several times.

Pre-purchase inspection and documents

Whether the aircraft is Irish or Swiss, a pre-purchase inspection by a certified mechanic independent of the seller remains the best protection against unpleasant surprises. It covers the condition of the airframe, the consistency of the engine logbook, maintenance history and the compliance of the avionics with current European requirements (transponder, 8.33 kHz radio) — requirements common to both countries thanks to the application of EASA standards.

  • Up-to-date airframe, engine and propeller logbooks, with no chronological gaps, whether kept in Ireland or Switzerland.
  • History of maintenance invoices from the last three years, from an approved workshop in the relevant country.
  • No undisclosed structural damage, verifiable via the maintenance file and a thorough visual inspection — a particular point of attention in Ireland, where exposure to the Atlantic climate can favour corrosion on a poorly protected aircraft.
Point of caution — Never finalise a purchase, in Ireland or in Switzerland, without having seen the original logbooks and having their consistency checked by a professional independent of the seller.

Which country to choose depending on your profile

The choice between Ireland and Switzerland depends above all on your project and budget. Ireland can suit a buyer looking for a classic touring single-engine at an entry-level budget, with the administrative advantage of a simplified registration transfer within the European Union. Switzerland is better suited to a buyer seeking a well-maintained, high-end aircraft, but requires carefully anticipating the customs and tax questions specific to a country outside the European Union, especially in the event of a cross-border import or export.

In both cases, the small size of each market often means widening the search across Europe to find an aircraft matching your exact criteria. The decisive factor remains the quality of maintenance and the transparency of the aircraft's history, far more than the country of registration alone.

Tip — If you are considering an import between the two countries, have the applicable customs and tax formalities confirmed in advance by a specialist professional: this avoids unpleasant surprises after signing.
