Buying a Used Aircraft in Bulgaria or Romania: Which Country to Choose

Market, prices, registration, taxation and pitfalls to avoid: a complete comparison between Bulgaria and Romania to help you choose where to buy your used light aircraft.

Two developing South-East European markets

Bulgaria and Romania share a common border and a comparable trajectory since joining the European Union: light aviation markets still modest in volume compared with Western Europe, but developing steadily, supported by more accessible operating costs and a growing presence of flying clubs. For a buyer widening their search beyond the denser Western European markets, both countries offer opportunities that are often less contested, sometimes with a more favourable price-to-aircraft ratio.

Both markets nonetheless remain limited in absolute size: finding a specific aircraft can require more patience than on a larger market such as Germany or France, and widening the search to either country, or to both at once, is often the best strategy.

Size and activity of the used market

The Bulgarian light aviation market remains small, concentrated around a handful of active airfields and clubs, with supply dominated by classic training single-engine aircraft and a growing microlight fleet. Proximity to the Black Sea and a climate favourable to year-round recreational flying are advantages regularly cited by local pilots.

The Romanian market, supported by a more populous country and a larger territory, offers a slightly broader aircraft fleet, with a stronger historical aeronautical tradition (long-established flying clubs, well-established flight schools). The used supply there also remains concentrated on touring and training single-engine aircraft, with a growing presence of microlights and modern engines.

Price comparison

In both countries, price levels for a comparable aircraft remain broadly lower than those seen in Western Europe, a factor that attracts some international buyers looking for good value. Differences between Bulgaria and Romania are mostly explained by the local scarcity of certain models and the respective size of each market rather than by any structural cost difference.

In both cases, comparing several listings of similar models remains the best way to assess a fair price: the gap between two aircraft of the same model and year is almost always explained by remaining engine hours before overhaul and the quality of the logbook, rather than by country of origin alone.

Registration: authorities and procedures

In Bulgaria, aircraft registration falls under the Bulgarian civil aviation authority, with the LZ- prefix. In Romania, the Romanian civil aviation authority manages the registration, with the YR- prefix. Both countries, as European Union members applying common EASA rules, follow a registration transfer framework between member states comparable to that seen anywhere in Europe.

  • Valid certificate of airworthiness (CofA) — a prerequisite for any flight under the new registration, whichever the destination country.
  • Deregistration from the register of origin — required before the new registration if the aircraft changes country.
  • Complete logbook — airframe, engine and propeller, to be submitted to the new authority for the file to be processed.
Good to know — Generally allow several weeks for a registration transfer between the two countries, a timeline comparable to that observed for any intra-European transfer, varying according to how complete the submitted file is.

Taxation and purchase-related costs

As with any transaction in Europe, applicable taxation depends above all on the buyer's status (private individual or professional), the country of tax residence and the country where the aircraft will be registered after the sale. A sale between private individuals is generally not subject to VAT in Bulgaria or in Romania, whereas a sale by a professional or a company may be subject to it under the applicable national rules.

For a cross-border transaction between the two countries, or with a buyer resident in another member state, it is worth checking any intra-community VAT formalities applicable between professionals, as well as the administrative fees specific to each destination country. Using a professional experienced in cross-border transactions limits the risk of procedural error.

Pre-purchase inspection and maintenance network

In both countries, a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic qualified on the type remains essential before signing anything. The network of approved maintenance shops there is less dense than in Western Europe, which can make choosing a shop competent on the relevant type somewhat more limited, particularly for less common models locally.

  • Up-to-date airframe, engine and propeller logbooks, with no chronological gaps in the aircraft's history.
  • History of maintenance invoices from the last three years, translated from the original language if needed.
  • No undisclosed structural damage, verifiable via the maintenance file and a thorough visual inspection.
Point of caution — Never finalise a purchase without having seen the original logbooks and having their consistency checked by a professional independent of the seller, a point all the more important in a market where the volume of comparable listings remains limited.

Which country to favour depending on your profile

For a buyer looking for a classic training single-engine aircraft on a tight budget, Bulgaria is worth exploring first for its overall operating cost, often seen as particularly accessible, and its climate favourable to regular recreational flying. For a buyer who prefers a slightly larger fleet and a well-established historical aeronautical tradition, with recognised flight schools, Romania deserves particular attention.

A pilot based in the region, or planning regular use in South-East Europe, will find a practical advantage in registering their aircraft in the country where they fly most often, for proximity to the maintenance shop and local administrative familiarity. Conversely, a geographically distant buyer should factor in from the start the cost and time of ferrying the aircraft to their home airfield, whether it is located in Bulgaria or Romania.

The overall cost of ownership should also factor in future parts availability and maintenance follow-up: an aircraft of a model uncommon locally may require using a shop in a neighbouring country for certain specialised operations. This factor, often underestimated, deserves to be anticipated before settling on one listing rather than another.

Good to know — In any case, a pre-purchase inspection by a qualified independent mechanic remains essential, whether the aircraft is located in Bulgaria, Romania or elsewhere in Europe.

Ultimately, widening your search to both markets often provides access to a broader supply and better negotiating opportunities, especially when the aircraft profile you are looking for matches the speciality of one country or the other. Taking time to calmly compare the listings available in both countries, rather than limiting yourself to the geographically closest market, remains the best strategy for finding the aircraft best suited to your use and your budget.
