Why the Citation II remains a sought-after entry-level jet

The Cessna Citation II is one of the most widely available twin-engine business jets on the used market. Designed as an enlarged evolution of the Citation I, it was built in large numbers from the late 1970s onward, making it today one of the most accessible entry points into business jet ownership for a private owner or a small professional operation.

Its cabin comfortably accommodates several passengers on regional and continental missions, with fuel burn and fixed costs noticeably lower than those of higher-category jets. The large number of airframes still flying guarantees an abundant supply of spare parts and a network of maintenance shops experienced with the type — two factors that weigh heavily in the decision to buy an already-mature jet.

On the used market, available aircraft span several decades of production and widely varying avionics levels, from the original analogue panel to a full digital retrofit. This diversity makes it essential to define your intended use clearly before setting a budget and targeting a model year.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used Citation II depends above all on the age of the airframe, total airframe and engine hours, the remaining engine potential before the next overhaul, the avionics fit and cabin condition. An older aircraft with reduced engine potential and original avionics trades at a noticeably more accessible entry level than a recent, well-equipped example fitted with a modern avionics retrofit.

Beyond the purchase price, the budget should include ancillary costs: a thorough pre-purchase inspection by a shop qualified on the type, registration and transfer fees, insurance, and, where needed, an avionics upgrade to meet current navigation and traffic-surveillance requirements. These items can represent a significant share of the total budget, particularly on an aircraft with older avionics.

What to check before buying

For a business jet like the Citation II, a pre-purchase inspection carried out by an approved maintenance shop, independent of the seller and experienced with the type, is essential before signing anything. Several points deserve particular attention, beyond what is checked on a light piston aircraft.

  • Complete engine and airframe logbooks, with no chronological gaps, and full traceability of every intervention and replaced part.
  • Engine hours elapsed and remaining potential before the next major overhaul — the single most decisive point in price negotiation.
  • History of scheduled structural inspections required by the manufacturer, and compliance with applicable service bulletins.
  • Condition and compliance of the avionics with current navigation and traffic-surveillance requirements in the intended airspace.
  • Overall condition of the cabin, pressurisation and ancillary systems (air conditioning, de-icing), which are costly to restore if neglected.
Good to know — On a business jet, the cost of a thorough pre-purchase inspection is nothing compared to the cost of a bad surprise discovered after signing: never skip this step with an approved shop independent of the seller.

Engines, SMOH and the cost of a major overhaul

The Citation II is fitted with two turbofans. Engine status, expressed in SMOH (hours elapsed since the last major overhaul), is the central element of any negotiation: an aircraft offered at an attractive price but whose engines are approaching their overhaul limit can actually represent a far heavier investment than an example priced higher but with substantial remaining engine potential.

The major engine overhaul is the heaviest expense item over the life of a business jet in this category. It is essential to ask the seller for the exact SMOH of each engine, as well as any engine coverage programs (hourly-cost contracts), which smooth out this expense but also affect the aircraft's sale price and must be transferred or renegotiated with the new owner.

Tip — Never take a declared SMOH at face value without cross-checking it against the original engine logbooks: that is the only document that is authoritative on the aircraft's real history.

Operating costs and crew

Operating a Citation II involves fixed and variable costs noticeably higher than those of a light piston aircraft: insurance, hangar parking, scheduled maintenance checks, fuel and, for most private operators, one or two pilots qualified on the type. Depending on the applicable regulations and the aircraft's configuration, a specific type rating may be required for the pilot in command.

Maintenance at an approved shop familiar with the type, combined with rigorous and well-documented record-keeping, helps limit unexpected costs and preserve the aircraft's resale value. Subscription-based maintenance programs (through the engine manufacturer or specialist shops) are worth considering to smooth out maintenance spending over time.

Tip — Always ask for the last three complete maintenance invoices and the details of any current engine coverage contracts before making an offer: they give an accurate picture of the aircraft's real cost of ownership.

Airworthiness, certification and regulatory requirements

Like any aircraft, a used Citation II must hold a valid certificate of airworthiness (CofA) and a logbook kept up to date in accordance with the requirements of the relevant oversight authority. A registration transfer between European countries follows the common rules applicable to any aircraft, with prior deregistration from the register of origin if the aircraft changes country of registration.

On a business jet, the compliance of the avionics with the navigation and traffic-surveillance requirements applicable in the intended airspace deserves particular scrutiny, as an upgrade can represent a significant investment if the aircraft is not already equipped. It is recommended to have these points confirmed by a professional before committing, since the exact requirements can vary by oversight authority and evolve over time.

Financing and owning a Citation II

Acquiring a business jet like the Citation II is rarely financed the same way as a light aircraft: a dedicated aviation loan, a finance lease, or fractional ownership among several users are the most common options for spreading the purchase and operating costs. The choice depends above all on expected usage intensity: an aircraft flown only a few hours a year often benefits from fractional ownership, while intensive professional use more often justifies full ownership or a dedicated holding structure.

Whichever ownership structure is chosen, it remains essential to budget the purchase price and annual operating costs separately, since for a business jet the latter represent a substantial share of the total cost of ownership over time.

Resale and the used market

The Citation II benefits from an active used market, supported by a large installed fleet and good parts availability, which generally makes resale easier than for jets built in smaller numbers. A well-maintained aircraft, with a transparent history, comfortable remaining engine potential and up-to-date avionics, resells more easily and on better terms than one whose technical file has grey areas.

Comparing several Citation II listings of a similar model year and engine status remains the best way to assess a fair price, both for buying and for preparing a future resale. Price differences between two similarly aged aircraft are almost always explained by remaining engine potential and the level of installed avionics.
