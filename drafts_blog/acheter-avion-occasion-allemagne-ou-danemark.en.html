Buying a used aircraft in Germany or Denmark: which country to choose

Market, prices, tax, registration and maintenance: a comparison between Germany and Denmark to help you decide where to look for your used aircraft.

Two Nordic markets of very different sizes

Germany and Denmark share a common border and a strong light aviation culture, but the two used-aircraft markets differ considerably in size. Germany, a much more populous country with a considerably denser airfield network, offers a far larger supply of used aircraft than Denmark. Denmark, for its part, compensates for a smaller supply with a compact market where listings are often well documented and where the owner community remains relatively easy to reach directly.

For a buyer based in northern Germany or southern Scandinavia, widening the search to both countries gives access to a notably larger volume of listings without straying too far from the intended home base.

Size and activity of the used market

Germany's light aircraft fleet is one of the largest in continental Europe, with abundant supply of classic touring single-engines (Cessna 172, Piper PA-28, Diamond DA40) as well as a substantial volume of microlights and two-seaters. The size of the market often allows several very similar aircraft to be compared before deciding.

The Danish market is much smaller in volume, in line with the country's size, but remains active within a well-structured network of local flying clubs. Supply is more concentrated on a handful of common models, with a notable share of aircraft rigorously maintained within flying clubs, where the maintenance history is often well documented and accessible.

Comparing prices

Price levels between the two markets remain broadly comparable for the same model and maintenance condition, with no systematic premium or discount in either direction. Variations observed are mostly down to local supply and demand, and to the relative scarcity of certain aircraft types in the smaller Danish market.

In both countries, comparing several listings of similar models remains the best way to assess a fair price before committing. In the smaller Danish market, it can be worth occasionally widening the search to neighbouring Germany to get a better comparison point for a given model.

Tax and purchase-related costs

As with any transaction in Europe, the applicable taxation depends above all on the buyer's status (private individual or professional), the country of tax residence and the country in which the aircraft will be registered after the sale. A sale between private individuals is generally not subject to VAT in either country, while a sale by a professional or a company may be subject to it under the applicable national rules.

For a cross-border transaction between Germany and Denmark, it is worth checking any intra-EU VAT formalities applicable between professionals, as well as the administrative fees specific to each destination country. Using a professional experienced in cross-border transactions reduces the risk of procedural errors.

Registration: the LBA versus the Trafikstyrelsen

In Germany, aircraft registration is handled by the Luftfahrt-Bundesamt (LBA), under the D- prefix. In Denmark, it is the Trafikstyrelsen (the Danish transport authority) that manages the registration register, under the OY- prefix. Both authorities apply common EASA rules for registration transfers between member states, which makes the process considerably simpler than an import from outside the European Union.

  • Valid certificate of airworthiness and ARC — a precondition for any flight under the new registration, whichever destination country is chosen.
  • Deregistration from the register of origin — required before the new registration if the aircraft changes country.
  • Complete technical file — airframe, engine and propeller logbooks to be submitted to the new authority.
Good to know — Generally allow 2 to 8 weeks for a registration transfer between Germany and Denmark depending on how complete the file is, a timeline comparable to that observed for any intra-European transfer.

Maintenance network and parts availability

Germany has a particularly dense network of approved maintenance shops, with good parts availability for the most common models. Denmark, with a smaller number of workshops, nonetheless has operators experienced with the models most common locally, often attached to the flying clubs themselves.

The geographic proximity between the two countries, together with the frequent use of English alongside Danish technical documentation, often makes it possible to have an aircraft maintained in either country with no major constraint, provided the chosen workshop is properly approved for the type concerned. For a rare or uncommon aircraft type in Denmark, a German workshop is often easier to find.

Which country to favour depending on your profile

For a buyer looking for a classic touring single-engine with the widest possible choice, Germany generally offers a larger volume of listings and therefore more scope for comparison. For a buyer whose future home base is in northern Europe or Scandinavia, the Danish market deserves particular attention: geographic proximity limits ferrying costs, and local listings are often well documented thanks to rigorous club-based maintenance.

A pilot planning to remain based in Denmark will find a practical benefit in registering their aircraft locally, if only to simplify day-to-day administrative procedures with the Trafikstyrelsen. Conversely, a pilot who favours long-distance touring flights across continental Europe will often find more practical advantages in sticking to the German market, which is better served by airfields and general-purpose workshops.

Overall ownership costs should also factor in ferrying: an aircraft spotted far from your home base will add extra cost and delay before it can first be used, whether it is located in Germany or in Denmark. This often-underestimated factor is worth quantifying before settling on one listing over another.

Good to know — In all cases, a pre-purchase inspection by a qualified independent mechanic remains essential, whether the aircraft is located in Germany, Denmark or elsewhere in Europe.

Ultimately, widening your search to both markets often provides access to a broader supply and better negotiating opportunities, especially for a buyer based in northern Germany or southern Scandinavia who can reasonably consider an aircraft located on either side of the border.
