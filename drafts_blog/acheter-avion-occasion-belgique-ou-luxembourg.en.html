Buying a used aircraft in Belgium or Luxembourg: which country to choose

Market, prices, tax, registration and maintenance: a comparison between Belgium and Luxembourg to help you decide where to search for your used aircraft.

Two neighbouring, very different small markets

Belgium and Luxembourg are direct neighbours, both within the EASA area, but their light aviation markets differ sharply in size and in the profile of the people buying and selling. Belgium has a considerably larger light aircraft fleet, supported by a dense network of airfields and a well-established community of pilots and flying clubs, particularly around Brussels, Antwerp and Wallonia. Luxembourg, a much smaller country, has a used market that is limited in volume but active, built around a handful of airfields and a frequently international clientele, given the country's position at the crossroads of several borders.

For a buyer based in Belgium, widening the search to Luxembourg opens up an additional pool of listings, with a very short road trip between the two countries making a viewing or a pre-purchase inspection easy to arrange. For a buyer based in Luxembourg, the Belgian market offers a considerably larger volume of listings, with deeper choice both among common models and more specialised aircraft.

Both markets share the same European regulatory framework (EASA) and the same certification standards, which greatly simplifies cross-border transactions. A buyer targeting a specific model therefore has every reason to systematically compare listings on both sides of the border before deciding, the short distance between the two countries making this particularly easy.

Size and activity of the used market

The Belgian market offers a fairly wide supply of general aviation classics (touring single-engine aircraft, microlights) as well as gliders and light helicopters, with a significant presence of international sellers who benefit from the country's central location. That diversity makes it easier to compare several similar aircraft before deciding, a real advantage when negotiating a purchase price.

The Luxembourg market, much smaller in volume given the size of the country, remains active at its own scale, driven in part by a business-aviation clientele and by immediate proximity to Belgium, France and Germany. Listings there are fewer, but sellers are often international and the aircraft on offer tend to be well maintained, since the market is simply too small to absorb poorly kept aircraft for long.

Comparing the prices charged

Price levels between the two markets remain broadly comparable for the same model and the same maintenance condition, with observed differences owing more to local supply and demand at any given time than to a structural difference between the two countries. The smaller Luxembourg market can sometimes show slightly higher prices on the most sought-after aircraft, simply for lack of comparable local supply.

In both cases, comparing several listings of similar models remains the best way to assess a fair price before committing, regardless of which country the aircraft you want is located in.

As the Luxembourg market is particularly small, a buyer specifically targeting this country should set up alerts and move fast once a listing matches their criteria, always subject to a satisfactory pre-purchase inspection.

Tax and purchase-related costs

A private sale involving an aircraft already in free circulation within the European Union generally does not trigger a new VAT charge, whether the sale takes place in Belgium or Luxembourg, since the tax was already paid when the aircraft was first put into circulation within the EU. The situation differs for a sale made by a VAT-registered professional, or for an aircraft imported from outside the EU, which can trigger import VAT calculated on its customs value, in either country.

For a cross-border transaction between Belgium and Luxembourg, it is worth checking precisely any intra-EU VAT formalities applicable between professionals, as well as the ownership-transfer costs specific to each country. Using a professional familiar with cross-border transactions (broker, specialised lawyer) helps avoid procedural mistakes that can prove costly, particularly regarding VAT or customs documentation.

Registration: DGTA/BCAA versus DAC

In Belgium, the competent authority is the Direction générale Transport aérien (DGTA), also known by its Dutch name Bestuur van de Luchtvaart (BCAA), which issues the OO- prefix. In Luxembourg, registration falls under the Direction de l'Aviation Civile (DAC), which issues the LX- prefix. An aircraft bought in either country can be registered in the other, depending on the buyer's choice: keeping the registration of the purchase country is not mandatory.

  • Check the consistency between the logbook, the technical file and the aircraft's actual condition before any change of registration.
  • For an aircraft imported from another country, plan for its prior deregistration before the new registration can be issued.
  • Plan for administrative lead times, which can vary significantly between the DGTA/BCAA and the DAC.
  • Gather a valid Certificate of Airworthiness (CofA), the bill of sale and proof of liability insurance before the first flight under the new registration.
Good to know — Common EASA rules govern transfers between member states and make them simpler than an import from outside the European Union. Expect generally 2 to 8 weeks depending on how complete the file is, regardless of the destination country.

Maintenance network and parts availability

Belgium has a well-developed network of approved maintenance workshops for the most common models, which makes maintenance easy regardless of the aircraft's final country of registration. Luxembourg, a much smaller country, has a more limited number of approved workshops on its own territory, but benefits directly from the immediate proximity of the Belgian, French and German maintenance networks, with no competent workshop ever very far away given the short distances in this region.

For rarer models or specific engines, it remains useful to check in advance the availability of parts and the proximity of a specialised workshop before finalising the purchase, regardless of the aircraft's country of origin. Luxembourg's small size works in its favour here: turning to a neighbouring Belgian, French or German workshop always remains a simple, quick option.

The language barrier is generally limited in both countries: a large share of aviation professionals speak English alongside their national language, which makes interactions easy for a buyer coming from the other side of the border.

Which country to favour depending on your profile

For a buyer who favours a wide choice and immediate proximity to their flying club or home airfield, searching primarily on the Belgian market often remains the simplest solution, given the volume of listings available. For a buyer willing to widen their search to find a well-maintained aircraft from an international seller, or prepared to wait for a rare listing that precisely matches their criteria, also keeping an eye on the Luxembourg market provides access to complementary supply and makes it easier to compare the technical files available.

Good to know — In all cases, a pre-purchase inspection by a qualified independent mechanic remains essential, whether the aircraft is located in Belgium, Luxembourg or elsewhere in Europe.

Ultimately, the country of purchase matters less than the actual condition of the aircraft and the quality of its maintenance record. Comparing listings from both markets widens your options and gives you a stronger negotiating position, whether on price or on the terms of the transaction.
