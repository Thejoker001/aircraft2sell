Why the Aeroprakt A22 appeals to microlight pilots

The Aeroprakt A22, sold under the Foxbat name in several countries, is a high-wing microlight designed by the Ukrainian manufacturer Aeroprakt. Its all-metal riveted structure, exceptional visibility thanks to a large glazed canopy, and gentle flying characteristics make it particularly popular for training, introductory flights and low-speed touring. Its design favours mechanical simplicity and ease of maintenance, two qualities sought after by private owners, flying clubs and flight schools alike.

On the used market, the A22 comes in several successive versions, with differences in engine, avionics and some standard equipment. This diversity of versions makes it essential to identify the exact example in question before comparing prices across listings.

Its low stall speed and slow-flight manners also make it a popular choice for aerial observation and short strips, a type of use that sometimes shapes the airframe hours and the kind of wear found on a used example.

Foxbat A22 versions: L, LS and recent updates

Before settling on a budget, it is worth clearly distinguishing between versions of the model. The A22L, the original version, has a simpler airframe and generally an analogue panel. The A22LS, more common on the European market, features a slightly enlarged cabin and more modern avionics options. More recent updates from the manufacturer add digital cockpits and improved interior fittings.

A buyer should verify the exact designation of the aircraft in question with the seller or in the manufacturer's documentation: listings sometimes use the generic "Foxbat" name without specifying the version, which can make it harder to compare offers and skew the assessment of a fair price.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used A22 depends mainly on the year of manufacture, the exact version, airframe and engine hours, and the level of avionics fitted. The oldest examples, with basic avionics and reduced engine potential, trade noticeably lower than well-equipped recent versions.

Beyond the purchase price, plan for the usual ancillary costs: an independent pre-purchase inspection, microlight registration fees, insurance and, if needed, an avionics update or a refresh of the cabin interior.

Rotax engine and remaining potential

Like the vast majority of microlights in its class, the A22 is mainly fitted with an engine from the Rotax 912 family, known for its reliability and the wide availability of spare parts across Europe. The number of hours elapsed since the last major overhaul (SMOH) remains the central indicator to check: the higher the remaining potential before the next overhaul, the more value the aircraft retains on the resale market.

It is also worth checking that components subject to a calendar limit, not just an hourly one, have been replaced within the timeframe set by the engine manufacturer, regardless of the actual flight hours flown. An engine that has seen little use but was poorly preserved during long periods of inactivity can develop internal faults despite low hours on the clock.

Riveted aluminium airframe: what to watch

Unlike some composite-structure competitors, the A22 is built around an all-metal, traditionally riveted airframe. This design generally makes repairs and visual diagnosis easier, but it calls for a careful check of corrosion, particularly at the contact points between rivets and skin, as well as a check of play in the flight controls and any impact marks on the landing gear.

The large glazed canopy, a signature feature behind the aircraft's exceptional visibility, should also be inspected carefully: deep scratches, stress cracks or sealing defects can require a costly replacement. Checking the condition of the hinges and the canopy's closing mechanism usefully rounds out this check.

What to check before buying

As with any used aircraft, a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic qualified on the type is essential before finalising a transaction. For an A22, a few points deserve particular attention.

  • Precise identification of the exact version (L, LS, recent update) and a match with the documents supplied by the seller.
  • Detailed engine history: hours since the last overhaul, compliance with the Rotax maintenance program, traceability of replaced parts.
  • Condition of the riveted aluminium airframe: checking for corrosion, excessive play in the controls and impact marks on the gear.
  • Condition of the canopy and its seals, a sensitive point given its large glazed area.
  • Complete logbook and technical file, with no grey areas in the aircraft's history.
Good to know — Never rely solely on the seller's statements about overall condition: an independent pre-purchase inspection by a mechanic qualified on the type remains the best protection before committing.

Maintenance and the logbook

The operating cost of the A22 stays moderate for its class, helped by the mechanical simplicity of its metal airframe and the good availability of parts linked to its Rotax engine. The main expense items are the periodic maintenance required by the manufacturer's program, insurance, parking and, further down the line, the engine overhaul once its hourly potential is reached.

A logbook kept rigorously, at a shop familiar with the type, is one of the strongest arguments for reassuring a future buyer. Asking for the most recent maintenance invoices before making an offer lets you check that the declared hours match the work actually carried out.

Tip — Always ask for the last three maintenance invoices before making an offer: they give an accurate picture of how seriously the aircraft has been maintained.

Registration and microlight regulations in Europe

Microlights fall, depending on the European country, under their own national rules, distinct from the certification that applies to conventional aircraft. Registration procedures, training requirements and the limits of the category vary from one country to another, which can affect the steps required for a cross-border purchase or a change of country of operation.

Before buying an A22 in a country other than the one where you plan to fly it, it is advisable to check with the relevant national microlight authority or federation on the procedures for transferring registration and on whether the pilot's licence will be recognised.

Resale and the used market

The A22 benefits from an active used market in Europe, supported by its reputation for toughness and a loyal community of owners and schools. A well-maintained aircraft, with a transparent history and a clearly identified version, generally resells more easily and on better terms than one whose technical file has grey areas.

Comparing several listings of similar aircraft remains the best way to assess a fair price, both for buying and for preparing a future resale. Price differences between two aircraft of the same model year are most often explained by the exact version, remaining engine potential and the level of avionics fitted.
