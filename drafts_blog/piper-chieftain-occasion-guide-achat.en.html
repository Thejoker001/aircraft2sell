The Piper Chieftain, a twin built for transport

The Piper PA-31-350 Chieftain is the stretched, most powerful version of the Navajo family, designed for passenger and light cargo transport over medium distances. With its stretched cabin offering more seating and turbocharged engines more powerful than the standard Navajo's, the Chieftain has become a benchmark aircraft for air taxi operators, multi-engine flight schools and light cargo operators.

On the used market, the Chieftain attracts buyers looking for substantial payload, good range and a rugged airframe, in exchange for an operating budget notably higher than a single-engine aircraft or a light twin such as the Seneca.

The Chieftain's cabin, longer than the original Navajo's, typically offers several rows of seating, making it a sought-after aircraft for larger families or small professional teams travelling together. This payload capacity, combined with good useful load, explains why the Chieftain long held a prominent place in regional light transport fleets, before being gradually replaced by turboprops on some demanding commercial routes.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used Chieftain depends above all on the aircraft's age, total airframe hours, the status of both turbocharged engines (remaining potential before overhaul), the installed avionics, and the operating history — an aircraft that has been in intensive charter use needs more thorough checking than one used under private ownership.

Beyond the purchase price, your budget should include related costs: a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic familiar with the type, registration and transfer fees, twin-engine insurance generally higher than for a single, and possibly an avionics upgrade if the aircraft needs one.

Points to check before buying

As with any used aircraft, a pre-purchase inspection carried out by an independent mechanic qualified on the type is essential before finalising a deal. For a Chieftain, certain points deserve particular attention.

  • Complete history of both engines: hours since the last overhaul, consistency between the two powerplants, traceability of replaced parts.
  • Condition of the turbochargers and their accessories, among the most expensive components to replace on this engine type.
  • Airframe corrosion, particularly in moisture-exposed areas and the spars.
  • Avionics condition and compliance with current regulatory requirements (transponder, traffic surveillance).
  • Complete logbook and technical file, with particular attention if the aircraft has been used in commercial operation.
Good to know — Never rely solely on the seller's statements about the engines' general condition: a written independent inspection report, including a recent oil analysis, remains the best protection against unpleasant surprises after purchase.

Turbocharged engines: a key point of attention

The Chieftain's two turbocharged engines deliver the power needed to carry a substantial payload, but they also demand stricter operating and maintenance discipline than naturally aspirated engines. Following correct engine-management procedures, temperature limits and the manufacturer's maintenance intervals heavily influences the aircraft's longevity and reliability.

Before buying, it is worth asking the seller for the precise history of turbocharger interventions and confirmation that the maintenance programme has been followed, so you can anticipate a potentially significant expense if these components are approaching their limit.

Maintenance and operating costs

Operating costs for a Chieftain are logically higher than for a single-engine aircraft or a light twin, due to managing two turbocharged engines, a larger airframe and higher fuel burn. The main cost items are the mandatory annual inspection, twin-engine insurance, parking suited to its size, and engine overhaul once the hourly potential is reached for each of the two powerplants.

Having the aircraft maintained at an approved shop experienced with the type helps limit unpleasant surprises and preserves its resale value. Rigorous, well-documented maintenance records, particularly for the engines, are a strong selling point for a future resale.

Insurance budget also deserves particular attention: the premium for a twin in this category depends heavily on the pilot's experience on type, the declared use (private or commercial) and the parking location. A pilot with little experience on heavy twins may be required by the insurer to complete additional training hours before full coverage is granted, which represents an extra cost and delay to anticipate in your purchase plan.

Professional use: charter, flight school and light cargo

The Chieftain has long been a reference aircraft for air taxi operators, multi-engine flight schools and light cargo operations, thanks to its payload and rugged profile. Buyers considering professional use should anticipate specific requirements tied to commercial operation, as well as stricter maintenance tracking than for private use.

For personal use, the Chieftain remains a relevant choice for families or small groups wishing to travel with more passengers and baggage than a light twin allows, provided you accept the corresponding operating budget.

Resale and the used market

The used Chieftain market remains active in Europe, supported by a significant installed fleet and good parts availability thanks to its kinship with the rest of the Navajo family. A well-maintained aircraft with a transparent history and up-to-date avionics generally resells more easily and on better terms than one with gaps in its technical file, particularly regarding engine history.

Comparing several listings of similar models and model years remains the best way to assess a fair price, both for buying and for preparing a future resale.
