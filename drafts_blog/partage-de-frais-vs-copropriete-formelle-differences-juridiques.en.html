Cost-Sharing vs Formal Co-Ownership: Legal Differences

Sharing an aircraft with other pilots doesn't mean the same thing depending on the structure chosen. Legal status, ownership, insurance, taxation and exiting a partner: what really distinguishes simple cost-sharing from formal co-ownership.

Cost-sharing and formal co-ownership: definitions

"Cost-sharing" describes an informal arrangement between several pilots who split the costs of an aircraft that one of them remains the sole owner of, or that all of them jointly rent from a third-party owner. There is no dedicated legal structure: a written agreement (often a simple cost-sharing agreement) sets out the rules for splitting costs and booking flight slots, but ownership of the aircraft itself is not shared in a legal sense.

Formal co-ownership goes further: several people each hold a registered ownership share of the aircraft, most often through a dedicated legal structure (a civil company, association or equivalent depending on the country) that owns the used aircraft and in which the co-owners are partners or members. Each co-owner then holds an actual property right in the aircraft, not just a usage right defined by contract.

Legal status and liability

In a simple cost-sharing arrangement, the sole owner remains legally liable for the aircraft towards third parties, the authorities and the insurer; the other pilots are merely contractual users. In the event of a dispute between participants, ordinary contract law applies, with all the limitations of an agreement that was often not drafted by a lawyer.

In a formal co-ownership organised through a company, liability is in principle shared between the co-owners according to their shares, and the structure itself bears certain obligations (registration, maintenance, insurance) as a legal entity. This separation protects each co-owner individually better, but implies heavier administrative management: articles of association to draft, meetings, and accounting for the structure.

Good to know — A poorly drafted cost-sharing agreement often offers no real protection in the event of a serious disagreement (non-payment, accident, resale). A written agreement, even a simple one, remains essential as soon as several people regularly use the same aircraft.

Ownership of the aircraft and the register

In a classic cost-sharing arrangement, only one name is generally listed on the registration register as owner, even if several people fund the aircraft in practice: the other participants have no right enforceable against the aircraft itself, only against the contractual agreement that binds them to the owner.

In a formal co-ownership organised through a company, it is the structure itself that is registered as owner with the civil aviation authority, and each co-owner holds a share of the company rather than a direct fraction of the aircraft. This setup clarifies matters when a share is resold: it is the transfer of company shares that changes hands, without requiring a new registration of the aircraft itself.

  • Check who is actually listed on the registration register before making any financial commitment.
  • In a company structure, ask for the articles of association and the shareholders' agreement before joining the co-ownership.
  • Have the exact split of shares or usage quotas put in writing, even in a simple cost-sharing arrangement.

Insurance and liability cover

In a cost-sharing arrangement, the insurance policy is generally taken out by the sole owner, who must check that the policy actually covers all participating pilots and not just themselves: an incomplete declaration of regular users can invalidate cover in the event of a claim. In a formal co-ownership, insurance is most often taken out directly by the structure, covering all co-owners designated as authorised pilots, which simplifies verification but requires keeping that list up to date each time a co-owner changes.

Point of caution — In both cases, check that liability insurance explicitly covers every pilot who is going to fly the aircraft, either by name or by qualification category, before any first use.

Taxation and VAT

Tax treatment differs depending on the structure chosen. Cost-sharing between private individuals generally falls outside the scope of VAT as long as the amounts paid strictly correspond to a split of real operating costs (fuel, maintenance, hangar, insurance) with no margin or commercial activity. A co-ownership organised as a company may, depending on its legal form and actual activity, be subject to the accounting and tax obligations specific to any legal entity, even when its sole purpose is holding the aircraft for the use of its members. In both cases, it is recommended to have the precise tax situation confirmed by a professional before committing, as the rules vary depending on the country of registration and the status of the participants.

Governance, decisions and a partner's exit

In a cost-sharing arrangement, decisions about the aircraft (major repair, avionics upgrade, resale) remain in principle solely in the hands of the owner, with the other participants only having informal negotiating power. A participant's exit is generally simple: it is enough to end the cost-sharing agreement, with no particular formality linked to the aircraft itself.

In a formal co-ownership, important decisions are in principle taken collectively according to the rules set out in the articles of association (simple majority, unanimity for certain decisions), which protects each co-owner but can also slow down an urgent decision. A co-owner's exit goes through the transfer of their share to a third party approved by the other partners, or through an internal buyback, a process that must be planned for from the drafting of the articles of association to avoid a deadlock if no one wishes to buy the share.

  • Set out, in writing and from the start, the rules for exit and share transfer, whatever structure is chosen.
  • Set a valuation mechanism for the share in case of resale, to avoid a disagreement when a partner leaves.
  • Clarify who decides on major expenses (major overhaul, avionics) and above what threshold.

Legal pitfalls to avoid

The most common mistake in an informal cost-sharing arrangement is never formalising the agreement in writing, which leaves every disagreement to be settled case by case with no clear rule, particularly in the event of an accident or a participant's non-payment. In a formal co-ownership, the most common pitfall is underestimating the administrative burden of the structure (accounting, meetings, updating the articles of association every time a co-owner changes) and letting it become out of step with the reality of the participants. In both cases, failing to check that insurance actually covers all the real pilots, not just the official signatory or signatories, remains a recurring source of unpleasant surprises in the event of a claim.

Tip — Always have the cost-sharing agreement or the co-ownership structure's articles of association reviewed by an aviation law professional before committing, whatever the amount involved.

Which structure to choose for your use case

Simple cost-sharing suits a small group of pilots who already know each other, for occasional or seasonal use, and who prioritise administrative simplicity over stronger legal protection. Formal co-ownership is better suited to a long-term project involving several co-owners over time, a larger investment, or an explicit wish to separate ownership of the aircraft from each person's individual use. In all cases, the written agreement — whether a simple cost-sharing agreement or the articles of association of a co-ownership company — remains the single most important document to get right before the aircraft is first shared.
