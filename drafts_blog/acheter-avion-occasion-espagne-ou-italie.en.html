Buying a Used Aircraft in Spain or Italy: Which Country to Choose

Market, prices, taxation, registration and maintenance: a comparison between Spain and Italy to help you decide where to look for your used aircraft.

Two Mediterranean markets, two different profiles

Spain and Italy both enjoy a climate favourable to light aviation almost year-round, but their used-aircraft markets have fairly different profiles. Spain benefits from a vast network of airfields and flight schools, with activity particularly strong in the south of the country (Andalusia) and along the Mediterranean coast, driven in part by flight training for pilots from other European countries. Italy, for its part, concentrates most of its general aviation in the north (Lombardy, Veneto, Piedmont), with a smaller fleet but a growing leisure-flying scene.

A buyer based in either country can perfectly well find an interesting aircraft in the other, provided they understand the administrative and practical specifics of each before entering a cross-border transaction.

Size and dynamism of the used-aircraft market

The Spanish used-aircraft market is large and diverse, supported by a significant number of flight schools and clubs that regularly renew their fleet of piston single-engine types (Cessna 172, Piper PA-28, Tecnam). This training activity feeds a steady flow of used aircraft, often well maintained thanks to intensive but closely supervised use.

The Italian market is smaller in absolute volume but remains active, particularly in the north of the country. Supply there tends to lean more towards microlights and recently certified or amateur-built light aircraft, with a strong appeal for economical types to operate.

Comparing prices

Price levels between the two markets remain broadly comparable for the same model and the same maintenance condition, with observed differences owing more to local supply and demand than to a structural difference between the two countries. However, an aircraft used by a Spanish flight school, maintained rigorously and with full documentation, may show more flight hours than an Italian club aircraft used more occasionally.

In both cases, comparing several listings of similar models remains the best way to assess a fair price before committing, wherever the coveted aircraft happens to be.

Seasonality also plays a role: the Italian market often sees a peak in listings at the end of the flying season in regions where activity is concentrated in the warmer months, whereas the Spanish market, thanks to a milder climate for most of the year, offers a more stable supply over time. Widening your search to both countries therefore often provides access to a wider and more stable choice.

Taxation and purchase-related costs

The taxation applicable to the purchase of a used aircraft depends above all on the buyer's status (private individual or professional), their country of tax residence and the country in which the aircraft will be registered after the transaction, more than on the single country where the aircraft is located at the time of purchase. In a cross-border transaction between Spain and Italy, it is worth checking precisely the possible intra-community VAT formalities applicable between professionals, as well as the ownership-transfer fees specific to each country, which can vary significantly.

Using a professional familiar with cross-border transactions (broker, specialist lawyer) helps avoid procedural mistakes that can prove costly, particularly regarding VAT or customs documentation when the aircraft changes country of registration.

Registration and administrative procedures

In Spain, civil aircraft registration is handled by AESA (Agencia Estatal de Seguridad Aérea), under the EC- prefix. In Italy, it is ENAC (Ente Nazionale per l'Aviazione Civile) that maintains the national register, under the I- prefix. An aircraft bought in one of the two countries can be registered in the other, depending on the buyer's choice: keeping the registration of the country of purchase is not mandatory. This change, however, involves specific procedures with the destination authority, including submitting the complete technical file, checking the validity of the certificate of airworthiness and, where applicable, a compliance inspection.

  • Check that the logbook, technical file and the aircraft's actual condition are consistent before any change of registration.
  • Plan ahead for administrative timelines, which can vary noticeably between AESA and ENAC depending on their current workload.
  • Check for any specific onboard equipment requirements in the destination country.
  • Budget for ferry-flight costs if an inspection or compliance work is needed before the transfer.

Maintenance network and parts availability

Spain has a well-developed network of approved maintenance workshops, particularly in the regions where flight schools are concentrated, which makes maintaining the most common types straightforward. Italy also has a network of competent workshops, concentrated mainly in the north, but which can be sparser in some central and southern regions for less common types.

For rarer models or specific engines, it is worth checking in advance the availability of parts and the proximity of a specialist workshop before finalising the purchase, regardless of the aircraft's country of origin. The language barrier can also come into play: a buyer not comfortable in Spanish or Italian will often favour an English-speaking workshop, an option generally available in both countries for routine technical documentation.

Climate and operating conditions

Climate is an important factor for many buyers. Southern Spain and the islands (Balearics, Canaries) offer favourable flying conditions for most of the year, which limits storage and maintenance constraints linked to humidity or frost. Northern Italy experiences more pronounced winters, with sometimes harsher conditions for outdoor aircraft storage, while the south of the country is closer to Spanish Mediterranean conditions.

For an aircraft stored outdoors, these climate differences can have a real effect on the condition of the airframe and paintwork over the years, a point worth checking carefully during any pre-purchase inspection, whatever the aircraft's country of origin.

Which country to favour depending on your profile

For a buyer looking for an aircraft that has been used for flight training, with rigorous and well-documented maintenance, the Spanish market often offers a wider choice thanks to the weight of flight schools. For a buyer interested in a recent microlight or an amateur-built aircraft at a controlled price, the Italian market can present interesting opportunities, particularly in the north of the country.

Good to know — In all cases, a pre-purchase inspection by a qualified independent mechanic remains essential, whether the aircraft is located in Spain, Italy or elsewhere in Europe.

There is nothing stopping you from searching for an aircraft in one of the two countries while planning to have it maintained or registered in the other, as long as ferrying distances remain reasonable and administrative procedures are anticipated. The two markets complement each other more than they compete, for a buyer willing to widen their search.

Ultimately, the country of purchase matters less than the actual quality of the aircraft and how well it has been maintained. Comparing listings from both markets widens your options and gives you a better negotiating position, whether on price or on the terms of the transaction.
