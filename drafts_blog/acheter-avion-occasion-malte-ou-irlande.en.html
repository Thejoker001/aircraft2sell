Buying a used aircraft in Malta or Ireland: which country to choose

Two EASA registers both known for their attractiveness, but built on very different logics. A full comparison of the second-hand market, registration and tax rules to help you decide.

The used aircraft markets in Malta and Ireland

Malta and Ireland have almost nothing in common in terms of the light aviation second-hand market, which is exactly what makes comparing them interesting for a buyer who is primarily looking for a favourable regulatory and tax framework rather than a large local supply of aircraft.

Malta has an extremely small domestic light aviation market: the territory is tiny, the number of airfields is limited, and the locally based fleet is modest. The Maltese register (registration prefix 9H-), however, has become an international reference for aircraft registration, including business jets and aircraft that are never physically based on the island. Listings advertised as "for sale in Malta" are therefore usually aircraft already registered 9H- through management companies, rather than a local flying-club fleet comparable to other European countries.

Ireland, by contrast, has a genuine general aviation ecosystem: active flying clubs, flight schools, and a fleet of light singles (Cessna 172, Piper PA-28, a number of Diamonds) comparable to other north-west European countries. The Irish register (prefix EI-) is also heavily used for airliner and business aircraft leasing, which has historically built a solid legal and tax infrastructure around aviation that benefits light aviation indirectly.

Two registers with different logics

Both countries are EU members operating under the EASA framework, which simplifies transferring an EASA-certified aircraft already between the two registers or from another member state. But the underlying philosophy of each register differs significantly.

For a buyer who genuinely wants to base and operate the aircraft locally (flying from a nearby airfield, keeping it there), Ireland offers a far more natural setting. For a buyer primarily looking for an international registration structure — for example through a holding company, with no intention of physically basing the aircraft in the country of registration — the Maltese register has historically been more commonly used for that purpose, particularly for business jets and turboprops.

Tax and VAT

Tax treatment is often the deciding factor between these two countries, and it is also the most sensitive topic: the rules evolve and depend heavily on the legal structure used (purchase in a personal name, through a company, private or commercial use). Advice from a tax specialist familiar with aviation is essential before any decision, in either case.

Malta has in the past put in place a VAT leasing scheme for aircraft that could reduce the effective taxable base for certain aircraft types, which partly explains the register's attractiveness for operations structured through a Maltese company. This type of arrangement remains governed by EU VAT rules and has been subject to regulatory changes over the years; it should never be assumed applicable without up-to-date confirmation from local counsel.

In Ireland, a sale between private individuals is generally not subject to Irish VAT, following the same principles applied elsewhere in the EU: a sale by a professional or a company may be subject to VAT, and an aircraft imported from outside the EU remains subject to import VAT. Ireland does not offer a specific scheme comparable to the Maltese arrangement for private aviation.

Point of caution — Never pick a country of registration purely on the promise of a tax advantage heard online or from a commercial intermediary. Have the structure validated by a tax specialist who specifically knows aviation before signing anything.

Registration and procedures

For a purchase between private individuals already registered in the target country, the process is simply an update of ownership with the relevant authority (Transport Malta or the IAA). For an aircraft imported from another country, deregistration from the original register is required before the new registration.

  • Certificate of airworthiness and current ARC — check validity before starting any registration process.
  • Bill of sale — a key document for any register transfer, in both countries.
  • Proof of ownership and, where applicable, the holding structure — a Maltese or Irish holding company involves its own formation and ongoing management requirements.

In both cases, procedures are handled entirely in English, which makes life easier compared to a register that only operates in a non-English local language. Registration typically takes anywhere from a few weeks to two months depending on how complete the file is and, where relevant, on setting up the holding structure.

Basing and operating costs

Beyond registration, the real cost of ownership depends heavily on where the aircraft is actually based and maintained, which does not necessarily match the country of registration.

If the aircraft is genuinely based in Malta, hangarage and local maintenance costs can be high given the small size of the market and the limited number of approved workshops on the island; many owners of 9H-registered aircraft actually base them elsewhere in Europe. If the aircraft is based in Ireland, the network of workshops and airfields is far more developed, with costs broadly comparable to the UK or France for a light single.

Tip — Always separate the country of registration from the aircraft's actual base when budgeting: maintenance and parking costs depend on the latter, while tax treatment and administrative procedures often depend on the former.

Malta or Ireland: which buyer profile

The choice between the two countries depends mainly on your actual intended use of the aircraft, more than on a general preference for one register over the other.

  • You want to fly locally, based at an airfield close to home — Ireland fits a classic general aviation use case much better, with a flying club and a workshop nearby.
  • You are buying through a professional structure, for a turboprop or a jet, with no intention of a local base — the Maltese register has historically been preferred in this specific case, subject to up-to-date tax and legal validation.
  • You are still unsure about the structure — an upfront consultation with an aviation tax specialist and a registration manager remains the essential starting point before any purchase.

Pitfalls to avoid

The most common mistake is choosing a country of registration purely for an assumed tax advantage, without verifying that the specific aircraft, its intended use and its ownership structure actually make that arrangement applicable. A second classic pitfall: underestimating the cost and complexity of managing a foreign holding company (accounting, filing obligations) for what is actually simple, occasional private use, where a more direct registration in the aircraft's real base country would have been simpler and cheaper over time.

Tip — Always have an independent pre-purchase inspection carried out regardless of the intended country of registration: the register tells you nothing about the real condition of the airframe, engine or avionics.
