Certified Electric Aircraft: The State of the Used Market in 2026

Certified electric aircraft remain an early-stage market: where does it genuinely stand in 2026, and what should you expect if you're considering a used one?

An early-stage market

Certified electric aircraft are not a theoretical category: real aircraft fly, get registered and are used commercially in Europe today. But the number of models that have earned a full type certificate remains very small, and the used market that would follow from it is, for now, close to non-existent. It is worth separating three very different categories that often get blurred in conversation: uncertified prototypes and demonstrators, projects in ongoing certification announced by various manufacturers, and aircraft that have actually been granted a type certificate by a recognised civil aviation authority. Only this last category concerns a buyer looking for something registrable and insurable like any conventional light aircraft.

That scarcity is not surprising: existing certification standards were built around piston and turbine propulsion, and adapting them to a fully electric powertrain — with its own questions on battery thermal management, electrical redundancy and behaviour in the event of a cell failure — is a long and costly process for manufacturers that mostly remain small.

The reference case: the Pipistrel Velis Electro

The Pipistrel Velis Electro is today the best-documented case of an electric aircraft that has earned a full type certificate from the European Union Aviation Safety Agency (EASA), a milestone reached in 2020. It is a tandem two-seater designed primarily for initial flight training, built by the Slovenian manufacturer Pipistrel (since acquired by Textron). It is now used by several European flying schools for introductory flights and parts of PPL training, its limited endurance (roughly an hour of flight, with charging between flights) fitting well with circuit work and local exercises.

Beyond this specific case, numerous other electric or hybrid aircraft projects are regularly announced by start-ups and established manufacturers, at very different stages of development. Until an aircraft has actually been granted a full type certificate by a recognised authority, it should not be treated as equivalent to the Velis Electro for a purchase: only actual certification guarantees standard registration and insurability under normal market conditions.

Good to know — Always confirm the exact certification status of any aircraft marketed as "electric" before negotiating: a demonstrator, an experimental-category test aircraft and a type-certified aircraft follow very different rules for purchase, insurance and registration.

Why a used market barely exists yet

Three factors explain the near-total absence of a genuine used market for certified electric aircraft in 2026. First, the fleet in service remains small and is concentrated mostly with flying schools and professional operators, who tend to run their aircraft intensively rather than sell them quickly. Second, these aircraft are still early in their operational life: there isn't enough track record yet to properly assess resale value after several years of use and several battery replacement cycles. Third, the pool of private buyers remains narrow: the limited range of current models steers them mainly toward training or local flying, a different usage profile from what most buyers of used light aircraft are actually looking for.

In practice, a private buyer searching today for a used certified electric aircraft will mostly find aircraft coming out of flying-school fleets after a period of intensive use, rather than a diversified secondary market comparable to the one for Cessna 172s or Piper PA-28s.

Maintenance and batteries: different rules from piston aircraft

Maintaining a certified electric aircraft departs noticeably from the usual benchmarks of light piston aviation. The familiar idea of engine hours before TBO on a piston engine has no direct equivalent: tracking instead centres on battery charge and discharge cycles, its remaining capacity and ageing, parameters monitored electronically and recorded in dedicated documentation separate from a traditional engine logbook.

  • Battery cycles — the figure to check first, rather than engine hours in the classic sense, together with the remaining capacity stated by the manufacturer.
  • Battery replacement — a specific cost item to plan for, whose scale depends directly on observed wear and the manufacturer's policy.
  • Electric logbook — ask for full traceability of charge cycles, not just a conventional flight-hours history.
  • Software updates — battery management systems keep evolving; check the aircraft has received the manufacturer's recommended updates.
Point of caution — Never buy a used certified electric aircraft without obtaining a precise battery health assessment and a written estimate of eventual replacement cost from the manufacturer or an approved centre: it carries the same weight as checking remaining engine potential on a piston aircraft.

Insurance and resale value: the open questions

Insuring a certified electric aircraft is still a recent exercise for aviation underwriters, who have little historical claims data specific to this type of powertrain. Expect a more detailed conversation with your broker than for a conventional piston aircraft, particularly around the replacement value of the battery in the event of a claim.

Medium-term resale value also remains largely uncertain for lack of sufficient track record: unlike a Cessna 172 or a Cirrus SR22, whose depreciation curves have been documented for decades, there is not yet a reliable benchmark for a certified electric aircraft that has accumulated several years of real operational use. Any precise figure quoted on this point today would be speculation rather than established market data.

Training and current use

As the market stands in 2026, the dominant use of certified electric aircraft remains initial flight training within flying schools, particularly for early dual-instruction hours and circuit work, where the limited endurance of these aircraft is not a practical obstacle. Some operators also point to a cost-per-flight-hour argument, since electricity is generally cheaper than aviation fuel, although that calculation depends heavily on local electricity prices and how battery depreciation is accounted for over time.

Classic touring or leisure cross-country flying remains, for now, out of reach for the certified models currently available, whose range is measured in tens of minutes rather than hours.

Pitfalls to avoid if you consider a used purchase

The main pitfall is confusing a genuinely certified aircraft with a prototype, a demonstrator or a development project presented as imminent: only an actual type certificate, issued by a recognised authority, guarantees standard registration and insurability. The second pitfall is neglecting battery condition in favour of the usual checks alone (airframe, avionics): on an electric aircraft, the battery carries the same financial weight as the engine, and deserves at least as rigorous a check.

Tip — Before committing to anything, always request a pre-purchase inspection carried out by a centre approved by the aircraft's manufacturer, capable of specifically assessing battery and electrical system health, in addition to a conventional airframe inspection.

What to watch over the next few years

The certified electric aircraft market will mainly move on two factors worth watching: whether other manufacturers obtain new type certificates beyond the case already established, and how battery range actually improves, which is what would genuinely broaden use beyond initial training. Until both move meaningfully, the used market will stay narrow, concentrated around aircraft coming out of flying-school fleets.

For a buyer interested in this segment, the most reliable approach is to follow actual EASA certification news rather than manufacturer marketing announcements, and to compare any planned purchase against equivalent piston aircraft on total cost of ownership, battery included.
