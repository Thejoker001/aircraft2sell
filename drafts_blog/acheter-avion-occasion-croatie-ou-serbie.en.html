Buying a used aircraft in Croatia or Serbia: which country to choose

Market, prices, tax, registration and maintenance: a comparison between Croatia and Serbia to help you decide where to look for your used aircraft in the Balkans.

An EU member market facing a non-EU market

Croatia and Serbia share an aviation history rooted in the former Yugoslavia, an Adriatic coastline or close proximity to it, and a well-established light aviation tradition, but their administrative status diverged sharply after Croatia joined the European Union in 2013. Croatia now applies the EASA regulatory framework like any other member state, while Serbia, an EU candidate country, keeps its own national aviation certification system, separate from the European framework.

This difference in status has very concrete consequences at every stage of a cross-border purchase: recognition of airworthiness documents, applicable taxation, customs formalities and the registration procedure itself. It should not, however, discourage buyers from comparing both markets, as each has its own strengths for someone shopping for a used aircraft in the region.

Size and activity of the used market

Croatia's light aviation market benefits from an active network of flying clubs, particularly along the Adriatic coast and further inland, with a steady supply of touring single-engines (Cessna, Piper, Zlin) as well as microlights, supported by the growth of air tourism and flying schools. EU membership also makes it easier for already EASA-certified aircraft from other member states to reach the Croatian market.

The Serbian market remains active but smaller in volume, shaped by the legacy of Yugoslav aviation: locally designed trainer and aerobatic aircraft (Utva, Soko), gliders and a handful of Western aircraft imported over the decades. The overall fleet tends to be older on average, which also makes Serbia an interesting destination for enthusiasts of vintage or unusual types rarely found elsewhere in Europe.

Comparing prices

For the same common Western model, asking prices in Croatia generally remain comparable to those seen elsewhere in the European Union, with no particular discount linked to the country's geographic location. In Serbia, listed prices for equivalent Western aircraft can appear more attractive at first glance, but this gap is often offset, or even exceeded, by the import and compliance costs needed to operate the aircraft outside Serbia.

Before settling on a listed price in Serbia, it is essential to work out the full cost of putting the aircraft into service outside the country: transport, customs formalities, import taxation and any technical compliance work required.

Tax, customs and purchase-related costs

In Croatia, a sale between private individuals is generally not subject to VAT, while a sale by a professional may be subject to it under applicable Croatian and European rules. Croatia's membership of the EU single market also means an aircraft bought in another member state can usually move there without customs formalities, with intra-EU VAT rules applying between professionals.

Serbia sits outside the EU customs territory, so buying an aircraft there and then importing it into a member state involves a customs declaration, and potentially customs duties plus import VAT, calculated in the country of final destination according to that country's own rules. As the exact amounts depend on the declared value, the importing country and the applicable customs regime, it is advisable to use a customs broker or a professional experienced in aircraft imports before committing to a purchase.

Registration: the Croatian CCAA versus the Serbian CAD

In Croatia, aircraft registration is handled by the Croatian Civil Aviation Agency (CCAA), under the 9A- prefix. Once EASA-certified, the aircraft can generally be transferred to another member state under the common mutual-recognition procedures used across the Union.

In Serbia, the Civil Aviation Directorate of the Republic of Serbia (CAD) manages the national register, under the YU- prefix, following a certification framework of its own that is not automatically recognised by EASA. Importing an aircraft registered in Serbia into a European register therefore usually requires a thorough technical review of the airworthiness file, and sometimes an import certification process through an approved organisation, to establish equivalence with EASA requirements.

  • Complete, translated airworthiness file — airframe, engine and propeller logbooks, essential for any regulatory equivalence assessment.
  • Check the type-certification basis — a Western-designed aircraft is generally easier to import than a locally designed type with no recognised EASA equivalent.
  • Deregistration from the register of origin — required before any new registration in another country.
Good to know — The time and cost of a registration transfer between Serbia and an EU member state are generally higher than for a straightforward intra-European transfer, due to the extra regulatory-equivalence step involved.

Maintenance network and parts availability

Croatia has Part-145 or Part-CAO approved workshops spread between the coast and the interior, benefiting from easier parts supply for common Western models thanks to EU single-market access.

In Serbia, the maintenance network operates to national standards, with strong historical expertise on Yugoslav-origin aircraft, but access to parts can be more complex for recent Western models, relying more heavily on imports from Western Europe. For an older, locally designed aircraft, however, the expertise found in Serbian workshops is often better than anywhere else in Europe.

Which country to favour depending on your profile

For a buyer looking for a classic Western aircraft that can be easily registered in any member state without a complex import process, Croatia is the simpler choice, with the added benefit of a dynamic tourism market that supports future resale.

For a buyer drawn to Yugoslav-designed aircraft, gliders or historic aerobatic trainers, and willing to plan for non-EU import formalities, Serbia can offer interesting opportunities, provided customs and certification costs are carefully budgeted before the purchase, ideally with the help of an aircraft-import specialist from the negotiation stage onward.

Good to know — Whichever country you choose, a pre-purchase inspection by a qualified independent mechanic remains essential before committing, and matters even more for an aircraft whose technical file will later need to be assessed by a foreign certification authority.

Ultimately, the choice between Croatia and Serbia depends less on a raw price gap than on your tolerance for administrative procedures and the aircraft's intended future use. Taking the time to work out the full acquisition cost, including transport and compliance, remains the best way to avoid an unpleasant surprise after signing.
