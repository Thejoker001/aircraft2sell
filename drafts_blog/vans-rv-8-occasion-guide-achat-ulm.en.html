Used Vans RV-8: buying guide

Why the RV-8 appeals to aerobatic and recreational pilots

The Vans RV-8 is one of the most popular kit-built experimental aircraft in the world. Designed by Vans Aircraft, this tandem-seat, low-wing two-seater combines a riveted metal structure, a light airframe and crisp flying qualities that make it a favourite for recreational flying, two-up touring and a reasonable amount of light aerobatics. Its tandem seating, inherited from fighter aircraft layouts, gives it a distinct look and handling feel compared with a classic side-by-side two-seater.

On the used market, RV-8s are found in very different states of finish: some aircraft were built with exemplary care by experienced amateur builders, while others show signs of a rushed or less rigorous build. This variability, typical of kit-built aircraft, calls for extra diligence compared with buying a factory-built certified aircraft.

A kit-built aircraft: what it means for the buyer

The RV-8 is not a type-certificated aircraft produced on a production line: it was assembled by a private individual from a kit, then cleared to fly by the relevant authority under an experimental aircraft status, or in some countries under a microlight/light aircraft status. This status has real consequences for a buyer: there is no manufacturer airworthiness directive identical to that of a certified aircraft, and the quality of the aircraft depends almost entirely on the rigour of the original builder and the build records that have been kept.

Before any purchase, it is essential to request the complete build file: plans followed, kit serial number, assembly photos, and if available the initial inspection report issued by the authority or organisation that approved the first flight. An incomplete or missing build file should be treated as a strong warning sign, regardless of the aircraft's apparent condition.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used RV-8 depends above all on the quality of the original build, the age of the aircraft, total airframe and engine hours, engine status (remaining potential before overhaul), and the level of avionics fitted. A well-built example with a complete build file and consistent maintenance will trade noticeably above an aircraft with a murky history, even at similar flight hours.

Beyond the purchase price, your budget should include ancillary costs: an independent mechanic's pre-purchase inspection by someone familiar with experimental aircraft, registration and transfer fees, insurance specific to this type of aircraft, and possibly an avionics refresh if the aircraft needs one.

What to check before buying

As with any used aircraft, a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic qualified on amateur-built experimental aircraft is essential before finalising a transaction. For an RV-8, a few points deserve particular attention.

  • Quality of the riveting and metal structure: look for any signs of corrosion, poorly set rivets or previous repairs that are not well documented.
  • Complete engine history: hours elapsed since the last major overhaul (SMOH), compliance with the recommended maintenance program, traceability of replaced parts.
  • Consistency between the build file, the kit serial number, and the markings present on the airframe.
  • Condition and updates of the installed avionics, as well as compliance with current regulatory requirements (transponder, traffic-monitoring systems).
  • Complete logbook and technical file, with no grey areas in the aircraft's history since its first flight.
Good to know — On a kit-built aircraft, never rely solely on the seller's statements about the quality of the original build: a written independent inspection report, carried out by a mechanic experienced with experimental aircraft, remains the best protection against a bad surprise after the purchase.

Logbook and build records

The build file of an RV-8 is an integral part of both its value and its safety record. It should ideally include the plans followed, photos taken at each stage of assembly, invoices for components and the engine, and the final inspection report that led to the issuance of the airworthiness certificate (CofA) or the equivalent document depending on the country of registration.

The logbook kept since the aircraft's first flight should chronologically record every maintenance intervention, modification and any incidents. Any break in this chronology, or the absence of certain documents, should be clarified with the seller before moving forward with the transaction.

Maintenance and operating costs

The operating cost of an RV-8 remains generally moderate compared with a certified aircraft of similar performance, as structural parts and some components can be cheaper to replace on an experimental aircraft. The main expense items remain the mandatory annual inspection, insurance, parking and, in the longer term, engine overhaul once the hourly potential is reached.

Having the aircraft maintained by a mechanic experienced with amateur-built aircraft, or by the original builder if still available, helps limit unpleasant surprises and preserve its resale value. Rigorous, well-documented maintenance records are a strong argument at a future resale.

Tip — Always ask for the original builder's contact details if possible: they are often the best source of information on the aircraft's real quirks and its build history.

Resale and the used market

The RV-8 benefits from an active used market in Europe, supported by a large community of amateur builders and good parts availability from the kit manufacturer. A well-built aircraft, with a transparent build file and consistent maintenance, generally resells more easily and on better terms than one whose records have grey areas.

Comparing several listings of similar models remains the best way to assess a fair price, both for buying and for preparing a future resale. Price differences between two aircraft with similar hours are almost always explained by the quality of the original build and remaining engine potential.
