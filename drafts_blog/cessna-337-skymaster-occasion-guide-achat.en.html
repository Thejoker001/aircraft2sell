The Skymaster: a one-of-a-kind twin concept

Built by Cessna from 1963 into the early 1980s, the 337 Skymaster occupies a unique place in light-aircraft history. Unlike a conventional twin with one engine under each wing, the Skymaster pairs a tractor engine at the front of the fuselage with a pusher engine at the rear, both on the aircraft's centerline. This "push-pull" or centerline-thrust layout was designed to deliver the main benefit of twin-engine redundancy and safety without the biggest drawback of a conventional twin: asymmetric thrust following an engine failure.

The Skymaster saw notable military service under the designation O-2, used by the US armed forces for observation and forward air control missions, which greatly contributed to its reputation. In Europe, and in France in particular, the airframe was also built under license by Reims Aviation as the F337, which explains the sizeable fleet of French-built examples found on the used market across the continent.

Understanding the push-pull layout and its benefits

The main appeal of the Skymaster's tandem layout is the absence of a critical minimum control speed (Vmc) after an engine failure: thrust stays centered on the longitudinal axis regardless of which engine quits, which considerably simplifies single-engine handling compared with a conventional twin. That's a strong argument for pilots who want the safety of a twin without the workload and Vmc discipline a conventional twin demands.

This layout has a trade-off, however: the two engines do not share the same cooling conditions. The front engine is directly exposed to the incoming airflow, while the rear engine, buried in the fuselage, relies on a more complex induction and exhaust-air circuit for cooling. This thermal asymmetry is historically the most well-documented weak point of the Skymaster and should be understood before any purchase.

Good to know — The Skymaster's rear engine is statistically subjected to more thermal stress than the front engine. Many experienced owners recommend close monitoring of cylinder head temperatures (CHT) on this engine, especially during climb and in hot weather.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used Skymaster depends on the year of construction, the variant (normally aspirated or turbocharged, fixed or retractable gear), total airframe and engine hours, and the remaining potential before the next overhaul on each of the two engines. The Skymaster remains a niche market: pricing is generally more accessible than a conventional twin of comparable performance, making it an interesting option for a pilot looking for a twin-engine experience on a controlled budget — provided the specific operating and parts costs of this uncommon type are factored in.

On top of the purchase price come the usual costs for a used aircraft: a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic qualified on the type — ideally already familiar with the Skymaster, since its technical quirks can confuse a mechanic used to conventional twins —, registration and transfer fees, insurance, and any documentation compliance work if needed.

What to check before buying

Beyond the standard checks that apply to any used aircraft, the Skymaster has technical particularities that deserve extra attention during a pre-purchase inspection.

  • Rear engine cooling history: signs of past cylinder head temperature excursions, cowling repairs or replacements, and compliance with any cooling modifications installed by previous owners.
  • Consistency of remaining potential between the front and rear engines: it's not unusual for the two engines to have been overhauled at different times, which may mean one of them needs an overhaul soon.
  • Condition of the rear engine's propeller and shaft, access to which is more constrained for inspection and maintenance than a conventional installation.
  • Corrosion and structural condition of the tail booms and twin tail surfaces, specific to the Skymaster's twin-boom architecture.
  • Documented availability of type-specific parts (cowlings, cooling system components, retractable-gear parts), a sensitive point for an aircraft that has been out of production for decades.
Tip — Have the pre-purchase inspection carried out by a mechanic with proven Skymaster experience. The rear engine's cooling particularities and the twin-boom architecture are not always well understood by a shop that has never worked on the type.

The main Skymaster variants

The Skymaster came in several versions during its production run, with notable differences in performance and technical complexity.

  • 337 and Super Skymaster: normally aspirated, fixed-gear versions, the simplest and most affordable to maintain.
  • T337 (Turbo Skymaster): turbocharged engines offering better high-altitude performance, at the cost of more demanding engine maintenance.
  • 337G / Super Skymaster retractable: the most capable version, but also the most complex to maintain.
  • Reims F337: version built under license in France by Reims Aviation, well represented on the European market.
  • Riley Rocket and other engine conversions: third-party modifications fitting more powerful engines, to be assessed case by case based on the available conversion documentation.

Maintenance and operating costs

Operating a Skymaster means covering the costs of two engines — fuel, overhauls, consumable parts — which puts its operating budget above a comparable single-engine aircraft, but often below a conventional twin of equivalent power, since the Skymaster's two engines are generally less powerful individually. The main challenge remains the availability of parts specific to the push-pull architecture and the scarcity of shops genuinely experienced on the type, particularly in Europe.

A complete logbook, documenting each engine's history separately, is essential to properly assess an example. Rigorous follow-up by a shop familiar with the Skymaster, with traceable parts replacement on both engines, remains the strongest argument for a good resale down the line.

Tip — Before making an offer, ask for separate front and rear engine logbooks and the last three maintenance invoices: they often reveal a gap in potential or history between the two engines that should be factored into the negotiation.

The used market and resale

The Skymaster remains a niche aircraft sought after by a loyal community of owners, drawn to its twin-engine safety without a critical Vmc and its unusual architecture. Demand is narrower than for a well-known conventional twin, which can lengthen resale times, but well-maintained examples with a transparent engine history on both powerplants generally find a buyer without excessive difficulty.

Comparing several listings of Skymasters of comparable generation and hours remains the best way to assess a fair price. Price differences between two examples are almost always explained by the remaining potential on each of the two engines, the variant (normally aspirated, turbocharged, retractable gear) and the documented quality of rear engine cooling system maintenance.
