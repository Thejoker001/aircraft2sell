Checking an Aircraft's Accident/Incident History Before Buying

How to check an aircraft's accident and incident history before buying: official registers, investigation reports, logbooks, and red flags to watch for.

Why checking accident/incident history matters

A used aircraft that has been through an accident or a serious incident is not necessarily a bad buy — it all depends on the nature of the event and the quality of the repair that followed. What is unacceptable is buying without knowing. A gear-up landing properly repaired by an approved shop, documented and inspected, can be perfectly safe. A hidden structural damage, never disclosed, is a risk that can cost its new owner dearly and jeopardize their insurance coverage.

Accident/incident history also shapes resale value. An aircraft with a well-documented past and traceable repairs is negotiated differently from one whose past remains unclear, even if both are technically airworthy today. Checking this history before purchase protects both your safety and your investment.

Accident, serious incident, incident: the distinctions

Civil aviation authorities and independent safety investigation bodies (such as the NTSB in the United States, the AAIB in the United Kingdom, the BEA in France, or EASA at the European level) distinguish several categories of events, with severity thresholds that determine the level of investigation:

  • Accident — an event resulting in serious injury, death, or substantial damage compromising the aircraft's structure, performance, or flight characteristics.
  • Serious incident — circumstances indicating that an accident nearly occurred (near-collision, high-speed runway excursion, in-flight engine failure without a final adverse outcome...).
  • Incident — an occurrence associated with the operation of an aircraft which affects or could affect safety, without reaching the severity level of an accident.

This distinction matters for your research: accidents and serious incidents are almost always subject to a public investigation report, while minor incidents may only be recorded in an operator's or maintenance shop's internal records, with no official publication.

Registration and register: your starting point

The first step is to identify the aircraft precisely by its registration mark and, ideally, its manufacturer serial number. With these two identifiers, you can:

  • Check the aircraft registration record of the relevant country, which often lists ownership changes and sometimes administrative notes.
  • Look for the aircraft's prior registrations if it has changed country — an airframe resold multiple times across continents deserves extra attention to documentation continuity.
  • Cross-reference the serial number against public aviation safety event databases, which generally index events by registration and/or serial number.

Frequent re-registration or a change of country of registry is not inherently suspicious, but it complicates research: each jurisdiction has its own databases and publication timelines, and an event that occurred under a previous registration may be harder to trace under the current one.

Reading safety investigation reports

Most countries have an independent aviation safety investigation body, separate from the regulatory authority, tasked with establishing the causes of accidents and serious incidents (not with assigning legal liability). Their reports are generally public and free to consult:

  • Search by registration or serial number in the database of the investigation body for the country where the aircraft was operated at the time of the event — not only the seller's current country of residence.
  • Also check international databases that aggregate reports from several countries, useful if the aircraft's history spans multiple jurisdictions.
  • Read the full report, not just the summary: the identified causes, the damage found, and the recommendations give a precise picture of what was actually damaged and how it was addressed.
  • Check whether specific airworthiness directives were issued following the event, and whether they have been applied to this exact airframe.
Good to know — The absence of a public report does not guarantee the absence of an event. Some minor incidents are never made public, especially when they did not meet the mandatory investigation threshold. A clean database search complements your due diligence; it does not replace it.

Cross-checking the logbook and engine logbook

Official registers give you an external view. The aircraft logbook and the engine logbook, by contrast, are the internal record of the aircraft's life, kept by its successive operators. Reading them carefully often reveals what no public database will ever mention:

  • Look for unexplained gaps in the flight timeline — several months or years with no activity can correspond to downtime for a major repair.
  • Spot mentions of a major overhaul or an off-schedule airframe component replacement, which can indicate prior damage.
  • Check the consistency between the engine hours recorded in the logbook, those in the engine logbook, and those shown on the avionics if the aircraft is so equipped — a significant discrepancy deserves an explanation.
  • Compare the SMOH (hours since major overhaul) declared by the seller with the corresponding invoices and shop work orders.
  • Request the invoices and work orders tied to any structural repair mentioned, not just the logbook entry: the shop that performed the work, the parts replaced, the inspections carried out.

Red flags you should not ignore

Some elements, taken individually, prove nothing; taken together, they justify increased vigilance and, often, a thorough technical inspection before proceeding further with the transaction:

  • A seller who is reluctant or evasive about providing the full logbook, or who only offers a summary instead of the original documents.
  • Missing, torn out, or rewritten pages in the aircraft logbook or engine logbook.
  • An ownership history with several closely spaced resales, particularly after a change of country of registration that is hard to trace.
  • Vague references such as "minor repair" with no detail, invoice, or identifiable shop reference.
  • A price noticeably below market for an apparently comparable model and condition, with no coherent explanation.
Watch out — None of these flags alone should cause you to walk away automatically. But each one justifies a specific question to the seller, and a cross-check before continuing the negotiation.

Bringing in an independent expert

Document research has its limits: it reveals what has been disclosed or published, not what has been hidden. That is why checking accident/incident history should always be combined with a pre-purchase technical inspection carried out by a mechanic approved independently of the seller. Only a physical inspection can confirm whether structural repair work has taken place, documented or not.

An expert familiar with this type of case can also guide your document research: they know which databases are relevant for the aircraft's country of operation and can spot inconsistencies between the logbook and the physical condition found during the inspection.

Deciding with full information

A well-documented, consistent accident/incident history, with repairs traced and performed by approved shops, is not automatically a reason to walk away — for many older airframes, it is in fact the norm rather than the exception. What should make you walk away is inconsistency: missing documents, shifting explanations, refusal of transparency.

In every case, build an explicit clause into the purchase agreement making the transaction conditional on a satisfactory history check and a successful technical inspection, with the right to withdraw without penalty if the seller failed to disclose something material.

Tip — Keep a copy of all the research you carried out (screenshots of databases consulted, downloaded reports): this file is evidence of your due diligence, useful in case of a later dispute.
