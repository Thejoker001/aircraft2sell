Variable-pitch vs fixed-pitch propeller: maintenance and costs

Fixed-pitch or variable-pitch: two propeller technologies that change how simple a light aircraft is to operate, its performance, and its annual maintenance bill.

Two propeller philosophies for light aviation

On a piston-powered light aircraft, the propeller converts engine power into thrust. Two broad families share the used-aircraft market: the fixed-pitch propeller, whose blade angle is set once and for all, and the variable-pitch propeller (also called "constant-speed" in its most common form), whose blade angle adjusts in flight to optimize engine efficiency throughout each phase of flight.

This technical choice, often tied to the engine and the aircraft's model range, has direct consequences on purchase budget, the mechanical complexity on board, and the maintenance items to budget for every year. A typical trainer such as the DR400/120 or a base-model Cessna 172 flies with a fixed-pitch propeller, while higher-performance versions of the same family, or touring aircraft such as the TB20 Trinidad, use a variable-pitch propeller.

How a variable-pitch propeller works

On a constant-speed variable-pitch propeller, a hydraulic or hydro-mechanical governor continuously adjusts the blade angle to hold the engine rpm selected by the pilot, regardless of load or airspeed. On takeoff, the blades adopt a fine pitch to maximize thrust; in cruise, a coarse pitch lets the engine turn more slowly at the same airspeed, which reduces fuel burn and noise.

This mechanism adds a propeller control lever in the cockpit, a governor, pressurized oil lines and, often on twins, a feathering system. These are all extra mechanical components to monitor and maintain that simply don't exist on a fixed-pitch propeller.

The strengths of fixed-pitch: simplicity and entry cost

A fixed-pitch propeller is a block of metal or composite with no moving parts at all. No governor, no hydraulic lines, no propeller control lever to manage in flight: the mechanical simplicity is total, which translates into a lower purchase cost, reduced weight, and maintenance limited to visual inspection and checking the hub bolt torque.

  • No moving parts to maintain: no governor and no dedicated hydraulic system.
  • Simplified piloting procedure: a single throttle lever to manage, with no propeller control lever.
  • Lower weight, which slightly benefits the aircraft's useful load.
  • Much lower replacement or repair cost in the event of a strike or blade erosion.

The trade-off is a fixed efficiency compromise: the blade angle is set to work well across one rpm range, which penalizes either takeoff or cruise performance, depending on the setting chosen by the engine maker.

The strengths of variable-pitch: performance and flexibility

A variable-pitch propeller lets you run the engine in its optimal efficiency range at every phase of flight: a short takeoff with pitch set for maximum thrust, an economical cruise with reduced engine rpm at the same airspeed, and better engine thermal management thanks to fine rpm control. On a twin, the ability to feather a propeller after an engine failure considerably reduces drag and improves safety.

  • Shorter takeoff roll and stronger initial climb thanks to a pitch optimized for thrust.
  • More fuel-efficient cruise at a lower engine rpm for the same airspeed.
  • Feathering capability on twins, a major safety advantage in the event of an engine failure.
  • Better engine protection against overspeed during descents or approaches.

These performance gains come at a price: increased mechanical complexity, more thorough pilot training, and recurring maintenance costs noticeably higher than with a fixed-pitch propeller.

Maintenance compared: frequency and parts involved

On a fixed-pitch propeller, maintenance essentially comes down to visual inspection of the blades (erosion, impacts, cracks), checking the hub, and complying with any airworthiness directives specific to the model. Wood or composite blades may need closer monitoring of their protective coating.

On a variable-pitch propeller, maintenance adds regular inspection of the governor, checking the hydraulic lines for leaks, overhauling or exchanging the propeller control lever assembly at the scheduled hours, and tracking an overhaul interval specific to the propeller itself (generally independent of the engine's), recorded in a dedicated propeller logbook. Any strike while stationary (propeller hitting an object) also requires an enhanced inspection, potentially a teardown inspection of the reduction gearbox or crankshaft — at a cost with no equivalent to a simple blade replacement on a fixed-pitch propeller.

Word of caution — The propeller logbook must always be consistent with the engine logbook and the aircraft's flight logbook. Any inconsistency in hours or history is a warning sign when buying used.

Purchase and operating costs over time

At purchase, an aircraft fitted with a variable-pitch propeller generally sells for more than an otherwise similar fixed-pitch aircraft, due to the higher manufacturing cost and the often more powerful engine that goes with it. In operation, the gap persists: periodic overhaul of the governor and of the propeller itself, plus pricier spare parts in the event of a repair, weigh on the annual budget.

Which choice for which use

For club flying, initial training or occasional personal travel over short runways and local trips, the fixed-pitch propeller remains the most rational choice: controlled purchase and maintenance cost, simpler piloting, and reliability proven over millions of cumulative flight hours worldwide.

  • Fixed-pitch: perfectly suited to training, club flying and occasional short-to-medium-range travel.
  • Variable-pitch: relevant for regular long-distance travel, high-altitude airfields, or twins seeking redundancy.
  • Available annual maintenance budget: often the most decisive factor between the two technologies.
  • Pilot training: some insurers and instructors recommend a specific variable-pitch checkout before taking the controls.

Conversely, a pilot seeking better short-field or high-altitude takeoff performance, a faster or more economical cruise on long trips, or the added safety of feathering on a twin, will find the variable-pitch propeller a justified investment despite the higher running cost.

Points to watch when buying used

During the pre-purchase inspection of an aircraft with a variable-pitch propeller, always ask for the complete propeller logbook, the history of any declared strikes or incidents, and the date of the last governor overhaul. Also check the remaining time before the propeller's next major overhaul, which can fall due before the engine's depending on the model.

On a fixed-pitch aircraft, attention should focus mainly on blade condition (leading-edge erosion, impacts, delamination for wood or composite blades) and on the propeller's compatibility with the installed engine, as certain combinations require a specific propeller matched to the power delivered.

Good to know — Just like the engine, the propeller has its own overhaul interval and its own replacement deadline, which must be factored in separately when calculating the real cost of a used aircraft.

In all cases, have the inspection carried out by an independent mechanic familiar with the installed propeller type, whether fixed-pitch or variable-pitch, before committing to the purchase.
