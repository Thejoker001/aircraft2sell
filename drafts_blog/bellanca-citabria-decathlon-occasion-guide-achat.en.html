A much-loved family of tailwheel aircraft

The Bellanca Citabria and its development the Decathlon form a family of light tailwheel aircraft built around a fabric-covered tubular steel fuselage, direct descendants of the Champion Aircraft of the 1960s. Valued for their ruggedness, handling and ability to withstand intensive use, these aircraft occupy a particular place on the used market: they appeal both to pilots looking to learn tailwheel flying and to those considering aerobatics or glider towing.

Their relative mechanical simplicity, combined with an airframe known to be remarkably durable when properly maintained, explains the loyalty of a community of owners who continue to maintain and fly examples that are sometimes several decades old.

Citabria or Decathlon: what are the differences

The Citabria was originally designed as a versatile touring and training aircraft, offered with several engine options and, depending on the model, certified for light aerobatics. The Decathlon, derived from the Citabria, was specifically strengthened for aerobatics: a structure sized for higher load factors, a symmetrical wing on certain versions, and flight characteristics designed for aerobatic figures.

For a buyer, the choice between the two depends above all on the intended use: a Citabria will suit a pilot looking for a touring aircraft, tailwheel training, or occasional towing very well, while a Decathlon is more suited to a pilot who wants to practise aerobatics regularly, whether in a club or amateur competition.

Depending on the generation and the successive manufacturers (Bellanca, then American Champion), there are also variants with different engine power and fuel capacity, which directly affect the aircraft's performance and range. Before buying, it is therefore worth identifying the exact model, its engine and any approved modifications, since two aircraft bearing the same commercial name can have quite different characteristics.

Good to know — The two aircraft share much of their airframe and parts, which makes maintenance easier and keeps spare parts readily available on the used market.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used Citabria or Decathlon depends above all on the age of the aircraft, the engine hours remaining before the next major overhaul, the condition of the fabric covering and the level of avionics fitted. An older example needing a recover will trade at a noticeably more accessible entry level than a recently recovered or restored aircraft with modern avionics included.

Beyond the purchase price, your budget should include ancillary costs: an independent mechanic's pre-purchase inspection with a mechanic familiar with the type, registration fees, insurance (often specific for aerobatic or towing use), and possibly a recover to plan for in the years following the purchase.

What to check before buying

As with any used aircraft, a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic qualified on the type is essential before finalising a transaction. For a Citabria or Decathlon, a few points deserve particular attention because of the fabric-covered tubular construction.

  • Condition of the fabric covering: look for signs of corrosion, weakening or undocumented repairs under the fabric.
  • Condition of the tubular structure: check for internal corrosion of the tubes, particularly at attachment points and under the floor.
  • Complete engine history: engine hours since the last major overhaul (SMOH), compliance with the maintenance program.
  • For a Decathlon or Citabria used for aerobatics: check that load factor limits were respected and review the history of stresses the airframe has been subjected to.
  • Complete logbook and technical file, with no grey areas in the aircraft's history.
Good to know — Never rely solely on the seller's statements about the condition of the fabric covering: a written independent inspection report, including a check of the structure under the fabric, remains the best protection before buying.

Aerobatics, towing, tailwheel training: which uses

The versatility of this family of aircraft explains much of its success on the used market. The Citabria is regularly used for glider towing, thanks to its ruggedness and sufficient power for this intensive use, as well as for tailwheel training, a skill increasingly sought after by pilots used to flying tricycle-gear aircraft.

The Decathlon, for its part, remains the benchmark for pilots who want to discover or practise basic aerobatics safely, in an aircraft certified for these manoeuvres and with predictable handling. Some owners also use these aircraft simply for the enjoyment of visual flying, on short strips or in mountain terrain, where their manoeuvrability and short takeoff distance are a real asset.

A single aircraft can indeed combine several of these uses over its lifetime: a Citabria bought for towing may well become, a few years later, the family touring aircraft of a new owner, while a Decathlon used by an aerobatics club may later join the collection of an enthusiast who prefers leisurely flying. This versatility explains why used listings rarely mention a single use, and why it is important, when buying, to piece together the aircraft's real mission history rather than relying solely on the listing's marketing description.

Maintenance and operating costs

The operating cost of a Citabria or Decathlon remains moderate compared with more recent aircraft in the same category, thanks to simple mechanics and reasonable fuel consumption. The main long-term expense remains the fabric recover, needed periodically depending on the condition of the fabric and storage conditions, as well as engine overhaul once the hourly potential is reached.

Having the aircraft maintained at an approved shop familiar with the type and with fabric-covered tubular construction helps limit unpleasant surprises and preserve its resale value. Use in aerobatics or towing also requires more frequent structural checks, due to the additional stresses involved.

Tip — Always ask for the date of the last recover and the last three maintenance invoices before making an offer: they give an accurate picture of how seriously the aircraft has been maintained.

Resale and the used market

The Citabria and Decathlon benefit from an active, international used market, supported by a loyal community of owners and good parts availability, even for the oldest versions. A well-maintained aircraft, with a recent recover and a transparent history, generally resells more easily and on better terms than one whose technical file has grey areas.

Comparing several listings of similar models and model years remains the best way to assess a fair price, both for buying and for preparing a future resale. Price differences between two comparable aircraft are almost always explained by the condition of the fabric covering, remaining engine potential, and the version (Citabria or Decathlon) concerned.
