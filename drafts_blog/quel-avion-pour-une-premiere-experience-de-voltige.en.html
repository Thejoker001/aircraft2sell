Which aircraft for your first aerobatic flying experience

Intro flight, traditional biplane, modern composite monoplane, flying club or outright purchase: how to pick the right entry point into aerobatics based on your budget and your goal.

Define your goal before choosing an aircraft

Before asking which aircraft to fly for your first taste of aerobatics, you first need to clarify what you're really after. Feeling a strong sensation for the length of a single flight, discovering the basics of acrobatic flying over a handful of sessions, committing to a full course leading to a qualification, or eventually aiming at competition: each of these goals points to a different solution, both in terms of aircraft and the structure you fly with.

There is no single "right" aircraft for a first aerobatic experience. The choice depends above all on the level of commitment you want, your available budget, and whether a club or school offering supervised aerobatic activity is nearby. That reflection, not the aircraft model itself, should drive the first decision.

The intro flight: the best way to start

For a genuine first experience, an introductory flight (sometimes called an aerobatic discovery flight) in a dual-control aircraft with a qualified instructor remains by far the most sensible option. It lets you feel the load factors, the basic figures and the overall sensations of aerobatics without a heavy financial commitment or specific prerequisites, with the instructor handling or assisting the controls depending on the format offered.

This option is usually flown on a suitable two-seat aircraft belonging to a club affiliated with a national aviation federation, or to a dedicated aerobatic school. It is also a good way to test the waters before going further: some people discover they love the sensations, others realise aerobatics isn't for them — better to find out after an intro flight than after a much larger commitment.

The traditional biplane for progressive learning

If the taste for aerobatics is confirmed, initial training is very often carried out on a traditional tube-and-fabric biplane such as the Pitts Special, or on a sturdy monoplane such as the CAP 10. These aircraft are known for predictable handling and ruggedness: they forgive a beginner's imprecision far more readily than high-level competition machines.

A traditional biplane is also generally cheaper to buy and to run than a modern composite monoplane, which is why clubs often favour it: it needs to be amortised over many hours of instruction and leisure flying.

  • Predictable handling, suited to learning basic figures.
  • Generally more controlled running costs than a competition monoplane.
  • Commonly available at clubs, with instructors used to teaching beginners.

The modern composite monoplane: who is it for

Modern composite monoplanes such as the Extra or the CAP 231/232 offer roll rate and climb performance well above a traditional biplane's. They are built for high-level competition and suit pilots who already master the basic figures and want more precision and dynamism.

For a genuine first experience, this type of aircraft is generally not the best fit: its responsiveness can be disorienting for a beginner, and its running cost is noticeably higher. It becomes relevant once the basics are mastered, to progress towards more demanding figures or move towards competition.

Flying club or aerobatic school vs outright purchase

For a beginner, going through a club affiliated with an aviation federation or an aerobatic school remains clearly preferable to buying an aircraft outright. It gives access to qualified pedagogical supervision, an aircraft already maintained and insured for this specific use, and it spreads the cost of an aircraft that is demanding to maintain, instead of carrying the fixed costs of a dedicated aerobatic aircraft alone.

Buying an aerobatic aircraft outright, even used, makes sense for a pilot who is already qualified and wants to train regularly without depending on a club's availability, or who is aiming at competition with a dedicated aircraft. For a first experience, however, it is a financial and logistical commitment that is clearly premature.

What budget for each option

Budget varies enormously depending on the level of commitment chosen. A single intro flight is a one-off, modest expense. Initial training over a few hours at a club, with an instructor, means a recurring but manageable budget that can often be spread out depending on the club's and the aircraft's availability.

Buying an aircraft, biplane or monoplane, always comes with substantial additional costs: aerobatic-specific insurance, reinforced engine and airframe maintenance, and rigorous tracking of the G-meter logbook that records the load factors actually experienced flight after flight.

Qualification, supervision and safety

Aerobatic flying comes with specific regulatory requirements: a dedicated pilot qualification, an aircraft certified for the load factors flown, and minimum safety altitudes that vary with the level of practice and the national regulations in force. These precise requirements depend on the civil aviation authority and the competent federation, and should be checked directly with them rather than assumed.

Good to know — For a first experience, always favour a club or school affiliated with a recognised aviation federation, where pedagogical supervision and safety compliance are guaranteed by an organised structure rather than by an isolated initiative.

Beyond regulation, the teaching progression itself is a safeguard: starting with an intro flight, then supervised basic figures on a forgiving aircraft, before moving to more demanding machines, considerably reduces the risks linked to ramping up too fast.

Our recommendation by profile

If you have never flown aerobatics, always start with an intro flight at a club or dedicated school: it is the simplest, least committing way to find out whether this activity suits you. If the experience convinces you, continue with a few training hours on a traditional biplane or a sturdy monoplane such as the CAP 10, which offer the best pedagogical balance for learning the basics safely.

Only once the fundamental figures are mastered, and if your practice becomes regular, does it make sense to consider a higher-performance composite monoplane, or even buying your own aircraft. For that later step, our dedicated guide to buying a used aerobatic aircraft covers the market, reference brands and inspection points to watch.

One last piece of advice, whatever your profile: take the time to visit several clubs or schools before committing. Atmosphere, the instructor's teaching style and how well the fleet is maintained vary noticeably from one organisation to another, and these factors often matter more for a successful first experience than the exact aircraft type used. A serious club will usually steer you naturally towards the aircraft best suited to your level, without you needing to insist on a specific one from the first contact.
