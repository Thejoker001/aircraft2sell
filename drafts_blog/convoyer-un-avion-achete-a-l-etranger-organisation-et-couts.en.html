Ferrying an aircraft bought abroad: organisation and costs

Paperwork, aircraft preparation, choosing a ferry pilot, insurance and realistic cost ranges — everything to plan for when bringing home an aircraft bought outside your country of residence.

Why ferrying deserves its own planning

Buying an aircraft abroad doesn't end when the contract is signed. Between becoming the owner and the day the aircraft lands at its new home base, you still need to organise a flight — sometimes hundreds or thousands of kilometres — in an aircraft whose true history you may not fully know yet, often crossing several countries and regulatory regimes.

Ferrying is routinely underestimated by buyers who focus on the purchase price and the technical inspection, then discover at the last minute that they still need a pilot qualified on type, temporary insurance, an international flight plan and customs formalities to handle. It is far better to plan this during the negotiation, not after signing.

Your options for bringing the aircraft home

Three broad approaches exist, and the right one depends on distance, aircraft type and your own qualification.

  • Flying it yourself — possible if you hold the type qualification and recent experience required, and your licence is recognised in every airspace along the route. The cheapest option, but it means taking on alone the technical unknowns of an aircraft you have just acquired.
  • A professional ferry pilot — an experienced pilot on type, often freelance or affiliated with a specialised company, handles the flight end to end. This is the most common option for light aircraft bought abroad.
  • Road or container transport — reserved for aircraft that can be disassembled (foldable microlights, some gliders) or cases where flying is judged too risky (an aircraft not currently airworthy, a transoceanic distance). Costly and logistically heavy, but it avoids any flight in an aircraft of uncertain condition.

For most intra-European purchases of airworthy light aircraft, helicopters or turboprops, a ferry flight by a qualified pilot remains the simplest and fastest solution.

Preparing the aircraft and the paperwork

Before any ferry flight can be considered, the aircraft needs a valid certificate of airworthiness (or a specific permit to fly if it does not currently have one) and insurance covering that precise flight. You should also gather:

  • An up-to-date flight logbook and engine logbook, needed to justify the aircraft's condition if checked during a stopover.
  • Registration documents and the certificate of airworthiness, even while ownership transfer is still in progress.
  • A flight plan that accounts for restricted zones, airport slot requirements and the aircraft's real range — not just its theoretical one.
  • A list of diversion airfields along the route, with fuel and handling availability confirmed.

If the pre-purchase inspection flagged minor reservations, it is generally best to have them resolved before ferrying rather than flying with open questions on the aircraft.

Finding and briefing a ferry pilot

A good ferry pilot knows the aircraft type, is used to international flights and knows how to handle transit formalities. To find one, several channels complement each other: clubs and associations dedicated to the type of aircraft involved, professional ferry-pilot networks, and recommendations from a broker or an approved mechanic who has already worked on a comparable deal.

Before confirming the mission, always ask for the pilot's qualifications and recent experience on this exact aircraft type, references from comparable missions (distance, countries crossed), and clarify in writing — not just verbally — who bears liability in the event of an incident.

Word of caution — Do not hire a pilot recommended only by the seller without independently verifying their qualifications. The seller's interest in seeing the flight happen quickly is not always aligned with your interest in flying safely.

Insurance during the ferry flight

The previous owner's usual insurance does not cover you: once ownership transfers, it is up to you (or the pilot hired, depending on the contract) to hold valid cover for that specific flight. Specialist aviation insurers offer temporary ferry-flight policies, which typically cover third-party liability and, as an option, hull damage.

Before the flight, check that the geographic coverage matches every country on the route, that the named pilot on the policy is the one actually flying, and that the policy duration allows for a weather delay without a gap in cover.

Customs, VAT and airworthiness formalities

Within the European Union, an aircraft already in free circulation generally raises no particular customs issue during the ferry flight itself, but VAT on the sale transaction must be settled according to the rules of the country of purchase and the final country of registration — a point to confirm with a professional before signing, not after the flight. For a purchase outside the European Union, customs formalities and regulatory compliance (airworthiness directives, equipment, sometimes recertification) need to be anticipated well before departure.

The registration transfer itself follows a timetable specific to each national authority: in some cases you fly under the seller's country registration during the ferry flight and only change registration on arrival; in others, the transfer must be finalised before the first flight. Always confirm the exact procedure with the relevant civil aviation authority or a specialised agent.

Budgeting the ferry flight: the cost items

The total cost of a ferry flight varies enormously depending on distance, aircraft type and the weather encountered along the way. The main items to budget for are: the ferry pilot's fee (often a daily rate plus an hourly flying rate), fuel, airport and navigation charges (landing fees, air navigation taxes), accommodation for the pilot on multi-day flights, temporary ferry insurance, and a margin for weather delays that can extend the mission by several days.

It is better to think in broad ranges rather than a fixed figure: a short ferry flight between neighbouring countries for a light aircraft is of a much lower order of magnitude than a long-haul or intercontinental ferry flight for a twin or a turboprop, and using a broker or a specialised Part-145 company for coordination adds a service fee on top of the budget. Always ask for an itemised quote rather than an opaque lump sum.

Tip — Negotiate who pays for what before signing the purchase: some sellers agree to cover part of the ferry cost to speed up the sale, especially if the aircraft has been on the market for a while.

On the day: what can delay the flight

Weather remains the most common cause of delay for a ferry flight, particularly over long distances crossing several climate zones. Build several days of margin into your personal schedule rather than a fixed arrival date, and stay in regular contact with the pilot throughout the mission.

An unexpected administrative check during a stopover, a minor technical issue found at start-up, or a missed airport slot can also push the flight back by a day. These are normal hazards of an international ferry flight, not warning signs in themselves — unless they recur or mask a more serious technical problem, in which case the aircraft should be re-examined before continuing.
