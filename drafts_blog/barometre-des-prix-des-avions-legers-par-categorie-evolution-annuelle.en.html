Light Aircraft Price Barometer by Category: Annual Trends

Microlights, classic touring single-engines, high-performance single-engines, light twins: an overview of the price trends seen in each used-aircraft category, and the factors that really drive value.

Why track a price barometer by category

Unlike cars, there is no single official valuation guide for used light aircraft: prices are set by comparing similar listings, category by category. A barometer by category lets us identify general qualitative trends — a segment that is broadly rising, another that is broadly stable or softening slightly — rather than claiming a precise national figure, which would be meaningless in a market this fragmented and heterogeneous.

Following these trends by category is useful both for a buyer, who wants to know whether they are negotiating in a favourable or a tight market, and for a seller, who needs to set an opening price at the right level to avoid needlessly lengthening the time to sell.

This barometer is built category by category rather than country by country, since price gaps between two aircraft in the same segment are generally larger than the average gap between two neighbouring national markets within the EU. Watching a category's evolution over several months gives a more reliable picture than a single snapshot, which is inevitably skewed by a handful of outlier listings that can pull an instantaneous average in either direction.

Microlights and leisure light aircraft

The microlight and leisure light-aircraft segment is driven by steady demand from first-time entrants to aviation, for whom the entry cost is the key decision factor. Trends on this segment are broadly stable, with demand holding up for recent, well-equipped aircraft (modern avionics, fuel-efficient engines), while older or less well-equipped models see their prices stay contained, as there isn't enough demand to push them upward.

This segment remains the most sensitive to the overall purchasing power of households practising leisure aviation: an unfavourable economic shift typically shows up fairly quickly in transaction volume, more than in the price level itself.

Classic touring single-engines

Classic touring single-engines (Cessna 172, Piper PA-28 and equivalents) form the core of the used light aviation market in Europe by listing volume. The general trend on this segment is a slight rise for the best-maintained and most recent aircraft, driven by sustained demand from flight schools and private individuals, while older examples or those with limited engine potential before overhaul remain stable, or even drift down if they accumulate years without an avionics upgrade.

Key point — On this segment, the price gap between two aircraft of the same model and the same year can exceed 30% depending on engine and avionics condition: build year alone explains only a small part of the final price.

High-performance single-engines

The high-performance single-engine segment (Cirrus SR20/SR22, Diamond DA40/DA42, TBM for single-engine turboprops) shows a broadly upward trend for recent, well-equipped examples, driven by sustained demand from owner-pilots looking for a fast, modern aircraft for personal or business travel. Older aircraft in this segment remain broadly stable, with the value gap versus recent versions (glass cockpit, modern safety systems) tending to hold rather than narrow.

The limited availability of recent, well-maintained aircraft in this segment helps support price levels, particularly for the models most in demand on the European market.

Light twin-engines

The light twin-engine segment shows a broadly stable trend, with narrower demand than single-engines, concentrated on professional use or multi-engine training. A twin's higher operating cost (double engine maintenance, fuel burn, insurance) weighs on demand and limits upward price movement, except for the most recent and best-equipped aircraft, which continue to find buyers quickly.

Tip — On this segment, annual operating cost should always factor into the buying decision as much as the purchase price: two aircraft listed at the same price can have very different ownership costs depending on the condition of their two engines.

The factors that matter more than build year

Whatever the category, the general price trends described above remain secondary to factors specific to each aircraft, which explain most of the price gap seen between two examples of the same model:

  • Engine hours remaining before major overhaul — an engine close to its time limit weighs heavily on price, whatever the age of the airframe.
  • Consistency and completeness of the maintenance logbook — a clear, gap-free history reassures buyers and supports price; an incomplete file justifies a discount.
  • Avionics equipment level — a recent glass cockpit or a recent radio/transponder upgrade meaningfully boosts the value of an older aircraft.
  • Storage conditions and climate — an aircraft hangared in an inland climate generally negotiates better than an equivalent left outdoors in a coastal or humid climate.

How to track and interpret price trends

The most reliable way to track price trends in a given category is to regularly compare several listings of similar aircraft (same model, comparable engine potential, similar equipment) over a period of several months, rather than relying on a single isolated figure or an overly general average that would mix non-comparable aircraft.

It is also useful to distinguish the asking price from the price actually agreed at sale: in a market where negotiation is the norm, the gap between the two can represent several percentage points and vary with how tight the market is for each category at a given time.

Advice for buyers and sellers

For a buyer, following these general trends helps gauge whether a given segment is relatively favourable for negotiation or, on the contrary, tightened by sustained demand that leaves little room to manoeuvre. For a seller, it helps set a realistic opening price, taking into account both the general trend of their category and the specific characteristics of their own aircraft (engine hours, avionics, maintenance history).

Time-to-sell is a useful complementary indicator alongside the price level itself: a category where listings sell quickly generally signals sustained demand, while a gradually lengthening average time-to-sell on a given segment often precedes a downward price adjustment, sometimes before that shows up in asking prices.
