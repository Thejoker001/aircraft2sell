Instrument Rating (IR/IFR): Training, Cost and Career Prospects

What is the IR/IFR qualification

The Instrument Rating, commonly known as the IR, is an additional qualification added to an existing pilot licence (private, commercial, or airline). It authorises its holder to operate an aircraft under Instrument Flight Rules, or IFR, meaning flying and navigating primarily by reference to the aircraft's instruments rather than relying on continuous outside visual reference.

Under the EASA regulatory framework, this qualification follows two main pathways: the classic full IR(A), the longer-standing complete programme, and the CB-IR(A) (Competency-Based Instrument Rating), a modular route introduced to make the qualification more accessible to private pilots, with a reduced volume of theoretical and practical training compared with the full course.

Why fly on instruments: the limits of VFR flying

A pilot who holds only a basic licence flies under Visual Flight Rules (VFR), which impose minimum weather conditions (visibility, cloud base) below which flight is not permitted. These constraints significantly limit how reliably a touring aircraft can be used, especially in autumn and winter across much of Europe, where low, persistent cloud layers make VFR flight impossible for large stretches of time.

The IR/IFR qualification removes much of this limitation: it allows a pilot to climb through a cloud layer, navigate in reduced visibility, and use instrument approach procedures to land at an equipped airfield even under a low ceiling. For an owner or prospective buyer of a touring aircraft, this is often the qualification that turns a "weekend hobby" aeroplane into a genuinely reliable means of getting from A to B.

Good to know — Flying IFR requires not only the pilot's qualification but also an aircraft certified and equipped for that kind of flight (avionics, navigation systems, sometimes de-icing). Always confirm the aircraft's actual IFR certification, not just whether it could theoretically be equipped for it.

Prerequisites before enrolling

Before enrolling in IR/IFR training, a pilot must hold a valid pilot licence (at least a private pilot licence for the CB-IR route) and an appropriate medical certificate. Both EASA pathways also require a minimum amount of cross-country flight experience as pilot in command before the practical exam, which is why most private pilots first build up a few dozen flight hours after earning their licence before considering the IR.

  • A valid pilot licence (PPL, CPL or ATPL depending on the career plan).
  • A medical certificate of the class required for the intended type of operation.
  • A minimum amount of cross-country flight experience as pilot in command, required before the practical exam.
  • For the full IR(A) route, a larger theoretical workload than the CB-IR, to plan into your schedule.

Training path: full IR or CB-IR

The full IR(A) route includes a substantial theoretical programme covering seven subjects (regulation, human performance, meteorology, communications, instrumentation, navigation and principles of flight), followed by a significant block of instrument flying hours, split between a certified flight simulator (FNPT or full-flight simulator) and real aircraft flight. This route is aimed primarily at pilots pursuing a professional career (CPL, ATPL).

The more recent CB-IR(A) route significantly reduces the required theoretical and practical volume, with the possibility of crediting some instrument flying hours already accumulated during earlier training. The minimum real flight hours required can be reduced for a pilot with prior instrument experience, with the balance completed in a simulator. Both routes conclude with a written theory exam and a practical skill test with an authorised examiner.

Tip — Always ask the training organisation (ATO) for the exact breakdown between theory, simulator and real flight hours: this varies noticeably between schools, as do the conditions for crediting prior experience.

The cost of an IR/IFR qualification

The cost of IR/IFR training varies significantly depending on the route chosen, the training organisation, the country, and the candidate's prior experience (which can reduce the number of billable hours). The main cost items are flight hours on an IFR-equipped aircraft, simulator hours, the theory course and exam fees.

CB-IR(A) (competency-based, reduced volume)€8,000–€15,000

Point of caution — These figures are general ranges and vary considerably depending on the school, the country, the aircraft type used for training and any credit for prior experience. Always request a detailed, up-to-date quote from your chosen ATO rather than relying on a generic estimate.

On top of the initial training cost, factor in the cost of renting an aircraft if you do not yet own an IFR-equipped one, as well as the recurring cost of staying current once the rating has been obtained.

Validity and keeping the rating current

The IR is not a one-time qualification: it must be renewed periodically through a proficiency check with a qualified instructor or examiner, at an interval set by the EASA regulations in force at the time of renewal. A pilot who allows the rating to lapse without renewing it on time may have to repeat part of the training requirements to regain it, depending on how long the lapse has been and the rules in effect.

Good to know — The exact rules on IR validity and renewal are updated from time to time: always check current regulations with your ATO or the relevant civil aviation authority rather than relying on older information.

Career prospects: private flying and professional aviation

For a private pilot, the IR/IFR qualification is primarily a gain in reliability and safety: it allows planned trips to go ahead despite poor weather, lets you climb legally through cloud layers, and lets you land at airfields equipped with instrument approach procedures, which considerably extends the practical range of a touring aircraft.

On the professional side, the IR is an almost unavoidable step on the way to a commercial pilot licence (CPL) and then an airline transport pilot licence (ATPL): no airline or commercial operator considers hiring a pilot without this qualification, since the vast majority of commercial air transport is conducted under IFR. The IR also opens the door to activities such as specialised aerial work, instrument flight instruction, or flying for air taxi and business aviation operators, where schedule reliability is a strong commercial requirement.

  • Private flying — greater trip reliability and access to equipped airfields in low visibility.
  • Professional career — an almost universal prerequisite for progressing toward the CPL or ATPL.
  • Business aviation and air taxi — an essential qualification for meeting clients' reliability expectations.

What it means for buying an IFR-equipped used aircraft

For a pilot planning to buy a used aircraft to make use of an IR/IFR qualification, it is essential to confirm that the aircraft in question actually holds the certification and equipment required for IFR flight (navigation avionics, redundant systems depending on the intended level of operation): not every touring aircraft is certified for this kind of flying, even when it appears to be fitted with modern instruments.

The maintenance logbook and avionics history deserve particular attention during the pre-purchase inspection of an aircraft intended for regular IFR use, since navigation avionics is often a significant cost item in the event of a failure or a required update. Budgeting for the cost of IR training, the aircraft itself and its IFR equipment together from the outset helps avoid unpleasant surprises after purchase.

Tip — If your purchase is driven by an upcoming IR qualification, ask the seller for the full details of the aircraft's certification and avionics updates before committing, and have these confirmed by an independent mechanic during the pre-purchase inspection.
