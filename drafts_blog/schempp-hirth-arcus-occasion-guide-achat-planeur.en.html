Overview: a high-performance two-seater

The Schempp-Hirth Arcus is a high-performance two-seat glider designed for cross-country soaring and advanced training. Its composite airframe and generous wingspan give it strong glide performance, which has made it popular both with clubs looking to train pilots in performance flying and with private owners who want to fly two-up, whether for pleasure, instruction, or competition preparation.

Since its launch, the Arcus has been produced in several variants, including self-launching and sustainer versions capable of taking off or regaining altitude without a tow plane or winch, which broadens its usable envelope considerably compared with a pure glider that depends entirely on ground launch methods.

T, M and E versions: how to tell them apart

Three main families of the type circulate on the used market. The simplest version is built purely for aerotow or winch launch, with no onboard engine. Other versions carry a sustainer or self-launch engine that allows the aircraft to take off under its own power or to climb back to altitude without relying on a tow plane. An electrically powered variant also exists, offering a different approach to range and propulsion-system maintenance.

Before buying, it is essential to identify the exact variant from the serial number and technical documentation: the type of propulsion fitted (or its absence) has a strong influence on price, the maintenance to plan for, and the kind of flying the aircraft is suited to.

Good to know — A self-launching version frees the owner from depending on a tow plane or winch being available, but it brings specific maintenance on the propulsion system that must be budgeted into the overall cost of ownership.

What budget to plan for a used Arcus

The price of a used Arcus varies significantly depending on the version (with or without an onboard engine), the year of construction, total flight hours and cycles, and the general condition of the composite airframe and canopy. Self-launching and sustainer versions generally command a higher price than pure glider versions, reflecting the cost and complexity of the onboard propulsion system.

On top of the purchase price, budget for the pre-purchase inspection, transport and rigging of the aircraft, insurance, and club or gliding association membership if the aircraft will be operated within that kind of structure rather than as straightforward private ownership.

What to check before buying

As with any used glider, a pre-purchase inspection carried out by a mechanic qualified on the type is essential before closing a deal. For an Arcus, a handful of points deserve particular attention.

  • Condition of the composite airframe: look for impact damage, delamination or prior repairs, especially on the wings and fuselage.
  • Condition of the canopy: cracks, deep scratches or sealing defects that could require costly replacement.
  • For powered versions, the propulsion system's history and condition, including operating hours and full traceability of work performed.
  • Condition of the flight controls, airbrakes and retractable landing gear, if fitted.
  • Complete, consistent maintenance and flight logbooks, with no gaps in the record of periodic inspections.
Good to know — Never rely solely on the seller's description of the aircraft's condition: a written independent inspection report remains the best protection against an unpleasant surprise after purchase.

The self-launch system: maintenance and care

For versions fitted with a sustainer or self-launch engine, the propulsion system is a maintenance item in its own right, distinct from the upkeep of a pure glider. It is worth checking the proper operation of the extension/retraction mechanism, the overall condition of the powerplant (or the electrical system on an electric variant), and the traceability of the specific maintenance work carried out on this system.

This maintenance item should be factored into the overall cost of ownership, since it adds to the regular airframe inspections and should never be overlooked when negotiating the purchase price.

Maintenance and periodic inspections

Routine maintenance of an Arcus follows the periodic inspection programme defined by the manufacturer, including regular checks of the airframe and equipment. Having this work carried out by an approved workshop experienced with the type helps avoid unpleasant surprises and preserves the aircraft's resale value.

A rigorous, well-documented record of these periodic inspections, kept in an up-to-date maintenance logbook, is a strong selling point at resale time.

Licence and ratings required

Flying two-up in an Arcus requires a valid glider pilot licence, together with any additional ratings that may be required depending on the intended use (dual flight, instruction, or flying a self-launching version, which may call for specific familiarisation with the propulsion system).

It is advisable to check with your club or gliding association on the exact requirements that apply, particularly for a first checkout on a version equipped with a sustainer or self-launch engine.

Resale and the used market

The Arcus benefits from an active used market in Europe, driven by its popularity with gliding clubs and private owners looking for a capable two-seater. A well-maintained aircraft with a transparent history and a complete maintenance logbook generally resells more easily than an example whose technical file has gaps.

Comparing several listings of similar versions remains the best way to judge a fair price, both for buying and for preparing a future resale. Price differences between two aircraft of the same vintage are most often explained by the presence or absence of onboard propulsion and the quality of the documented maintenance.
