Why the Jodel D112/D119 remains a sought-after classic

The Jodel D112 and its derivative, the D119, are among the most iconic French light aircraft of the post-war era. Designed by Édouard Joly and Jean Délémontez in the 1950s, these low-wing two-seaters won fans with their simple construction, pleasant handling and low operating cost. Several hundred were built in France, and a large number of them still fly today thanks to a community of owners and specialist workshops that keep them airworthy.

On the used market, the D112 and D119 attract a varied audience: private pilots looking for an economical aircraft for leisure flying, vintage aviation enthusiasts drawn to traditional construction methods, and flying club members looking for a machine that is simple to maintain in-house. Their distinctive low-wing silhouette and reputation for ruggedness give them strong sentimental value on top of their practical appeal.

D112 vs D119: what are the differences

The D119 is a direct evolution of the D112, with a slightly lengthened fuselage offering more space and comfort for the occupants, as well as a somewhat higher payload. The airframe is built on the same principles as the D112: a wooden structure (mostly spruce), fabric covering and plywood for the fuselage and wings, which calls for inspection and maintenance methods very different from those of a conventional metal aircraft.

In practice, the two models fly in a very similar way and broadly share the same range of engines. The choice between the two mostly comes down to availability on the market and the condition of the particular example on offer: it is far more important to favour an aircraft with a clear history and consistent maintenance than to insist on one version over the other.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used D112 or D119 varies significantly depending on the condition of the wood-and-fabric airframe, the age of the fabric covering, remaining engine potential and the quality of the finish. An aircraft that needs fabric re-covering or structural work will trade well below a recently restored, freshly re-covered example, whose price can get close to that of an equivalent light metal aircraft.

On top of the purchase price, budget for the usual ancillary costs: a pre-purchase inspection by a mechanic familiar with wood-and-fabric structures, registration fees, insurance, and possibly a provision for a partial or full fabric re-cover in the medium term if it is approaching its age limit.

What to check before buying

A pre-purchase inspection of a Jodel D112/D119 requires specific expertise related to its wood-and-fabric construction, different from that of a metal or composite aircraft.

  • Condition of the fabric covering: look for signs of ageing, cracking or loss of tension, and check the date of the last full re-cover.
  • Wood structure of the fuselage and wings: signs of moisture damage, rot or fungal attack, particularly around spars and joints.
  • Complete logbook and technical file, with traceability of structural repairs and fabric covering work carried out over the years.
  • Engine history: hours elapsed since the last major overhaul, compliance with the maintenance program, and traceability of replaced parts.
  • Validity of the certificate of airworthiness (CofA) and compliance with any airworthiness directives applicable to the type.
Good to know — A wood-and-fabric aircraft needs a mechanic experienced with this type of construction for the pre-purchase inspection: structural issues are not always detected the same way as on a metal aircraft.

Engine and engine hours

Jodel D112 and D119 aircraft have been built and modified with different engines over the decades, most often low-power flat-four engines that are well known in the light aviation market and whose parts generally remain available. Tracking engine hours since the last major overhaul is a key element of the negotiation, as with any older light aircraft.

Before buying, it is advisable to ask for the full engine history, including any engine changes made since the original build, along with the paperwork proving their regulatory compliance.

Maintenance and operating costs

Routine maintenance of a Jodel D112/D119 remains broadly affordable compared with more modern aircraft, but it requires specific skills for anything involving the wood structure and fabric covering. Many owners rely on workshops that specialise in vintage aircraft, which can be scarce in some regions and may extend turnaround times.

The main expense items remain the annual inspection, insurance, parking and, in the medium term, a possible partial or full re-cover of the fabric depending on its age and condition. Rigorous maintenance, well documented in the logbook, remains the best argument for a smooth future resale.

Tip — Joining a type club or an owners' network for Jodel aircraft is often the fastest way to find a competent workshop and exchange best practices specific to this type of structure.

Resale and the used market

The used market for the D112 and D119 remains a niche, driven more by a community of enthusiasts than by a transaction volume comparable to that of more recently produced metal aircraft. Prices nonetheless hold up well over time for well-maintained examples with recent fabric covering and a transparent history, since this type of aircraft remains sought after for its charm and low operating cost.

To properly assess an aircraft before buying or selling, comparing several D112 and D119 listings available at the same time remains the best approach. The condition of the fabric and wood generally weighs much more heavily in the final negotiation than the aircraft's raw age or total airframe hours.
