Used Vans RV-9: buying guide

Why the RV-9 appeals to amateur builders

The Vans RV-9 belongs to the famous family of homebuilt kit aircraft designed by the American manufacturer Vans Aircraft, whose RV-7 and RV-8 are the best-known siblings. Unlike those two, which are designed for aerobatics and outright speed, the RV-9 was conceived as a cross-country touring and leisure aircraft, with a more efficient wing, a lower stall speed and a comfortable side-by-side cabin for two occupants.

That focus has made it a favourite among pilots looking for a fast, economical aircraft to operate, without sacrificing easy handling or cruise performance over long distances. On the used market, the RV-9 benefits from a solid installed fleet in both Europe and the United States, which makes finding parts and tapping into owner experience much easier.

RV-9 vs RV-9A: what's the difference

The RV-9 is available in two landing gear configurations: the RV-9, with conventional tailwheel gear, and the RV-9A, with tricycle gear and a nosewheel. The RV-9A is by far the more common choice on the used market, since tricycle gear makes learning and crosswind landings easier, while the tailwheel RV-9 requires specific training and more experience.

Beyond the landing gear, both versions share the same airframe and flight performance. The choice between the two mainly depends on the pilot's experience and personal preference, with the RV-9A remaining the simpler option for a first purchase within the Vans family.

What budget to plan for at purchase

As with any homebuilt aircraft, the price of a used RV-9 varies widely depending on the quality of the build, the level of avionics fitted, remaining engine hours and the condition of the installed powerplant. A well-maintained example with a factory-fresh Lycoming engine and modern digital avionics will command a noticeably higher price than an older airframe fitted with classic analogue instruments.

On top of the purchase price, budget for the usual ancillary costs: a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic familiar with homebuilt aircraft, registration fees, insurance and, where needed, an avionics upgrade.

What to check before buying

A homebuilt aircraft deserves particular attention during the pre-purchase inspection, since build quality depends directly on the care taken by the original builder rather than on a standardised factory production line.

  • Overall quality of structural assembly: rivet work, control surface alignment, finish of spars and ribs, visible during a thorough airframe inspection.
  • Complete engine history: engine hours since new or since the last major overhaul (SMOH), traceability of replaced parts and compliance with the engine manufacturer's maintenance program.
  • Consistency between the builder's log and the aircraft's actual condition: build photos, completed milestones, initial airworthiness inspections.
  • Condition and currency of the installed avionics, as well as compliance with the regulatory requirements applicable to this type of aircraft.
  • Complete flight logbook and maintenance logbook, with no grey areas or unexplained gaps in the flight history.
Good to know — For a homebuilt aircraft, favour a mechanic or inspector with specific experience of Vans kits: several inspection points differ noticeably from those of a factory-built aircraft.

Builder's log and traceability

The value and reliability of a used RV-9 rest largely on the quality of the builder's log kept by the successive builder(s). This log should ideally include photos taken at each stage of assembly, the serial numbers of installed components, and the reports of the inspections carried out before the aircraft's first flight.

A complete, well-kept log reassures a buyer considerably, since it allows critical structural points (spars, flight controls, engine mounts) to be checked against how the aircraft was actually assembled. Conversely, an incomplete or missing log should prompt caution and a more thorough technical inspection before making any offer.

Maintenance and operating costs

The operating cost of an RV-9 remains generally lower than that of a comparable factory-built aircraft, thanks to a lightweight airframe, an engine often shared with other common types, and well-controlled fuel consumption. The main expense items remain the mandatory annual inspection, insurance, parking and, longer term, a major engine overhaul once the hourly potential is reached.

Having the aircraft maintained by a mechanic familiar with homebuilt types helps preserve its resale value and avoid unpleasant surprises linked to non-standard build solutions sometimes adopted by the original builder.

Tip — Always ask for the contact details of the previous builder(s) before buying: they can answer specific questions about build choices that aren't always captured in the written log.

Resale and the used market

The RV-9 benefits from an active used market, supported by the overall popularity of the Vans Aircraft range and good availability of parts from the manufacturer. A well-built aircraft, with a complete technical log and consistent maintenance, generally resells more easily and on better terms than one whose build history has grey areas.

Comparing several listings of RV-9 and RV-9A aircraft of comparable build quality remains the best way to assess a fair price, both for buying and for preparing a future resale.
