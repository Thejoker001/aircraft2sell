Why the Discus 2 became a benchmark in the 18-Metre Class

The Schempp-Hirth Discus 2 is the successor to the original Discus, designed by the German manufacturer Schempp-Hirth Flugzeugbau to meet the evolving demands of the 18-Metre Class while remaining eligible, thanks to interchangeable wing-tip extensions, for a configuration close to Standard Class. That dual positioning has made it a highly sought-after single-seat glider for pilots who want one aircraft able to cover several competition and performance-flying categories.

Its reputation rests on a high glide ratio for its category, predictable handling and good forgiveness of pilot error, which has made it a popular choice both in clubs and for regional or national competition flying. Its large fleet across Europe has also built a solid base of pilots and mechanics familiar with the type, which makes maintenance and shared experience between owners easier.

On the used market, the Discus 2 comes in several successive versions, with gradual developments in the wing, cockpit layout and, for certain versions, the addition of a self-launching engine. This diversity means a fairly wide range of listings across Europe, but also a broad price range depending on version, age, airframe hours and the condition of the composite gelcoat.

Discus 2a, 2b, 2c and 2cT: what are the differences

The Discus 2a, the first in the line, carried forward the general philosophy of the original Discus while adapting it to 18-Metre Class requirements, with a fully composite airframe and wing-tip extensions allowing it to also fly in a configuration close to Standard Class. It's the oldest version available on the used market, typically trading at a more accessible entry price.

The Discus 2b introduced developments to the cockpit layout and certain aerodynamic details, while keeping the aircraft's overall philosophy intact. It's one of the most widely available versions on the European used market today, offering a good balance between availability and relatively modern equipment.

The Discus 2c brought further refinements to the wing, aimed at further improving the glide ratio and handling in weak thermal conditions, while remaining in the spirit of a glider accessible for club flying and regional or national competition.

The Discus 2cT adds a retractable self-launching engine to this base, allowing an independent takeoff or a climb back to altitude after an outlanding, at the cost of a higher empty weight and specific maintenance tied to the engine and its retraction system.

Before targeting one version or another, it's worth clearly defining the intended priority use — occasional club flying, regular cross-country flying, competition, or independence from a tow plane — since the gap in budget, airframe age and equipment between versions remains significant.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used Discus 2 depends above all on the version (2a, 2b, 2c or 2cT), the age and total airframe hours, the condition of the composite gelcoat, and the quality of the fitted instruments (electronic variometer, flight computer, competition-approved GPS logger). The oldest examples, with heavy club use, trade at a noticeably more accessible entry level than recent, well-equipped 2cT examples owned by a private individual.

Beyond the purchase price, your budget should include ancillary costs: an independent mechanic's pre-purchase inspection, registration and transfer fees, insurance, as well as the transport trailer and a hangar or tie-down spot depending on how you plan to operate the glider.

What to check before buying

As with any used glider, a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic qualified on the type is essential before finalising a transaction. For a Discus 2, a few points deserve particular attention.

  • Condition of the composite gelcoat: look for any impacts, delamination or previous repairs, particularly on the leading edges and forward fuselage, the areas most exposed to ground handling and trailer transport.
  • Total airframe hours and a complete logbook, with the history of periodic inspections and no chronological gaps.
  • Operation of the airbrakes and play in the flight controls, which see repeated use in performance flying.
  • Condition of the landing gear and of the wing-tip extensions (fittings, hinge wear) if the glider is used in both 15 and 18-metre configurations as needed.
  • Past storage conditions (hangar or trailer): an example regularly stored under cover keeps its composite gelcoat in noticeably better condition over time.
  • Presence and condition of the transport trailer, often sold with the glider, whose value and condition deserve to be assessed separately.
Good to know — Never rely solely on the seller's statements about overall condition: a written independent inspection report, ideally carried out by a shop approved on the type, remains the best protection against a bad surprise after purchase, especially for a performance single-seater that may have seen competition use.

The self-launching Discus 2cT

The Discus 2cT stands out for its retractable self-launching engine, which lets the pilot take off independently or climb back to altitude after an outlanding away from the home airfield. This option appeals to pilots flying cross-country over terrain with few alternate airfields, but it calls for a specific check of the engine, its retraction system and its operating conditions during the pre-purchase inspection.

Before buying a Discus 2cT, it's worth asking the seller for the complete history of work done on the powerplant and its retraction system, as well as the number of hours the engine has run, so you can anticipate the maintenance costs specific to this version compared with a non-powered Discus 2.

Maintenance and operating costs

The operating cost of a Discus 2 remains far lower than that of a powered aircraft of comparable size, which explains part of its appeal to private pilots and clubs alike. The main expense items remain the periodic airworthiness inspection, insurance, storage (hangar or trailer) and routine upkeep of the gelcoat and flight controls. For a Discus 2cT, an additional expense tied to the self-launching engine needs to be anticipated.

Having the glider maintained at an approved shop familiar with the type and with composite materials helps limit unpleasant surprises and preserve its resale value. Rigorous, well-documented maintenance records are a strong argument at a future resale, especially for a glider that may have seen regional or national competition use, where several pilots may have flown it over the seasons.

Tip — Always ask for the complete logbook and the latest periodic inspection reports before making an offer: they give an accurate picture of how seriously the glider has been maintained, especially for an example that may have seen competition use.

Resale and the used market

The Discus 2 benefits from an active used market across Europe, supported by a very large installed fleet among gliding pilots and good parts availability through the manufacturer's network and its approved shops. A well-maintained glider, with a transparent history and a gelcoat in good condition, generally resells more easily and on better terms than one whose technical file has grey areas.

Comparing several listings of similar models remains the best way to assess a fair price, both for buying and for preparing a future resale. Price differences between two examples of the same model year are almost always explained by airframe hours, gelcoat condition and documented maintenance quality, as well as by the version (2a, 2b, 2c or 2cT) concerned.
