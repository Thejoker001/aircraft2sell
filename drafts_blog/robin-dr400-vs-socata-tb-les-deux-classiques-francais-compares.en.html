Robin DR400 vs Socata TB: the two French classics compared

Fabric-covered wood or all-metal, sporty cabin or comfort cabin: a complete comparison of the Robin DR400 and the Socata TB to help you choose based on budget, use and maintenance constraints.

Two iconic French trainers

The Robin DR400 and the Socata TB (Tampico, Tobago, Trinidad) are two of the most iconic single-engine aircraft in French light aviation, found in large numbers in flying clubs and among private owners since the 1970s-80s. Both have trained generations of pilots in France and remain safe bets on the European used market today, with a large fleet still flying and spare parts still widely available.

While both aircraft target a similar customer — clubs, training, personal travel — their design differs profoundly: a fabric-covered wood structure with a cantilever low wing for the DR400, versus a more conventional all-metal low-wing structure for the TB. This design choice directly affects handling, cabin comfort and long-term maintenance costs.

The Robin DR400: fabric-covered wood and a signature low wing

The DR400 stands out for its mixed wood-and-fabric structure and its cantilever low wing with no struts or bracing wires, inherited from the Jodel family of aircraft. This design gives it handling that pilots particularly appreciate, good visibility in turns, and lively flying qualities, making it an excellent trainer as well as a light touring aircraft. Successive versions range from 120 to 180 horsepower (Lycoming O-235 to O-360), covering uses from pure training to fast touring, with dedicated versions also used for glider towing.

On the used market, the DR400 appeals thanks to its generally accessible entry price and the availability of a French workshop network well versed in its upkeep. However, the fabric structure requires regular monitoring of its condition and periodic re-covering, an expense specific to this type of construction that must be factored into the long-term budget.

The Socata TB: metal construction and a wide cabin

The Socata TB (whose turboprop side became the Daher TBM range, but which here refers to the piston single-engine Tampico, Tobago and Trinidad variants) uses a more conventional riveted all-metal structure and a cabin noticeably wider than the DR400's, appreciated for comfort on touring flights. Engines also range from 115 to over 250 horsepower depending on the version, with the top-of-range Trinidad (TB20/TB21) standing out for its retractable gear and variable-pitch propeller, unlike the DR400, which remains exclusively fixed-gear and, for most of the fleet, fixed-pitch.

This range's versatility, from the entry-level TB9 Tampico to the top-of-the-line TB20 Trinidad, covers a wide spectrum of uses and budgets within a single family, while keeping a metal structure whose maintenance is generally well mastered by workshops used to classic light aircraft.

Technical comparison and engines

Both families cover a wide range of power outputs and versions, which complicates a direct model-to-model comparison. The table below summarizes the broad trends seen on the used market.

The choice of a specific version within each family often has more impact on budget and use than the choice between the two families themselves, since the gap in power and equipment can be substantial from one model year to another.

Budget comparison: purchase and maintenance

For comparable engine power, both families trade in fairly similar price ranges on the used market, with the DR400 on average slightly more accessible to buy in its base versions. Maintenance, however, differs by nature: the DR400 requires monitoring of the fabric and the wood structure, while the TB demands increased vigilance for corrosion of the metal structure, particularly on aircraft that have spent long periods near the sea or in humid regions.

  • DR400: often a more accessible entry budget, but re-covering to anticipate depending on fabric condition.
  • Base TB (Tampico, Tobago): classic metal-structure maintenance, watch for corrosion.
  • TB20/TB21 Trinidad: higher purchase and maintenance budget, linked to the retractable gear and the variable-pitch propeller.
  • Engine overhaul (TBO): to budget for in both cases depending on remaining hours before the manufacturer's deadline.

In both families, the price gap between two examples of the same version is most often explained by remaining engine potential, the quality of documented maintenance and the installed avionics, far more than by the brand itself.

Which aircraft for which use

For club flying, initial training, or a pilot looking for sporty handling and a contained entry budget, the DR400 remains a natural choice, all the more relevant in France where the maintenance network and parts availability are particularly dense.

  • DR400: training, club flying, light touring and pilots looking for lively handling.
  • TB9/TB10: a metal alternative to the DR400, with a wider cabin for touring with a passenger or family.
  • TB20/TB21 Trinidad: fast, high-performance touring, for a pilot ready to take on the budget of retractable gear and a variable-pitch propeller.
  • Cabin comfort on long trips: generally an advantage for the TB on this criterion.

Conversely, a pilot who favours cabin comfort on long family trips, or who is after the cruise performance of the top-of-range TB20, will find a better fit in the Socata TB range, at the cost of specific metal-structure maintenance and, for the Trinidad, more complex mechanics.

Buying either one used: points to watch

On a DR400, the pre-purchase inspection must pay particular attention to the condition of the fabric (tension, lifting, UV damage), the wood structure beneath the covering, and the history of any previous re-covering. On a TB, priority goes to checking for corrosion on the metal structure, particularly at assembly points and under interior trim, as well as the consistency of hours between the flight logbook and the engine logbook.

Good to know — For a TB20/TB21 Trinidad, also ask for an up-to-date propeller logbook and the retractable gear's history, two items absent from the rest of the TB range as well as from the DR400.

In all cases, have the inspection carried out by an independent mechanic familiar with the exact type of the aircraft, and compare several listings of similar versions and model years before committing, whether it's a DR400 or a Socata TB.
