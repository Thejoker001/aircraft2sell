The Phenom 100 in brief: an entry-level jet built for owner-pilots

The Embraer Phenom 100 was designed to give private pilots and small operators access to light business jet ownership, without requiring a two-person crew in most operating configurations. With its single-pilot certified cabin and integrated avionics, it established itself as one of the benchmarks of the very light jet category shortly after launch in the late 2000s.

On the used market, the Phenom 100 appeals to buyers moving up from a single-engine turboprop or a high-end piston single into their first jet, drawn by the gain in cruise speed and operating altitude compared with a propeller-driven aircraft. This transition, however, calls for particular attention to training, operating procedures and total ownership cost, which is significantly higher than that of a light piston aircraft.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used Phenom 100 depends above all on the production year, total airframe and engine hours, engine status relative to the maintenance program, and whether a transferable engine coverage contract is in place. The earliest aircraft in the line, delivered in the very late 2000s, trade at a noticeably more accessible level than the latest Phenom 100E examples, which benefit from a revised cabin and newer avionics.

Beyond the purchase price, your budget should include the pre-purchase inspection carried out by a facility approved on the type, registration and airworthiness certificate transfer fees, insurance (noticeably higher than on a single-engine piston aircraft), and possibly a cabin interior refresh.

EMB-500 vs Phenom 100E: the differences that matter

The EMB-500 type designation covers the whole Phenom 100 line, but in practice the market distinguishes between the earliest production aircraft and the Phenom 100E version, introduced a few years after launch. The 100E brought a redesigned cabin with more storage space and a revised galley unit, along with an updated Garmin G1000 avionics suite.

For a buyer, this distinction mainly translates into a difference in cabin comfort and interior presentation rather than a performance gap: both versions share the same powerplant and very similar flight performance. The choice between the two will therefore mostly come down to available budget and how much weight is given to cabin comfort for the intended use.

Pre-purchase inspection: specific points to check

As with any used aircraft, a pre-purchase inspection carried out by a maintenance facility approved on the type is essential before finalising a transaction. For a Phenom 100, a few points deserve particular attention given its greater complexity compared with a light single-engine aircraft.

  • Complete history of both engines: engine hours since new or since the last major overhaul (SMOH), traceability of applied service bulletins.
  • Status and transferability of any hourly engine coverage contract, which can represent a substantial saving for the buyer if carried over without interruption.
  • Condition of the landing gear and tyres, which see greater stress on faster approaches than a light piston single.
  • Consistency of the airframe, engine and equipment logbook, with no chronological gaps or grey areas in the history.
  • Validity of the certificate of airworthiness (CofA) and compliance with the latest applicable airworthiness directives for the type.
Good to know — Have the inspection carried out by an approved shop specifically experienced with the Phenom 100: the system complexity of a light jet goes far beyond that of a piston single, and a generic inspection may miss points specific to the type.

Pratt & Whitney PW617F engines: maintenance and coverage programs

The Phenom 100 is powered by two Pratt & Whitney Canada PW617F turbofans. As with most jets in this category, these engines are maintained under a program set by the manufacturer, with scheduled intermediate inspections and a major overhaul once the hourly potential set by the manufacturer is reached.

Many owners enrol in an hourly engine coverage program, which smooths the cost of these major maintenance events over time rather than requiring a large one-off provision. Before buying, it is essential to ask the seller for the exact status of these engines, the hours remaining before the next scheduled maintenance milestone, and whether a coverage contract is in place and transferable to the new owner.

Tip — Always request the engine maintenance reports from the last three years as well as the coverage contract status certificate, if applicable: these documents give an accurate picture of the real cost of continued operation.

Garmin G1000 avionics: updates and obsolescence

The Phenom 100 is fitted with the integrated Garmin G1000 avionics suite, which has gone through several software updates since the aircraft's launch. A cockpit kept current on software and compliant with the latest regulatory traffic-monitoring requirements remains a strong argument both at purchase and at resale.

Before acquiring an example, it is advisable to check the installed software version, the availability of navigation database updates, and compliance with air traffic surveillance requirements in force in the airspace where the aircraft will operate. An aircraft requiring a significant avionics upgrade should have its purchase price adjusted accordingly.

Operating costs and pilot training

Moving from a light piston single to a jet like the Phenom 100 involves a step change in operating costs: fuel, insurance, airport parking and scheduled maintenance all represent expense items far higher than those of a piston aircraft. These recurring costs are essential to factor into your financing plan before committing, not just the initial purchase price.

On the training side, operating the Phenom 100 requires a specific type rating, whose cost and duration must be anticipated in the overall project budget. Having the aircraft maintained at an approved facility familiar with the type, and rigorously documenting every intervention, remains the best way to preserve its resale value over time.

Resale and the European used market

The Phenom 100 benefits from an active used market in Europe, supported by a sizeable installed fleet and good availability of technical support through the Embraer approved service centre network. An aircraft with a transparent maintenance history, an active transferable engine coverage contract and up-to-date avionics generally resells more easily than one whose technical file has grey areas.

Comparing several listings of similar aircraft remains the best way to assess a fair price, both for buying and for preparing a future resale. Price differences between two aircraft of the same model year are almost always explained by engine status, the presence of a transferable coverage contract, and the quality of documented maintenance.
