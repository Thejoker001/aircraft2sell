Used Piper Super Cub: buying guide

Why the Super Cub remains a STOL benchmark

The Piper PA-18 Super Cub has for decades been one of the world's benchmarks for short takeoff and landing (STOL) flying. Its light airframe, high-aspect-ratio wing, generous power-to-weight ratio and tailwheel landing gear give it remarkable performance on rough strips: short runways, grass, sand or snow depending on the equipment fitted. This versatility explains its lasting popularity among mountain pilots, bush-flying enthusiasts and schools that teach flying from unprepared strips.

Built from fabric-covered steel tube, the Super Cub shares a direct lineage with the historic Piper Cub while offering more power and payload. On the used market you'll find several engine options and production years, as well as many aircraft modified under supplemental type certificates (STCs) to add power, fit larger low-pressure tyres, or install skis or floats.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used Super Cub depends above all on the age of the airframe, the condition of the fabric covering and the date it was last recovered, the engine hours remaining before the next overhaul, and which STC modifications are installed. A stock aircraft with old fabric nearing the end of its useful life trades noticeably cheaper than an example recently recovered and fitted with performance modifications sought after by STOL flying enthusiasts.

Beyond the purchase price, your budget should include an independent mechanic's pre-purchase inspection, registration and transfer fees, insurance — often higher for mountain or bush flying use — and, where relevant, a fabric recover to plan for depending on the condition found.

What to check before buying

As with any used aircraft, a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic qualified on the type is essential before finalising a transaction. For a Super Cub, a few points deserve particular attention.

  • Condition of the fabric and the structure underneath: look for corrosion on the steel tube frame, which is often hidden by the covering.
  • Complete engine history: hours elapsed since the last overhaul (SMOH), compliance with the manufacturer's maintenance program.
  • Compliance and documentation of installed STC modifications (power, low-pressure tyres, skis, floats).
  • Condition of the tailwheel gear, shock struts and tailwheel, parts that take particular stress on rough strips.
  • Complete logbook and technical file, with no grey areas in the aircraft's usage history.
Good to know — A Super Cub used intensively for bush flying or glider towing can show more structural wear than an aircraft of the same calendar age flown for ordinary leisure use: usage history matters as much as the hours shown.

Tailwheel gear and fabric: specifics to master

The Super Cub is a tailwheel aircraft, which calls for different handling technique than a tricycle-gear aircraft, particularly during taxi, takeoff and landing. A pilot who has never flown tailwheel should generally plan for additional training before taking on the type, which represents a cost and a timeline to factor into the purchase project.

The fabric-covered structure also requires specific maintenance and a distinct technical vocabulary: fabric deteriorates over time from UV exposure and humidity and must be replaced at intervals set by its actual condition, independent of the aircraft's calendar age. A full recover is a significant investment that should be anticipated — or negotiated into the purchase price — if the condition found during the inspection warrants it.

Maintenance and operating costs

The operating cost of a Super Cub is generally moderate compared to more complex aircraft in the same category, thanks to its mechanical simplicity and the absence of sophisticated avionics on many examples. The main expense items remain the mandatory annual inspection, insurance, parking, engine maintenance and, depending on condition, periodic fabric recovering.

Having the aircraft maintained at an approved shop familiar with the type helps limit unpleasant surprises and preserve its resale value, particularly for STC modifications, which must remain fully documented and compliant.

Tip — Always ask for the latest maintenance invoices and the date of the last fabric recover before making an offer: they give an accurate picture of how seriously the aircraft has been maintained.

Resale and the used market

The Super Cub benefits from an active, durable used market in Europe, supported by a large installed fleet, strong demand from STOL and mountain flying enthusiasts, and good parts availability thanks to ongoing production of spare parts and STC modifications by several manufacturers. A well-maintained aircraft, with recent fabric and a transparent history, generally resells more easily and on better terms than one whose technical file has grey areas.

Comparing several listings of similar models and model years remains the best way to assess a fair price, both for buying and for preparing a future resale. Price differences between two aircraft of the same model year are almost always explained by fabric condition, remaining engine potential and the quality of the STC modifications installed.

It's also worth noting that stock, original Super Cubs can command noticeably different prices than aircraft that have undergone a full restoration or a ground-up rebuild on an existing certificate of airworthiness. These rebuilds, sometimes finished to a very high standard with extensive performance modifications, can trade close to the price of a comparable new aircraft, given how strong demand remains among mountain and bush flying enthusiasts in Europe.

Finally, before signing, always check the consistency between the current registration, the valid certificate of airworthiness and the aircraft's original file, particularly for examples that have changed country of registration more than once over their lifetime: a clear administrative history makes a future resale considerably easier.
