The DG-500: a reference two-seater from DG Flugzeugbau

The DG-500 is a two-seat glider built by the German manufacturer DG Flugzeugbau (formerly Glaser-Dirks), one of the historic names in high-performance soaring in Europe. Designed to deliver good performance in two-seat configuration without sacrificing handling or ease of flying, it has become a well-regarded reference used both for advanced training and for two-seat competition and club flying.

On the used market, the DG-500 benefits from a large installed base across Europe, particularly in Germany, Switzerland, Austria and France, where many clubs and ownership groups have operated the type for several decades. This wide distribution makes it easier to find an example and to access spare parts, a clear advantage over rarer types.

Before targeting a specific example, it's worth distinguishing clearly between the DG-500's different variants, which differ mainly in whether they carry an auxiliary engine, and through aerodynamic refinements across successive versions.

DG-500 versions: pure glider or motor-glider

The DG-500 comes in a pure-glider version, which depends on a winch or tow plane to launch, and a motor-glider version (DG-500M or Elan), fitted with an engine, usually retractable, that allows autonomous launch and relight in the air. This choice has a direct impact on budget, practical use and maintenance requirements.

The pure-glider version remains the preferred choice for club flying or two-seat competition, with a more accessible purchase and maintenance cost. The motor-glider version appeals to pilots seeking autonomy, particularly for instruction without depending on tow availability, at the cost of additional maintenance tied to the powerplant.

Good to know — Before comparing listings, check carefully which exact version is on offer: the price, maintenance and practical use of a pure-glider DG-500 and a motorised DG-500M are not directly comparable.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used DG-500 depends mainly on its age, airframe hours, the condition of the composite skin, the version (pure glider or motor-glider) and, for the latter, engine hours and condition. The oldest pure-glider examples trade at an accessible entry level, while recent, well-equipped motor-glider versions represent a noticeably larger investment.

Beyond the purchase price, your budget should include ancillary costs: a pre-purchase inspection by a workshop approved on the type, registration and transfer fees, insurance, and towing, hangar or parking fees depending on your chosen mode of operation.

What to check before buying

A pre-purchase inspection by a mechanic or workshop approved on the type remains essential before finalising a transaction. For a DG-500, a few points deserve particular attention.

  • Condition of the composite skin: look for any impacts, delamination or previous repairs, which can be hard to detect without a specialist inspection.
  • Total airframe hours and history of periodic inspections (CofA/ARC), with a complete logbook and no grey areas.
  • For a motor-glider version, complete history of the engine and retraction system, along with traceability of replaced parts.
  • Condition of the flight controls, airbrakes and landing gear, whose wear depends heavily on the intensity of club use.
  • Complete technical file, including any declared major repairs and their compliance with manufacturer procedures.
Good to know — Never rely solely on the seller's statements about overall condition: a written independent inspection report from a workshop familiar with the type remains the best protection against a bad surprise after the purchase.

Use: training, club or two-seat competition

The DG-500 is particularly valued for advanced two-seat training, thanks to predictable flight behaviour and good performance that let students discover high-performance soaring without the extreme sensitivity of a pure single-seat competition glider. It is also a common choice for two-seat club flying, whether for introductory flights or advanced-skills training.

In two-seat competition, the DG-500 remains a competitive type on many circuits, valued for its balance of glide performance, handling and ease of hand-over between two different crews sharing the same aircraft within a club or ownership group.

Maintenance and operating costs

The operating cost of a DG-500 remains comparable to other two-seat performance gliders in its category. The main expense items remain the mandatory periodic inspection, insurance, parking and, for the motor-glider version, specific maintenance of the powerplant and its retraction system.

Having the aircraft maintained at an approved workshop familiar with the type, and with composite structures in general, helps limit unpleasant surprises and preserve its resale value. Rigorous, well-documented maintenance records are a strong argument at a future resale, particularly for an aircraft shared within a club where usage intensity can be high.

Tip — Ask for details on how many different pilots have flown the aircraft at the club: a history of intensive shared use deserves a more thorough inspection, particularly of the flight controls and landing gear.

Resale and the used market

The DG-500 benefits from an active used market in Europe, supported by a large installed fleet and good parts availability thanks to the model's lasting popularity with clubs. A well-maintained aircraft, with a transparent history and a complete technical file, generally resells more easily and on better terms than one whose history has grey areas.

Comparing several listings of similar models, while clearly distinguishing between pure-glider and motor-glider versions, remains the best way to assess a fair price, both for buying and for preparing a future resale.
