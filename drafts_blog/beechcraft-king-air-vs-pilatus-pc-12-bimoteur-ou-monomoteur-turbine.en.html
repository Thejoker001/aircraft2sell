Two turboprop icons, two opposite architectures

The Beechcraft King Air and the Pilatus PC-12 rank among the most common business turboprops on Europe's used market, yet they rest on two radically different design philosophies. The King Air, produced across several generations and models for decades, is a twin-engine aircraft offering engine redundancy valued for commercial missions and regular flights. The PC-12, meanwhile, relies on a single turbine engine paired with a generous cabin, keeping operating cost down to a single powerplant.

Choosing between the two depends less on a direct performance comparison than on analysing the intended mission: type of routes, regulatory requirements depending on use (private or commercial), available operating budget, and tolerance for the operational risk of a single-engine turbine.

The King Air: a rugged, versatile twin

The King Air has long established itself as a benchmark in business and regional transport thanks to its ruggedness, operational versatility (passengers, cargo, special missions) and the sheer size of its in-service fleet, which makes parts availability and the maintenance network easier to access. The redundancy of two turbines is seen by many operators and companies as a significant safety advantage, particularly for regular flights over hard-to-reach areas or for commercial operations subject to specific regulatory requirements.

On the used market, the King Air offering is vast and spans several generations and cabin sizes, which makes it possible to find an aircraft matched to a specific budget and mission, but also calls for heightened vigilance regarding the condition and history of each individual aircraft, since configuration and avionics gaps can be significant between two aircraft of the same family.

The PC-12: a large-capacity single-engine turbine

The PC-12 was designed to offer a spacious, modular cabin comparable to many twins, combined with the economic advantages of a single turbine engine: a single powerplant to maintain, fuel consumption generally lower than a twin of comparable capacity, and often a lighter overall operating cost structure. Its modular cabin, which can be configured for passengers, cargo or a mix of both, has made it a particularly popular choice for varied missions, including medical configurations in some setups.

A single-engine turbine sometimes raises questions about redundancy in the event of an engine failure, but modern turbines show high reliability, and the PC-12 is widely operated in private business aviation as well as in certain commercial uses depending on applicable regulations. On the used market, the PC-12 line benefits from a more homogeneous offering than the King Air, with avionics and engine upgrades well documented by generation.

Budget comparison: acquisition and operation

Operating budget is generally the most decisive factor when weighing the two aircraft. A twin like the King Air means maintaining two turbines, two propellers and duplicated systems, which translates into maintenance costs and fuel consumption that are generally higher than a single-engine aircraft of comparable capacity like the PC-12.

Beyond these structural differences, the real cost per flight hour depends heavily on the aircraft's age, remaining engine potential before overhaul and intended frequency of use — two parameters to check precisely for each listing rather than relying on general averages.

Cabin, payload and typical mission

The PC-12 stands out for its particularly spacious cabin for a single-engine aircraft, offering good passenger and baggage capacity, with a cargo door that makes loading bulky equipment easier on some versions. The King Air, depending on model and generation, generally offers a narrower cabin but a broad range of configurations, from the lightest to the largest payload capacities.

  • King Air: relevant for operators seeking engine redundancy, a wide used-market choice by generation and a globally established maintenance network.
  • PC-12: relevant for a mission that prioritises cabin volume, configuration versatility and a controlled single-engine operating cost.
  • Type of routes planned: extended overwater or mountainous flights, where engine redundancy may weigh into the decision.
  • Regulatory framework of the intended use (private or commercial), which can affect each architecture's eligibility depending on the mission.

In both cases, the final choice also depends on local availability of spare parts and approved maintenance centres for the chosen type, a criterion never to be overlooked before a purchase.

Buying either one used: points to watch

Whether it's a King Air or a PC-12, engine history (time since overhaul, SMOH) must be checked with the utmost rigour, engine by engine for a twin. The maintenance logbook must be complete and consistent with the flight logbook, with no unexplained gaps, and any applicable airworthiness directive must be documented as complied with.

Good to know — For a King Air, always check the consistency of hours between the two engines and the two propellers: a significant gap between the two powerplants can signal that an overhaul will be needed on one of them in the short term.

As with any business turboprop, a pre-purchase inspection carried out by an independent mechanic familiar with the type, combined with a complete review of maintenance invoices and the maintenance programme followed, remains the best protection before signing.

Frequently asked questions

Is the PC-12 less safe than a twin like the King Air?

A single-engine turbine inherently lacks redundancy in the event of a failure, but modern turbines show high reliability and the PC-12 is widely operated in business aviation and in certain commercial uses depending on the regulations applicable in each country and for each mission.

Does the King Air always cost more to operate than the PC-12?

As a general rule yes: maintaining two turbines and the fuel burn of a twin remain higher than a single-engine aircraft of comparable capacity, but the real gap depends heavily on the model, generation and maintenance programme followed by each aircraft.

Which aircraft resells better on the European market?

Both benefit from a large in-service fleet and an active used market in Europe; resale depends mainly on the aircraft's overall condition, documented engine history and consistency of maintenance, more than on the model chosen.
