Used Learjet 45: the buying guide

Why the Learjet 45 is a benchmark midsize jet

Introduced in the late 1990s, the Learjet 45 marked a real step up from earlier generations of the brand: a wider cabin, a fully digital avionics suite, and cruise performance that kept alive the speed reputation the Learjet name had built since its earliest models. It remains a reference point today for buyers who want a midsize business jet able to handle frequent rotations without sacrificing cabin comfort.

On the used market, the Learjet 45 comes from several production years, with meaningful differences in installed avionics and standard fit depending on vintage. As with any business jet, that spread makes it essential to pin down your budget and mission profile before starting an active search.

This guide is written for a first-time private buyer considering a jet purchase as much as for a professional operator weighing several aircraft in this category before making a decision.

Budget to set aside for the purchase

The price of a used Learjet 45 is driven first by the aircraft's age, total airframe hours, the remaining engine potential before the next overhaul, and the avionics fitted. The oldest airframes, with reduced engine potential, trade at a far more accessible entry point than well-equipped recent examples, which represent a considerably larger investment.

Beyond the purchase price, budget for the costs that come with this category of aircraft: a pre-purchase inspection by a maintenance facility approved on type, registration and transfer fees, aircraft insurance, crew type training, and possibly an avionics or cabin refresh.

Cabin, speed and range

The Learjet 45's cabin was designed to offer more room than earlier models in the lineage, while keeping the high cruise speed that remains one of the most frequently cited reasons buyers choose this aircraft on the used market. That balance between cabin volume and raw performance makes it a sound choice for medium-range missions with a limited passenger count.

Range comfortably covers most intra-European sectors without a technical stop, which suits operators who favour frequent rotations over very long transatlantic legs. As with any business jet, real-world performance depends heavily on the exact aircraft configuration, its equipped empty weight, and the day's operating conditions.

What to check before buying

As with any used aircraft, a pre-purchase inspection carried out by a maintenance facility qualified on type is essential before closing a deal. For a Learjet 45, a handful of items deserve particular attention.

  • Complete engine history: hours since the last overhaul, compliance with the manufacturer's maintenance programme, traceability of replaced parts.
  • Condition of the structure and flight controls, with close attention to the areas most stressed during fast cruise segments.
  • Avionics currency and compliance with the regulatory requirements of the intended operating regions.
  • Complete logbook and technical file, with no grey areas in the aircraft's history.
  • Valid certificate of airworthiness and overall consistency of the documents provided by the seller.
Good to know — Never rely solely on the seller's description of the aircraft's condition: a written independent inspection report from a shop specialised on type remains the best protection against a bad surprise after purchase.

SMOH, overhaul timing and remaining engine potential

SMOH (hours since the last engine overhaul) is one of the most decisive factors behind the price and residual value of a used Learjet 45. An airframe close to its next overhaul deadline implies a significant expense to plan for in the near to medium term, while high remaining engine potential lets that deadline be pushed out and often justifies a higher asking price.

Before buying, it's worth asking the seller for full details of the engine maintenance programme followed, the date of the last overhaul, and the date of the next one, so this potential cost can be built into your financing plan if the deadline is approaching. An hourly engine maintenance programme, where one is in place, can also work in the aircraft's favour by smoothing out this type of expense over time.

Maintenance and operating costs

The operating cost of a Learjet 45 is broadly comparable to other midsize business jets in its class, with the main expense items being fuel, scheduled maintenance, insurance, parking, and, over the longer term, engine overhaul once the hourly limit is reached.

Having the aircraft maintained at a shop familiar with the type helps avoid unpleasant surprises and protects resale value. Rigorous, well-documented maintenance, recorded in the logbook, is a strong argument at a future resale.

Tip — Always ask for the last three maintenance invoices, as well as the details of the engine programme followed, before making an offer: these documents give an accurate picture of how seriously the aircraft has been maintained.

Resale and the used market

The Learjet 45 benefits from a large installed fleet across both Europe and North America, which keeps the used market relatively liquid and makes it easier to compare several similar listings before deciding. A well-maintained aircraft, with a transparent history, strong remaining engine potential and up-to-date avionics, generally sells more easily and on better terms than one whose technical file has grey areas.

Comparing several listings of similar aircraft remains the best way to judge a fair price, whether buying now or preparing a future resale. Price gaps between two aircraft of the same production year are almost always explained by remaining engine potential and the quality of the avionics fitted.
