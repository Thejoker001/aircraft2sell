Leaseback with a flight school: the real profitability

How a leaseback contract actually works, what income you can realistically expect, which hidden costs eat into it, and how to calculate your aircraft's real profitability before you sign.

What leaseback with a flight school actually is

Leaseback means putting your aircraft at the disposal of a flight school or flying club, which operates it for its students and members in exchange for paying the owner a share of the revenue. The stated goal is almost always the same: have commercial use cover a meaningful part of the aircraft's fixed costs (hangar, insurance, routine upkeep) while keeping some personal access to the aircraft on slots defined in the contract.

On paper the equation looks attractive: the more the school flies the aircraft, the more its fixed costs get diluted across a larger number of hours. In practice, real profitability depends on a set of factors that are routinely underestimated at the point of signing, and that deserve to be examined one by one before committing.

How a leaseback contract works

In a typical leaseback contract, the owner remains the legal owner of the aircraft but hands over operational use to the school or club for a defined, usually renewable, period. The school charges its students at its own hourly rate, and pays the owner a pre-agreed hourly rate that is generally lower than what the student is charged, the difference covering the school's margin (instructor costs, booking management, overheads).

The owner usually keeps a quota of personal hours at a preferential rate, or priority access according to a negotiated schedule. The contract also sets out how maintenance responsibilities are split: some schools handle routine maintenance and invoice it back, others leave this entirely to the owner, who remains responsible for the aircraft's continued airworthiness regardless of who actually flies it.

The real income an owner can expect

The owner's income equals the agreed payout rate multiplied by the hours the school actually flies over the period — not the hours theoretically available. An aircraft on leaseback does not fly continuously: seasonality in flight training, instructor availability, downtime for maintenance, and the slots the owner reserves for themself all mechanically reduce the hours the school can bill out.

As an illustration, an aircraft leased to a school at a given payout rate, but which in reality only flies half of the hours theoretically available over the year, will generate an annual income roughly half of what a best-case usage assumption would suggest. It is precisely this gap between an optimistic assumption and actual use that explains most of the disappointment owners report after a year or two of leaseback.

The hidden costs that eat into profitability

The first routinely underestimated cost is accelerated wear: an aircraft on leaseback generally flies far more hours per year than one kept for strictly private use, which brings major overhaul deadlines, engine overhauls and the replacement of limited-life items (tires, brakes, batteries, interior components heavily used by student training) closer that much faster.

  • Accelerated wear on the airframe and interior — repeated takeoffs and landings by students mean heavier use than the average private owner.
  • Engine deadlines arrive sooner — a higher annual hour count brings the major overhaul or SMOH closer, and should be budgeted for from the start.
  • School-use-specific insurance — a policy covering flight instruction generally costs more than a strictly private-use policy.
  • Unplanned downtime — any incident, even minor, grounds the aircraft and reduces the hours billable to the school during repairs.

The second hidden cost, less visible but just as real, is reduced availability for personal use: an owner wanting to fly on a given weekend may find the slot already taken by a student, particularly at schools where the schedule is managed primarily around commercial activity rather than around leaseback owners.

Calculating real profitability: a simplified method

To estimate the real profitability of a leaseback, start from a conservative flying-hours assumption rather than an optimistic one, by asking the school for its actual usage statistics on aircraft already on leaseback in its fleet. The calculation runs in three steps: estimate realistic annual income (payout rate x hours conservatively expected to actually fly), subtract all the aircraft's annual fixed costs (insurance, hangar, taxes), then subtract a maintenance reserve calculated on the hours actually flown, not on a theoretical assumption.

Good to know — Compare the result of this calculation with the cost of owning an equivalent aircraft outside of leaseback, fixed costs only, to judge whether leaseback genuinely improves the owner's economics or simply offsets part of the costs without a real net benefit.

A reasonable safety margin is to use, for the first years, the lowest usage figure the school communicates rather than its advertised average, to avoid an unpleasant surprise in the first year of actual operation.

The specific risks of leaseback

Beyond the uncertainty over the actual number of hours flown, leaseback exposes the owner to risks specific to flight-school use: damage caused by a student in training, premature wear of sensitive components, and even a faster resale depreciation due to a cumulative hours figure higher than the market average for the aircraft's age.

The risk of depending on the school itself also needs to be anticipated: the school closing down, changing management, or losing its approval can abruptly cut off the income stream and force the owner to find an alternative operating solution, or take back full management of the aircraft, on short notice.

The essential contract clauses

A solid leaseback contract must clearly state the hourly payout rate and how it can be revised, the quota of personal hours guaranteed to the owner and the conditions for booking those slots, the exact split of responsibility for routine maintenance and major overhauls, and the terms under which either party can terminate the agreement early.

  • Hourly rate and indexation — spell out whether and how it can change during the contract.
  • Usage reporting — regular access to the flight log and to the hours actually flown by the school.
  • Maintenance reserve — who funds the major overhaul reserve and how it is handled if the contract ends early.
  • Term and exit clause — conditions and notice period for either party to end the contract.
Word of caution — Have the contract reviewed by a professional familiar with aviation leaseback arrangements before signing, particularly the liability clauses covering incidents caused by a student.

Who leaseback actually makes sense for

Leaseback is best suited to an owner who already flies relatively few hours per year for personal use and whose main goal is to lighten fixed costs rather than generate substantial net income. For this profile, even a modest number of hours paid out by the school can meaningfully reduce the aircraft's annual cost of ownership.

Conversely, an owner who wants to fly frequently on their own schedule, or whose primary goal is net financial return rather than cost reduction, risks being disappointed by leaseback: shared availability and the frequent gap between theoretical and actually flown hours limit the net gain potential once all the hidden costs are factored into the calculation.

In every case, the decision should rest on real figures provided by the school — actual usage statistics for aircraft already on leaseback in its fleet, not optimistic sales projections — and on a contract that clearly protects the owner's interests over the long term.
