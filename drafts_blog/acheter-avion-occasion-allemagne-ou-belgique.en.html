Buying a used aircraft in Germany or Belgium: which country to choose

Market, prices, tax, registration and maintenance: a comparison between Germany and Belgium to help you decide where to search for your used aircraft.

A continental heavyweight versus a small, dense market

Germany and Belgium are direct neighbours within the EASA area, but their light aviation markets are nothing alike in size. Germany has one of the largest light aircraft fleets in continental Europe, built on a very dense network of airfields and a long-standing flying club tradition. Belgium, a much smaller country, offers a more limited market by volume but remarkably active given its size, with a notably international community of pilots and sellers, particularly around Brussels and Antwerp.

For a German buyer, widening the search to Belgium opens up an additional pool of listings without any major distance constraint: the two countries are well connected by road and air, and a viewing trip or a pre-purchase inspection is easy to plan. For a Belgian buyer, the German market offers a considerably larger volume of listings, with deeper choice both among common models and more specialised aircraft.

Both markets also share the same European regulatory framework (EASA), the same certification standards for type-certified and homebuilt aircraft alike, and a geographic proximity that makes cross-border exchange of parts, workshops and technical expertise straightforward. A buyer targeting a specific model therefore has every reason to systematically compare listings on both sides of the border before deciding.

Size and activity of the used market

The German market benefits from an abundant supply of general aviation classics: Cessna 172, Piper PA-28, Diamond DA40, as well as a large fleet of microlights and motor gliders. This abundance often makes it easy to compare several similar aircraft before deciding, a real advantage for negotiation, but it can also mean a slower turnover for some listings, as the local market absorbs part of the supply.

The Belgian market, smaller in volume, remains dynamic relative to the size of the country. It offers a notable supply of touring aircraft, microlights, gliders and light helicopters, together with a significant presence of international sellers who benefit from Belgium's central location. The immediate proximity to Germany, the Netherlands, Luxembourg and France strongly shapes both the composition of the supply and the buyers who browse it.

Comparing the prices charged

Price levels between the two markets remain broadly comparable for the same model and the same maintenance condition. Observed differences owe more to local supply and demand at any given time than to a structural difference between the two countries, although Germany sometimes commands a premium on aircraft fitted with modern avionics (glass cockpit), which are actively sought after on this market.

In both cases, comparing several listings of similar models remains the best way to assess a fair price before committing, regardless of which country the aircraft you want is located in.

As Belgium is a smaller market, interesting listings can sell quickly: a buyer specifically targeting this market should set up alerts and move fast once a listing matches their criteria, always subject to a satisfactory pre-purchase inspection.

Tax and purchase-related costs

A private sale involving an aircraft already in free circulation within the European Union generally does not trigger a new VAT charge, whether the sale takes place in Germany (Umsatzsteuer) or Belgium, since the tax was already paid when the aircraft was first put into circulation within the EU. The situation differs for a sale made by a VAT-registered professional, or for an aircraft imported from outside the EU, which can trigger import VAT calculated on its customs value, in either country.

For a cross-border transaction between Germany and Belgium, it is worth checking precisely any intra-EU VAT formalities applicable between professionals, as well as the ownership-transfer costs specific to each country. Using a professional familiar with cross-border transactions (broker, specialised lawyer) helps avoid procedural mistakes that can prove costly, particularly regarding VAT or customs documentation.

Registration: LBA versus DGTA/BCAA

In Germany, every aircraft based and operated in the country must be registered with the Luftfahrt-Bundesamt (LBA), under the D- prefix. In Belgium, the competent authority is the Direction générale Transport aérien (DGTA), also known by its Dutch name Bestuur van de Luchtvaart (BCAA), which issues the OO- prefix. An aircraft bought in either country can be registered in the other, depending on the buyer's choice: keeping the registration of the purchase country is not mandatory.

  • Check the consistency between the logbook, the technical file and the aircraft's actual condition before any change of registration.
  • For an aircraft imported from another country, plan for its prior deregistration before the new registration can be issued.
  • Plan for administrative lead times, which can vary significantly between the LBA and the DGTA/BCAA.
  • Gather a valid Certificate of Airworthiness (CofA), the bill of sale and proof of liability insurance before the first flight under the new registration.
Good to know — Common EASA rules govern transfers between member states and make them simpler than an import from outside the European Union. Expect generally 2 to 8 weeks depending on how complete the file is, regardless of the destination country.

Maintenance network and parts availability

Germany has a very dense network of approved maintenance workshops for the most common models, which makes maintenance easy regardless of the aircraft's final country of registration. Belgium, although smaller, also benefits from recognised approved workshops, particularly around its major airfields, and enjoys the immediate proximity of the German, Dutch and French maintenance networks.

For rarer models or specific engines, it remains useful to check in advance the availability of parts and the proximity of a specialised workshop before finalising the purchase, regardless of the aircraft's country of origin. Belgium's small size works in its favour here: no approved workshop is ever very far away, and turning to a neighbouring German or Dutch workshop always remains a simple option.

The language barrier is generally limited in both countries: a large share of aviation professionals speak English alongside their national language, which makes interactions easy for a buyer coming from the other side of the border.

Which country to favour depending on your profile

For a buyer who favours a wide choice and immediate proximity to their flying club or home airfield, searching primarily on the German market often remains the simplest solution, given the volume of listings available. For a buyer willing to widen their search to find the best-maintained aircraft at the best price, or interested in the international profile of some Belgian sellers, considering both markets in parallel gives access to a wider supply and makes it easier to compare the technical files available.

Good to know — In all cases, a pre-purchase inspection by a qualified independent mechanic remains essential, whether the aircraft is located in Germany, Belgium or elsewhere in Europe.

Ultimately, the country of purchase matters less than the actual condition of the aircraft and the quality of its maintenance record. Comparing listings from both markets widens your options and gives you a stronger negotiating position, whether on price or on the terms of the transaction.
