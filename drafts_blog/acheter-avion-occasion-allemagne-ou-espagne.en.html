Buying a Used Aircraft in Germany or Spain: Which Country to Choose

Why compare these two markets

Germany and Spain are both among the most active light aviation markets in Europe, but for different reasons. Germany owes its vitality to a dense network of airfields and a long-standing general aviation tradition, with numerous flying clubs and an abundant supply of touring single-engine aircraft. Spain, meanwhile, benefits from weather favourable to flying year-round and a rapidly growing network of flying schools and clubs, particularly around Madrid, Barcelona, Seville and Valencia.

For a European buyer, the choice between the two countries is not just about the seller's nationality: it involves real differences in supply volume, price ranges, registration procedures and tax constraints. This guide breaks down each dimension to help you point your search toward the market best suited to your project.

The German market in brief

The German used-aircraft market is one of the deepest in continental Europe. The density of airfields and the large number of active private owners and flying clubs generate a substantial volume of listings, making it easier to compare several similar aircraft before deciding. The most commonly listed models remain the classics of the European market (Cessna 172, Piper PA-28, Diamond DA40), with a notable supply of microlights and two-seaters.

An advantage frequently cited by buyers: German aircraft are, on average, well equipped with recent avionics (glass cockpit), a segment in particularly high demand in this market, and logbooks are generally kept with great documentary rigour.

The Spanish market in brief

The Spanish market is younger than the German one but growing steadily, driven by flying schools and a climate that allows flying almost all year round without strong weather constraints. The used-aircraft supply consists mainly of classic touring single-engine aircraft and microlights, with a growing segment of higher-performance touring aircraft (Cirrus, Diamond) favoured for long intra-European trips.

Listings circulate through specialist marketplaces, regional flying clubs affiliated with the Real Federación Aeronáutica Española, and word of mouth on the airfields. Supply volume there remains generally lower than in Germany, but prices can be slightly more attractive on some entry-level segments.

Price comparison by aircraft type

In both countries, price ranges remain broadly aligned with those seen elsewhere in the European Union for comparable aircraft. The real gap between two listings almost always depends more on airframe condition and remaining engine potential than on the country of origin.

Registration: LBA versus AESA

In Germany, every aircraft based and operated within the country must be registered with the Luftfahrt-Bundesamt (LBA), under the D- prefix. In Spain, the competent authority is the Agencia Estatal de Seguridad Aérea (AESA), which issues the EC- prefix. In both cases, buying an aircraft already registered in the country results in a simple update of the registered owner; an aircraft imported from another country requires prior deregistration from the register of origin.

  • Valid certificate of airworthiness and ARC — check this before signing, in both countries.
  • Bill of sale — dated, signed by both parties, stating the serial number, required by both the LBA and AESA.
  • Liability insurance certificate — required before first use under the new registration.
Good to know — Common EASA rules govern the transfer between EU member states and make it simpler than an import from outside the EU. Generally allow 2 to 8 weeks in both countries, depending on how complete the submitted file is.

VAT and taxation in both countries

A sale between private individuals is generally not subject to VAT, either in Germany (Umsatzsteuer) or in Spain (IVA). However, a sale carried out by a professional or a company may be subject to it in both countries, and an aircraft imported from a country outside the European Union remains subject to import VAT, calculated on its customs value. The main difference lies in the standard rate applicable when VAT is due: 21% in Spain, versus a slightly higher standard national rate in Germany. It is recommended to have your tax situation confirmed before acquisition, particularly if the aircraft has already changed country of registration several times.

Climate, infrastructure and real-world use

Spain's climate allows flying a greater number of days per year under stable conditions, a real benefit for training and for limiting wear linked to winter operating conditions. Germany, on the other hand, offers a denser network of airfields and a particularly well-developed fabric of approved maintenance workshops, which can make the technical follow-up of an aircraft easier once acquired.

For a buyer who does not intend to base the aircraft in the country of purchase, these considerations matter less: what remains essential is the quality of the technical file and the ease of the registration transfer to the destination country, two points that are comparable between the two markets thanks to the harmonised EASA framework.

Which country to choose based on your profile

If you are looking for the widest choice of listings to compare several similar aircraft and negotiate confidently, the German supply volume is an advantage. If you favour a climate that allows flying year-round and an entry-level segment that is sometimes slightly more accessible, the Spanish market deserves particular attention. In both cases, the rule remains the same: the actual condition of the aircraft and the consistency of its maintenance logbook matter more than its country of origin.

Tip — Whichever country you choose, never finalise a purchase without an independent pre-purchase inspection: it is the only reliable way to verify that the declared condition matches the aircraft's actual technical state.
