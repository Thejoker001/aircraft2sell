Certified vs ELA1/ELA2 Aircraft: Regulatory Differences When Buying

Regulatory overview: certified, ELA1, ELA2

Every certified aircraft operated in Europe falls under the EASA regulatory framework, but that framework is not uniform: it provides lighter rules for the lightest aircraft, grouped under the ELA1 and ELA2 categories ("European Light Aircraft"). These categories do not define a different status from certification itself — an ELA1 or ELA2 aircraft remains a certified aircraft, with a valid certificate of airworthiness — but they unlock simplified requirements for maintenance, oversight and, in some cases, initial certification.

Understanding which category an aircraft falls into before buying it is therefore not an administrative detail: it determines who may legally maintain the aircraft, which documents must be present in the file, and what level of structure (Part-M Light workshop, CAMO organisation, etc.) is needed to keep it airworthy after purchase.

The ELA1 category

The ELA1 category covers the lightest aircraft, in practice powered aeroplanes with a maximum take-off mass not exceeding 1,200 kg and no more than four seats, together with certain gliders and balloons. The aim of this category is to lighten the regulatory burden for the simplest leisure aviation, in particular by allowing airworthiness oversight that is less demanding than for a more complex aircraft, while keeping the basic safety requirements common to any certified aircraft.

For a buyer, a well-maintained ELA1 aircraft is in principle one of the simplest and least costly profiles to own from an administrative standpoint, provided its airworthiness file is complete and consistent since it entered service.

The ELA2 category

The ELA2 category is broader: it covers aeroplanes with a maximum take-off mass not exceeding 2,730 kg, certain light single-engine helicopters, and somewhat larger gliders and balloons. It therefore includes a large share of classic touring single-engine aircraft (such as the Cessna 172, Piper PA-28, Diamond DA40) that fall outside ELA1 because of their mass or number of seats.

An ELA2 aircraft also benefits from a lighter maintenance framework compared with heavier or more complex aircraft (high-mass twins, turboprops), but the requirements remain more structured than for an ELA1, particularly regarding the maintenance programme followed and, depending on the case, the use of a continuing airworthiness management organisation.

Maintenance and oversight: what changes

The main practical difference between a "classic" certified aircraft, an ELA1 and an ELA2 lies in how maintenance is organised. For ELA1 and ELA2 aircraft, the European framework allows lighter oversight through a simplified maintenance programme (often referred to as "Part-M Light"), sometimes without a mandatory approved CAMO (Continuing Airworthiness Management Organisation), depending on the type of operation. Beyond these categories, a heavier certified aircraft or one used in more complex commercial operations generally requires full oversight by a CAMO, with an approved maintenance programme and more demanding documentation requirements.

  • ELA1 — lighter airworthiness oversight, often without a mandatory CAMO for private, non-commercial use.
  • ELA2 — an intermediate framework, a simplified maintenance programme is possible but documentation requirements are more significant.
  • Certified outside ELA — full oversight by an approved organisation, an approved maintenance programme is mandatory.

Impact on the certificate of airworthiness

In every case, ELA1, ELA2 or otherwise, the aircraft must hold a currently valid certificate of airworthiness (CofA) and an up-to-date airworthiness review certificate (ARC). What differs is the process for renewing that ARC: for an ELA1 and, under certain conditions, an ELA2, renewal can be carried out by an independent airworthiness review staff member without systematically going through a full CAMO, which reduces the administrative burden and cost for the owner. For a heavier or more complex aircraft, renewal generally goes through the CAMO responsible for managing the aircraft's continuing airworthiness.

Good to know — An aircraft's ELA1 or ELA2 category never removes the need to verify the actual validity of the CofA and ARC at the time of purchase: an aircraft that is "simple" to maintain remains unusable if these documents are not in order.

Concrete consequences when buying

For a buyer, knowing the exact category of the aircraft under consideration helps anticipate three essential points: the likely annual cost of maintaining airworthiness, how easy it will be to find a competent approved provider locally, and the complexity of the file to check before signing. An ELA1 aircraft generally means a simpler file to audit, with fewer regulatory parties involved in its history. An ELA2 aircraft requires a somewhat deeper check of the maintenance programme followed and the consistency between work reports and the flight logbook.

  • Identify the aircraft's exact category (ELA1, ELA2 or outside ELA) before any price negotiation.
  • Check whether a CAMO has been managing the aircraft and, if so, request the history of that oversight.
  • Compare the maintenance programme actually applied with the one set out by the manufacturer or type certificate holder.

"Classic" certified aircraft outside ELA

Beyond the ELA1 and ELA2 categories, a heavier certified aircraft (a high-mass twin, a turboprop, a light business jet) falls under a more comprehensive oversight framework, with maintenance carried out through an approved Part-145 organisation and airworthiness managed by a dedicated CAMO. This more demanding framework logically comes with higher ownership costs, but also, generally, with a documentation trail that is more rigorous and easier for a buyer to audit, since it relies on approved organisations bound by strict traceability obligations.

How to choose based on your project

The choice between an ELA1, an ELA2 or a heavier certified aircraft depends above all on the intended use. For simple leisure flying with a controlled ownership budget, a well-documented ELA1 or ELA2 offers the best compromise between administrative simplicity and maintenance cost. For more intensive use, regular professional travel, or shared operation among several pilots, the more structured framework of a certified aircraft outside ELA, managed by a CAMO, can instead bring extra peace of mind despite a higher cost. In every case, have the exact category and the consistency of the airworthiness file confirmed by an independent professional before signing.

Word of caution — Never rely solely on the sales listing to determine an aircraft's regulatory category: have the certified maximum mass, number of seats and actual airworthiness status checked by a professional before making any offer.
