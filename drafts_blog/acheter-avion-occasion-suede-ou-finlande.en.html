Buying a used aircraft in Sweden or Finland: which country to choose

Market, prices, tax, registration and maintenance: a comparison between Sweden and Finland to help you decide where to look for your used aircraft.

Two neighbouring Nordic markets, two different moods

Sweden and Finland share the Gulf of Bothnia and a well-established amateur aviation culture, but the two used-aircraft markets have rather different characteristics. Sweden, a large country with a long coastline, has a considerably bigger light aircraft fleet, with supply spread across a large number of airfields. Finland, also very extensive but less populated, offers a more tightly focused market, concentrated around a handful of regions and strongly shaped by the culture of flying on lakes and leisure aviation over forest.

For a buyer based in Northern Europe, widening the search to both countries gives access to complementary supply: a wider and more diverse Swedish market, and a smaller Finnish market where certain specific configurations (float-equipped seaplanes, ski-equipped aircraft) remain well represented.

Size and profile of the used market

Sweden has one of the most active light aviation fleets in Northern Europe, driven by a long tradition of gliding, aerobatics and bush flying on skis or floats. Supply is wide and varied, including many aircraft suited to rough strips and long domestic distances, alongside classic touring single-engines.

Finland, for its part, offers a market more modest in volume, in line with its population size, but active within a well-structured network of flying clubs. Supply is concentrated on a handful of common models (Cessna 172, Piper PA-28, Diamond DA40), with a notable share of club aircraft rigorously maintained, as well as a notable presence of float-equipped seaplanes linked to the country's huge number of lakes.

Comparing prices

Price levels remain broadly comparable between the two countries for the same model and maintenance condition, with observed differences mostly down to the relative scarcity of certain types in the smaller Finnish market. For specific categories such as float- or ski-equipped aircraft, both countries sometimes command slightly higher prices due to strong local demand for these configurations.

In both countries, comparing several listings of similar models remains the best way to assess a fair price before committing. For a common model such as a Cessna 172, both markets generally offer similar price levels, which means the choice can mostly come down to geographic proximity and the configuration you are looking for.

Tax and purchase-related costs

As with any transaction in Europe, the applicable taxation depends above all on the buyer's status (private individual or professional), the country of tax residence and the country in which the aircraft will be registered after the sale. A sale between private individuals is generally not subject to VAT in either country, while a sale by a professional or a company may be subject to it under the applicable national rules.

For a cross-border transaction between Sweden and Finland, it is worth checking any intra-EU VAT formalities applicable between professionals, as well as the administrative fees specific to each destination country. Using a professional experienced in cross-border transactions reduces the risk of procedural errors.

Registration: the Transportstyrelsen versus Traficom

In Sweden, aircraft registration is handled by the Transportstyrelsen (the Swedish transport authority), under the SE- prefix. In Finland, it is Traficom (the Finnish Transport and Communications Agency) that manages the registration register, under the OH- prefix. Both authorities apply common EASA rules for registration transfers between member states, which makes the process considerably simpler than an import from outside the European Union.

  • Valid certificate of airworthiness and ARC — a precondition for any flight under the new registration, whichever destination country is chosen.
  • Deregistration from the register of origin — required before the new registration if the aircraft changes country.
  • Complete technical file — airframe, engine and propeller logbooks to be submitted to the new authority.
Good to know — Generally allow 2 to 8 weeks for a registration transfer between Sweden and Finland depending on how complete the file is, a timeline comparable to that observed for any intra-European transfer.

Maintenance network and parts availability

Sweden has a maintenance network spread over a large area, in line with the country's size, with workshops specialised in local configurations such as skis and floats. Finland, with a smaller number of workshops, nonetheless has operators experienced with the models most common locally, often attached to the flying clubs themselves, along with well-regarded specialists in maintaining float-equipped seaplanes.

The geographic proximity between the two countries, together with the frequent use of English alongside local technical documentation, often makes it possible to have an aircraft maintained in either country with no major constraint, provided the chosen workshop is properly approved for the type concerned. For a float-equipped aircraft, a specialised Finnish or Swedish workshop is often the most relevant choice.

Climate, airfields and flying season

Sweden shows marked climate differences between the south, where the flying season extends over most of the year, and the north, where winter imposes greater constraints, offset by the practice of ski flying. Finland experiences a comparable climate, with a long and harsh winter across most of the country and a summer season well suited to flying on lakes, particularly favourable for float-equipped seaplanes that benefit from the country's huge number of bodies of water.

For a buyer considering basing an aircraft in the north of either country, it is worth anticipating winter equipment (engine pre-heating, suitable tyres or skis) which can represent an additional investment compared with an aircraft based in the south.

Which country to favour depending on your profile

For a buyer interested in an aircraft suited to rough strips, skis or floats, or simply wanting access to a wider and more varied market, Sweden deserves particular attention. For a buyer looking for a classic touring single-engine within a compact, well-documented market, or specifically interested in a float-equipped seaplane suited to flying on lakes, Finland offers specific advantages.

A pilot planning to remain based in Finland will find a practical benefit in registering their aircraft locally, if only to simplify day-to-day administrative procedures with Traficom. Conversely, a pilot planning to fly regularly over long domestic distances within Sweden, especially towards the north of the country, will often find more practical advantages in registering with the Transportstyrelsen.

Overall ownership costs should also factor in ferrying: an aircraft spotted far from your home base will add extra cost and delay before it can first be used, whether it is located in Sweden or Finland. Sweden's larger internal distances, compared with Finland, are worth quantifying carefully before settling on a far-flung listing.

Good to know — In all cases, a pre-purchase inspection by a qualified independent mechanic remains essential, whether the aircraft is located in Sweden, Finland or elsewhere in Europe.

Ultimately, widening your search to both markets often provides access to a broader supply and better negotiating opportunities, especially for a buyer based in Northern Europe who can reasonably consider an aircraft located on either side of the Gulf of Bothnia.
