CubCrafters Carbon Cub vs Piper Super Cub: Which STOL Aircraft to Choose

Classic backcountry legend or modern lightweight cub: a full comparison between the Piper Super Cub and the CubCrafters Carbon Cub to help you pick the right STOL aircraft for your budget and mission.

Two STOL philosophies, one shared mission

Ask any backcountry or bush pilot to name the two aircraft that define the short takeoff and landing (STOL) world, and two names come up almost every time: the Piper Super Cub, launched in the 1950s and still regarded as the benchmark taildragger, and the CubCrafters Carbon Cub, a modern aircraft that reinterprets the classic cub shape with composite construction and a far more powerful engine.

Both share the same high-wing taildragger silhouette, but they were built with different goals in mind. The Super Cub leans on proven ruggedness and a huge, mature used market, while the Carbon Cub chases extreme STOL numbers through a lighter airframe and a bigger power-to-weight margin, which changes how the aircraft behaves on a short, rough strip.

Deciding between the two is rarely a question of which aircraft is objectively "better" — it is a question of which mission, budget and maintenance network fits your flying best.

The Piper Super Cub: the backcountry original

The Piper PA-18 Super Cub spent decades as the default choice for backcountry flying, light aerial work and off-airport training. Its welded steel-tube fabric-covered airframe, mechanical simplicity and the sheer volume of parts still in circulation make it an aircraft famous for longevity and for being easy to keep airworthy over the long run.

On the used market, Super Cubs show up in a wide range of configurations and engine conversions, from stock horsepower to significantly upgraded powerplants fitted by specialist shops over the decades. That variety is exactly why the asking price for two Super Cubs of the same year can differ enormously — the specific engine, modification history and condition of the fabric matter far more than the model year printed on the logbook.

Spare parts, type-specific mechanics and modification STCs are abundant for the Super Cub, a direct result of a production run and an active fleet that span more than seventy years.

The CubCrafters Carbon Cub: the modern, lightweight successor

The CubCrafters Carbon Cub keeps the classic cub silhouette but rebuilds everything underneath it: a composite and lightweight-alloy structure, a fuel-injected engine delivering substantially more horsepower, and an empty weight noticeably lower than a stock Super Cub. The result is a power-to-weight ratio that enables very short takeoff and landing distances even at high loaded weight.

Depending on the market, the Carbon Cub is sold as an experimental kit-built aircraft or in a certified version, and it ships with modern avionics and a finish that feels closer to a new aircraft than to a vintage taildragger — which generally puts it above a comparable Super Cub on acquisition price, especially for recent, well-equipped examples.

CubCrafters also offers several Carbon Cub variants, from travel-oriented trims to configurations tuned specifically for STOL competition flying, with different flap and leading-edge slat setups. That range makes it harder to compare two used examples purely by model name, since equipment, engine and finish can vary a lot within the same line.

STOL performance: takeoff, landing and useful load

On paper, both aircraft post remarkably short takeoff and landing distances compared with an ordinary light aircraft, but the Carbon Cub generally pushes further thanks to its higher engine power and lighter structure. A well-prepared Super Cub — low-pressure tundra tires, the right propeller, careful loading — still holds its own on rough strips and keeps an excellent reputation among seasoned bush pilots.

Actual useful load depends heavily on the installed engine and equipment: a well-converted Super Cub with a strong engine can rival a Carbon Cub on several numbers, but usually at the cost of a more demanding maintenance and inspection routine.

Budget: purchase price and running costs

Purchase budget is often the deciding factor between the two aircraft. A used Super Cub, particularly in a stock or modestly equipped configuration, is typically more accessible to buy than a recent Carbon Cub, whose price can approach that of a new aircraft once you factor in its finish and integrated avionics.

On the running-cost side, the Super Cub benefits from a dense network of mechanics and parts built up over decades of production and heavy use, which can keep certain maintenance costs down. The Carbon Cub, newer to the market, sometimes requires turning to a smaller circle of shops for engine- or composite-airframe-specific work.

Good to know — Always ask for the full engine history on a Super Cub (stock or converted, hours since overhaul): it's the single factor that moves price and performance the most from one example to another.

Which aircraft for which mission

The choice between the two should start with your actual mission. For sustained backcountry flying, regular aerial work on very rough strips, or a preference for proven mechanical ruggedness backed by a huge parts network, the Super Cub remains a safe bet, especially on a more moderate entry budget.

  • Piper Super Cub: well suited to heavy backcountry use, a more moderate purchase budget and maintenance backed by a very wide parts network.
  • CubCrafters Carbon Cub: the right pick for maximum STOL performance, a recent well-equipped aircraft and power-hungry leisure or backcountry flying.
  • Budget set aside for routine upkeep and any engine conversion to plan for depending on the specific aircraft.
  • Personal preference for a proven tube-and-fabric airframe versus a modern composite structure.

Conversely, a pilot chasing the shortest possible takeoff and landing rolls on a modern airframe — and willing to accept a higher purchase price and a more specialised maintenance network — will find the Carbon Cub the stronger argument for leisure flying or backcountry exploration.

Buying used: what to check

Whether you're looking at a Super Cub or a Carbon Cub, a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic familiar with the type is essential, especially on a STOL aircraft that sees regular rough-strip use, where the airframe and landing gear absorb repeated stress. On a Super Cub, pay close attention to fabric condition, possible corrosion in the tubular structure, and the complete engine history if it has been converted.

On a Carbon Cub, check the full build traceability if the aircraft was assembled as an experimental kit, along with engine history and compliance with the manufacturer's maintenance program. In both cases, ask for the complete logbook and maintenance invoices before making an offer, and compare several similar listings to judge a fair price.

Legal status is also worth checking carefully: depending on the market, a Super Cub may be certified or carry major modifications not always fully documented, while a kit-built Carbon Cub falls under a different regulatory category than a factory-certified model. This status has direct consequences for insurance, resale and maintenance obligations, and should be confirmed with the relevant authorities before any transaction.
