Liability-only vs all-risk aircraft insurance: a detailed comparison

Coverage, exclusions, deductibles and budget: everything you need to compare between a liability-only policy and an all-risk policy for your aircraft.

Two very different coverage philosophies

Aircraft insurance rests on two fundamentally different philosophies that are essential to understand before comparing quotes. Liability insurance protects the owner or operator against the financial consequences of damage caused to third parties: passengers, property on the ground, or other aircraft. An all-risk policy, by contrast, adds coverage for the aircraft's own hull — its intrinsic value in case of damage, total loss or theft — on top of that liability protection.

In almost every European country, some minimum level of liability insurance is a regulatory requirement for any aircraft in operation, regardless of its value. All-risk hull coverage, on the other hand, remains an optional extra that each owner chooses to take out or not, depending on the aircraft's value, its intended use and their own appetite for financial risk.

Liability insurance: what it actually covers

Liability cover compensates third parties harmed by the aircraft's operation, whether that means bodily injury to passengers or people on the ground, or material damage to third-party property, including another aircraft involved in a collision. It never covers damage sustained by the insured aircraft itself: a hard landing, a runway excursion or an engine failure that damages the airframe will not be compensated under liability cover alone.

  • Bodily injury to third parties — passengers on board and people on the ground, within the policy's coverage limits.
  • Property damage to third parties — property on the ground, airport infrastructure, or other aircraft involved in an incident.
  • Legal defence costs — legal assistance if the operator's liability is challenged.

For an older aircraft with low replacement value, a liability-only policy meeting the regulatory minimum can represent an economically sound choice, provided the owner accepts bearing the full cost of a total loss themselves in the event of a major incident.

All-risk cover: what it adds

An all-risk policy keeps the full liability cover in place while adding hull coverage, generally referred to as "hull" or "casco" cover. This coverage compensates the owner for damage sustained by the aircraft itself, whether that means a partial repair after an incident or replacement-value compensation in case of total loss.

  • Hull coverage — compensation in the event of damage, destruction or total loss of the airframe.
  • Theft coverage — usually included or available as an option depending on the policy, for an aircraft stolen from its parking spot.
  • Weather events — damage linked to hail, storms or flooding depending on the policy terms.

The cost of this extension depends directly on the insured value of the aircraft, its age, its type and the experience of the declared pilot. For a newer or higher-value aircraft, hull coverage generally represents a significant share of the overall premium, but it protects against a potentially very heavy financial risk in the event of a major incident.

Common exclusions to check in both policy types

Whichever policy is chosen, certain exclusions come up repeatedly in aviation insurance contracts and deserve a careful read before signing. Normal wear and tear, proven maintenance deficiencies, flying without a valid licence or rating, or using the aircraft outside its declared conditions (undeclared commercial use, for example) are among the most common exclusions.

It is essential to declare the aircraft's actual use precisely (private use, instruction, rental) at the time of underwriting, since any mismatch between declared use and actual use found at the time of a claim can lead to a refusal to pay out, whether under liability or all-risk cover.

Deductibles: as important a criterion as the premium

The deductible — the portion of a claim the policyholder must bear themselves — is just as decisive a comparison criterion as the annual premium. Two policies with a comparable premium can carry very different deductibles depending on the hull coverage chosen. A higher deductible mechanically lowers the premium but increases the owner's out-of-pocket cost in the event of a claim.

Some policies offer a reduced or waived deductible after a number of claim-free flying hours, or for a pilot who can demonstrate minimum experience on type. Others apply a differentiated deductible depending on the nature of the claim (ground damage, in-flight damage, total loss), which is worth clarifying line by line before comparing two quotes against each other.

Budget comparison by aircraft profile

Insurance budgets vary considerably depending on the type of aircraft, its insured value, the pilot's experience and the number of annual flying hours declared. As a general order of magnitude, liability-only cover represents a markedly smaller share of the total budget than an all-risk policy including hull coverage, with the gap depending directly on the aircraft's insured value.

Good to know — The exact cost of an aviation insurance policy varies considerably between insurers and according to the declared profile: only a personalised quote from a specialist broker will give you a reliable figure for your aircraft and pilot profile.

For an older, lower-value aircraft, the premium gap between the two policy types can remain limited, which often makes all-risk cover worthwhile even for a modest aircraft. For a newer or higher-value aircraft, the gap generally becomes more significant, without undermining the case for hull coverage given the financial risk it removes.

Which policy to choose depending on your use case

For a low replacement-value aircraft, belonging for example to a flying club or operated under a group ownership scheme with a limited collective budget, a liability-only policy, possibly supplemented by hull coverage with a high deductible, can be a reasonable compromise between protection and budget.

For a newer aircraft financed through a loan or lease, an all-risk policy generally becomes unavoidable: the lender very often requires it as a condition of financing, in order to protect its own security interest in the financed aircraft.

For an aircraft used for instruction or rented out to third parties, it is essential to check that the policy explicitly covers this use, which is frequently excluded by default from standard policies designed for strictly private use. A specialist aviation broker remains the best resource to refine this choice according to your exact profile, your aircraft and your budget.

Good to know — Before taking out any policy, always check that the aircraft's logbook is up to date and complete: a proven maintenance deficiency can lead to a refusal to pay out, regardless of the policy chosen.

Ultimately, the choice between liability-only and all-risk cover depends above all on the aircraft's value, how it is financed and how it is used, far more than on a general preference for one type of policy over the other.
