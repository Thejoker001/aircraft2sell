Buyer-seller aircraft disputes: procedures and remedies in Europe

What happens when a disagreement arises after the sale of a used aircraft? Here are the legal warranties that apply, the mediation routes available, and the court remedies possible, including for a cross-border transaction in Europe.

Why buyer-seller disputes happen

Selling a used aircraft involves large sums of money and technical information that a buyer cannot always verify alone before the deal closes. Most disputes stem from a gap between what was advertised and what is discovered after delivery: engine hours that don't match the logbook, a failure occurring a few weeks after the sale, an undisclosed prior repair, or disagreement over exactly what was included in the price (avionics, spare parts, propeller).

Other disputes concern the execution of the contract itself: a deposit paid followed by a sale that falls through, a delivery delay, or disagreement over who bears the ferry-flight and registration costs. In every case, the quality of the documentation exchanged before the sale — listing, inspection report, written correspondence — becomes the central piece of evidence for any later claim.

A purchase made without an independent pre-purchase inspection sharply increases the risk of a dispute, because the buyer later discovers issues that an approved mechanic could have identified before signing.

Hidden defects and legal warranties

In most European legal systems, a buyer who discovers a serious defect after the sale — one that was not apparent at the time of purchase and existed before it — can invoke the hidden-defect warranty or an equivalent non-conformity mechanism. The common principle is this: the defect must have existed before the sale, must not have been visible on a normal inspection, and must render the aircraft unfit for its intended use or substantially reduce its value.

The exact conditions for applying this protection — time limits to act, burden of proof, scope of the warranty — vary significantly from one country to another, and depend on whether the seller is a professional dealer or a private individual. A professional seller is generally subject to stricter information and warranty obligations than a private seller, who can more easily limit their liability through a clearly drafted "as is" clause accepted knowingly by the buyer.

  • Keep every document predating the sale that describes the aircraft's condition: the listing, email exchanges, the inspection report.
  • Commission an independent expert report as soon as the defect is discovered, before any repair, to date and document the problem.
  • Check the sale contract's clauses on warranties, "as is" sales, and the governing law.
Word of caution — Never carry out a substantial repair before an independent expert has documented the defect: acting too quickly can undermine your ability to prove the defect existed before the sale.

Amicable settlement and mediation

Before considering a lengthy and costly court action, amicable settlement remains the fastest and least risky route for both parties. A written formal notice detailing the defect found, the supporting evidence, and the remedy sought (repair, price reduction or cancellation of the sale) is often the formal starting point of a negotiation.

When the parties cannot agree directly, an independent mediator or conciliator — sometimes offered by an aviation trade association or a chamber of commerce — can help find a compromise without going to court. This route is particularly well suited to disputes where both parties have an interest in preserving a future relationship, for example between professional players on the used-aircraft market.

A broker or intermediary who took part in the transaction can also play a useful role as a facilitator, provided they remain neutral and do not exclusively defend one party's interests.

Court remedies and competent jurisdiction

If the amicable route fails, either the buyer or the seller can bring the matter before the competent civil or commercial court. In Europe, which jurisdiction is competent and which law applies typically depends on the parties' place of residence, the place where the contract was signed or performed, and any jurisdiction or governing-law clauses included in the sale agreement. Such clauses, when they exist and are validly drafted, are generally respected by courts, which underlines the importance of negotiating them carefully before signing.

Court proceedings almost always involve a contradictory technical expert assessment, in which an expert appointed by the court examines the aircraft and its documentation to establish the origin and the timing of the defect. This phase can take several months or longer, depending on the technical complexity and the court's workload.

Good to know — Before starting proceedings, have a lawyer specialised in aviation law assess the balance between the likely cost of the action (expert fees, legal fees, delays) and the amount actually at stake.

The role of the broker and the independent expert

An aviation broker engaged early in the negotiation can significantly reduce the risk of a dispute, by structuring the sale (written purchase agreement, a clause making the sale conditional on a satisfactory inspection, staged payments) and by ensuring the documents provided by the seller are complete and consistent. In the event of a dispute, their transaction file often becomes valuable evidence.

The independent expert, for their part, intervenes at two key moments: before the sale, during the pre-purchase inspection, to detect anomalies; and after the sale, in case of disagreement, to produce an objective technical report on the origin and likely timing of the defect. Choosing a recognised expert with no ties to either party strengthens the credibility of their report before a mediator or a court.

  • A serious broker documents every step of the transaction in writing, which limits later disputes.
  • The post-sale expert should be chosen independently of both parties, ideally Part-145 / Part-CAO approved and experienced with the specific aircraft type.

Cross-border disputes: specifics in Europe

Many used-aircraft sales in Europe involve a buyer and a seller based in different countries, sometimes with an aircraft registered in yet a third country. This cross-border dimension adds real complexity: the law governing the contract and the competent jurisdiction do not always coincide with the country where the aircraft is registered, nor with where it is physically located at the time of the dispute.

Within the European Union, common rules on judicial cooperation in civil matters generally make it easier to recognise and enforce a judgment issued in one member state before the courts of another member state, which limits — without eliminating — the risk that a court victory ends up having no practical effect. Outside the Union, the situation depends on whatever bilateral agreements exist between the countries involved, which is why it is better to clarify this point contractually before the sale rather than discover it once a dispute has already started.

Tip — For any cross-border sale, have the purchase agreement specify the governing law and the chosen method of dispute resolution (mediation, arbitration, a designated court), rather than leaving this point implicit.

Preventing disputes before signing

The best protection against a dispute remains prevention at the time of sale. A written purchase agreement that precisely describes the aircraft's condition, the equipment included, the payment terms, and a clause making the sale conditional on the outcome of the pre-purchase inspection covers the vast majority of sources of later disagreement. Systematically requesting the complete logbook, the engine history (SMOH) and a current certificate of airworthiness (CofA) before committing further reduces the risk of discovering a problem after the sale.

Finally, documenting every exchange in writing — rather than relying on a verbal agreement — gives each party the means to assert their rights if, despite every precaution, a dispute still arises.
