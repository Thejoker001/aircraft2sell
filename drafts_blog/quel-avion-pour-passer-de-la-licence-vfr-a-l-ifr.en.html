Which aircraft to move from a VFR to an IFR rating

Cirrus, Diamond or IFR-equipped Cessna: how to pick the aircraft best suited to your progression towards instrument flying, based on your budget and intended use.

Why the aircraft matters when moving to IFR

Moving from a VFR rating to an instrument rating fundamentally changes the way you fly: you no longer navigate mainly by looking outside, but by relying on instruments, navigation and published procedures. The aircraft used for this training therefore has a direct impact on the quality of the learning, well beyond simply meeting the minimum equipment requirements.

A poorly equipped aircraft, or one whose avionics aren't representative of current standards, makes training harder and can make the eventual move to a real touring aircraft, once the rating is earned, more difficult than it needs to be. A modern, well-equipped aircraft, by contrast, lets you focus on the procedures rather than fighting dated avionics.

This choice also has a medium-term financial impact: a pilot who trains on the type of aircraft they plan to buy or regularly rent afterwards builds directly on that experience, while training on an aircraft very different from the one used later adds an extra adaptation phase, with its own cost in familiarisation hours.

What actually changes going from VFR to IFR

Beyond the rating itself, instrument flying demands more discipline: strict adherence to departure and approach procedures, workload management in degraded conditions, and constant monitoring of the flight and navigation instruments. The aircraft should offer reliable avionics, an autopilot where possible to reduce workload during advanced training, and backup instruments in case of a failure.

  • Certified GPS/IFR navigation avionics, with up-to-date published approaches.
  • A working autopilot, particularly useful for approach training and managing mental workload.
  • Backup instruments independent of the main system in case of an electrical or instrument failure.
  • Some level of de-icing or anti-icing capability, depending on the weather conditions typically encountered at your home base.

This extra discipline translates concretely into a higher cockpit workload: reading approach charts, denser radio communication with air traffic control, and managing navigation and monitoring engine parameters at the same time. It is precisely to absorb that workload that choosing an aircraft with a well-designed cockpit layout, with avionics that are readable at a glance, makes a real difference during the training phase.

Cirrus SR20/SR22: integrated avionics and safety

The Cirrus SR20/SR22 line has become a benchmark for advanced IFR training, thanks to its factory-integrated avionics, its whole-aircraft backup parachute, and a cockpit layout designed around managing information in IFR conditions. The multi-function display centralises navigation, onboard weather and engine monitoring, which reduces the cognitive load on a pilot in transition.

These aircraft particularly suit pilots who, after earning their rating, plan to use their own aircraft for real IFR touring, since the SR22's avionics and cruise performance in particular remain relevant well beyond the training phase alone.

Diamond DA40/DA42: a stable platform for instrument training

The Diamond DA40, and to a lesser extent the DA42 for schools that also train on twins, offer a platform known for being stable and predictable — two qualities that matter a great deal during instrument training. Their composite structure and good cabin visibility ease the gradual transition from visual flying to instrument flying, without the handling surprises that some older aircraft can present.

Many IFR training schools use DA40s fitted with modern avionics for exactly this reason: the aircraft stays predictable in simulated degraded conditions, leaving the student pilot more mental bandwidth to focus on the procedure rather than on raw handling.

The DA42, for clubs and schools that have one, adds a further dimension to training: managing two engines simultaneously under IFR conditions also lays the groundwork for a possible later progression to a multi-engine rating, while keeping the same stability and predictability philosophy as the DA40.

IFR-equipped Cessna 172/182: the economical, common choice

The Cessna 172, and the more powerful 182, remain very common choices for IFR training because their running costs are generally lower than those of a recent Cirrus or Diamond, and because aircraft fitted with modern avionics are widely available both on the used market and in flight schools. Their well-documented flight characteristics and ruggedness make them proven platforms for practising approach procedures without having to worry about exotic mechanics.

For a tighter budget, a 172 with up-to-date IFR avionics is often the most realistic compromise between purchase cost, hourly cost and training quality.

The Cessna 182, more powerful and able to carry more useful load than the 172, suits pilots who already plan, once rated, to fly regular IFR trips with several passengers or substantial baggage. It remains less common in school fleets than the 172, though, which can limit the availability of a suitably equipped training aircraft depending on the region.

The final choice depends above all on what you plan to do once you hold the rating: a pilot who expects to fly occasional local IFR has different needs from a pilot planning to use their own aircraft for regular IFR cross-country flights with a filed flight plan.

It's also worth comparing these three families on a criterion that's often overlooked: the local availability of equipped aircraft for rent or in a club near you. A pilot who doesn't yet own an aircraft is better off choosing the platform with the richest local supply, so they can keep flying IFR regularly after earning the rating, not just during training.

Buying a used IFR-equipped aircraft: points to watch

Whether it's a Cessna, a Diamond or a Cirrus, buying a used IFR-equipped aircraft means checking that the installed avionics match the approaches you can actually use, the status of navigation database updates, and the maintenance history of every piece of electronic equipment — often more expensive to replace than the airframe itself.

Good to know — Ask for the complete logbook and check that each avionics unit has its own maintenance history: poorly maintained avionics can cost more to bring up to standard than the price difference between two comparable listings.

A pre-purchase inspection carried out by an independent mechanic familiar with the installed avionics remains essential, alongside comparing several similar listings to assess a realistic market price.

Finally, keep in mind that avionics evolve faster than the airframe itself: an aircraft that is otherwise well maintained mechanically may still need a significant avionics upgrade to stay fully usable for IFR flying over the long term. Factoring that into the negotiation, rather than discovering it after the purchase, avoids a budget surprise that can represent a significant share of the aircraft's total price.
