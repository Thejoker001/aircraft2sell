Cessna 206 vs Pilatus PC-6 Porter: which rugged bush plane to choose

Two bush-flying legends, two different philosophies

The Cessna 206 Stationair and the Pilatus PC-6 Porter both rank among the most respected bush planes in the world, yet they neither share the same origin nor the same design philosophy. The Cessna 206 is an American piston single-engine aircraft, a rugged and versatile evolution of the fixed-gear Cessna line, built to carry passengers and freight off rough strips without demanding an exotic rating. The Pilatus PC-6 Porter, on the other hand, is a Swiss aircraft designed from the outset for mountain utility flying, with short take-off and landing (STOL) capabilities that have few equals among fixed-wing aircraft.

Comparing the two aircraft therefore means weighing two different approaches to ruggedness: an affordable, widely available piston single on one side, and a specialised utility aircraft on the other — costlier to buy and to run, but built for missions that few fixed-wing aircraft can handle at all.

The Cessna 206 Stationair: the versatile piston single

The Cessna 206, marketed as the Stationair, has become a reference for light transport off rough strips thanks to its spacious cabin, reinforced fixed gear and large cargo door that makes loading bulky freight straightforward. Powered by a piston engine, it remains more affordable to maintain than a turbine aircraft and can be flown by a privately rated pilot without requiring a demanding specific type rating.

On the used market, the 206 comes in several generations, some fitted with STOL kits (gap seals, vortex generators) that noticeably improve its short-field performance. This versatility makes it a popular choice both for recreational flying over rough terrain and for light commercial operations carrying passengers or freight into remote areas.

The Pilatus PC-6 Porter: the turbine STOL benchmark

The Pilatus PC-6 Porter was designed from day one to operate from extremely short strips and demanding terrain, particularly in mountain environments. Its rugged airframe, high-lift wing and turboprop powerplant (on the Turbo Porter versions, by far the most common on the used market) give it short take-off and landing performance that still stands, today, as a benchmark among fixed-wing aircraft.

This specialisation comes at a cost: the PC-6 is far more complex to operate than a piston single, requiring a turbine type rating and more demanding maintenance procedures. In return, it offers a payload capacity and mission versatility — parachute operations, freight, air ambulance, mountain sightseeing flights — that go well beyond what a Cessna 206 can deliver.

Performance and STOL capability compared

On pure performance, the two aircraft do not play in the same league. The Cessna 206, especially when fitted with a STOL kit, already offers very decent short-field capability for a piston single, more than enough for the vast majority of rough strips encountered in general aviation. The PC-6 Porter, thanks to its design built entirely around mountain flying and its turbine powerplant, goes considerably further: it can operate from much shorter strips and in altitude and terrain conditions that the 206 simply cannot approach with the same safety margins.

PowerplantPiston engine, simpler maintenanceTurboprop, specific type rating required
STOL capabilityGood, excellent with a STOL kitExceptional, designed from the outset for mountain flying

This performance gap largely explains the gap in price and operating complexity between the two aircraft: the PC-6 should not be seen as simply a more powerful 206, but as a working tool built for missions the 206 cannot safely undertake.

Acquisition budget and operating costs

At comparable age and equipment, the Cessna 206 remains significantly more affordable to buy than a Pilatus PC-6 Porter. Its maintenance, limited to a widely used piston powerplant, is also easier to budget for over time, backed by a large network of shops familiar with the type across Europe.

The PC-6 Porter, as a specialised turbine aircraft, requires a significantly higher acquisition and operating budget: fuel, turboprop maintenance, more limited parts availability and generally higher insurance given its mission profile. This extra cost is justified for operators who genuinely need the aircraft's exceptional STOL capabilities, but represents a disproportionate investment for ordinary recreational use.

Good to know — Before committing to either aircraft, ask for a detailed insurance quote matching your pilot profile and mission: the premium gap between a piston single and a turbine utility aircraft can be substantial.

Which aircraft for which mission

The choice between the Cessna 206 and the Pilatus PC-6 Porter should first be based on the mission you actually intend to fly. For recreational use, personal travel or light commercial operations over rough but not extreme strips, the 206 offers an excellent balance of ruggedness, versatility and controlled cost.

  • Cessna 206: ideal for recreational flying over rough terrain, light passenger or freight transport, and a first experience with a bush plane.
  • Pilatus PC-6 Porter: relevant for operations requiring extreme STOL performance, substantial payload or specialised missions (mountain, parachute operations, heavy freight).
  • Pilot rating and experience: transitioning to a turbine requires specific training that not every private pilot has undertaken.
  • Shop network and parts availability in your region, often a deciding factor for a less common turbine aircraft.

Conversely, an operator who genuinely needs to access extremely short strips, at altitude or over demanding terrain, will find in the PC-6 Porter a tool with no equal among piston singles, provided they accept the budget and operating complexity that come with it.

Buying either one used: points to watch

Whether it's a Cessna 206 or a Pilatus PC-6 Porter, the aircraft's usage history deserves particular attention: one flown intensively off unpaved strips or in mountain operations experiences different structural stresses and wear than one used mainly from paved aerodromes. For the 206, check the condition of the reinforced landing gear, whether an approved STOL kit has been fitted, and the complete engine history. For the PC-6, the inspection should focus first on the turboprop, its operating hours and its maintenance history with a shop approved on the type.

Good to know — Always have the pre-purchase inspection carried out by an independent mechanic familiar with the relevant type, and ask for the complete logbook and maintenance invoices before committing.

In all cases, comparing several listings of similar aircraft in age, equipment and usage history remains the best way to assess a fair price, whether for an affordable piston single or a specialised turbine utility aircraft.
