Buying a used aircraft in Serbia or Bulgaria: which country to choose

Market, prices, tax, registration and maintenance: a comparison between Serbia and Bulgaria to help you decide where to look for your used aircraft in the Balkans.

An EU member market versus a non-EU market

Serbia and Bulgaria share a common border and an aviation heritage shaped by Eastern European aviation, but their administrative status has differed fundamentally since Bulgaria joined the European Union in 2007. Bulgaria now applies the EASA regulatory framework like any other member state, while Serbia, a candidate country for accession, retains its own national aviation certification system, distinct from the European framework.

This difference in status has concrete consequences at every stage of a cross-border purchase: recognition of airworthiness documents, applicable taxation, customs formalities and the registration procedure. It should not, however, discourage comparing the two markets, each of which has its own advantages for a buyer of a used aircraft in the Balkan region.

Size and activity of the used market

The Bulgarian light aviation market benefits from EU membership, with a regular supply of Western touring single-engines (Cessna, Piper, Diamond) as well as microlights, helped by the free circulation of aircraft already EASA-certified from other member states. The Black Sea coast and the associated air tourism also contribute to market activity.

The Serbian market remains active but smaller in volume, with supply marked by the legacy of Yugoslav aviation: locally designed training and aerobatic aircraft (Utva, Soko), gliders and a number of Western single-engines imported over the decades. The fleet there is on average noticeably older, which also makes it an interesting destination for enthusiasts of collectible aircraft or types rarely found elsewhere in Europe.

Comparing prices

For the same common Western model, price levels asked in Bulgaria remain generally comparable to those seen elsewhere in the European Union, with no particular discount linked to geographic location. In Serbia, asking prices for equivalent Western aircraft may appear more attractive at first glance, but this gap is often offset, or even exceeded, by the import and compliance costs needed to operate the aircraft outside Serbia.

Before settling on an asking price in Serbia, it is therefore essential to quantify the full cost of putting the aircraft into service outside the country: transport, customs formalities, import taxation and any necessary technical compliance work.

Tax, customs and purchase-related costs

In Bulgaria, a transaction between private individuals is in principle not subject to VAT, while a sale by a professional may be subject to it under applicable Bulgarian and European rules. Bulgaria's membership of the EU internal market also means an aircraft bought in another member state can generally circulate there without customs formalities, with intra-EU VAT rules applying between professionals. The Bulgarian lev (BGN), pegged to the euro, also limits exchange-rate risk for a buyer from the eurozone.

As Serbia lies outside the European Union's customs territory, buying an aircraft there and then importing it into a member state instead involves a customs declaration, and potentially customs duties as well as import VAT calculated in the final destination country, under that state's own rules. As the exact amounts depend on the declared value, the country of import and the applicable customs regime, it is advisable to use a customs broker or a professional experienced in aircraft imports before committing.

Registration: Bulgaria's GDCA versus Serbia's CAD

In Bulgaria, aircraft registration is handled by the General Directorate Civil Aviation Administration (GDCA), under the LZ- prefix. Once EASA-certified, the aircraft can generally be transferred to another member state under the common mutual recognition procedures applicable within the Union.

In Serbia, it is the Civil Aviation Directorate of the Republic of Serbia (CAD) that manages the national register, under the YU- prefix, under a certification framework specific to the country, not automatically recognised by EASA. Importing an aircraft registered in Serbia onto a European register therefore usually involves a thorough technical review of the airworthiness file, and sometimes an import certification process with an approved organisation, to establish equivalence with EASA requirements.

  • Complete, translated airworthiness file — airframe, engine and propeller logbooks, essential for any regulatory equivalence assessment.
  • Checking the type certification status — a Western-designed aircraft is more likely to be easily importable than a locally designed type with no recognised EASA equivalent.
  • Deregistration from the register of origin — required before any new registration in another country.
Good to know — The time and cost of a registration transfer between Serbia and an EU member state are generally higher than a simple intra-European transfer such as the one applicable from Bulgaria, due to the extra regulatory recognition step required.

Maintenance network and parts availability

Bulgaria has Part-145 or Part-CAO approved workshops, benefiting from easier parts supply thanks to EU single-market membership for common Western models. Proximity to other South-East European markets also makes it easier to travel for an inspection or a scheduled overhaul.

In Serbia, the maintenance network operates to national standards, with good historical expertise on Yugoslav-origin aircraft, but access to parts can be more complex for recent Western models, relying more heavily on imports from Western Europe. For an older, locally designed aircraft, however, the expertise of Serbian workshops is often superior to what would be found elsewhere in Europe.

Which country to favour depending on your profile

For a buyer wanting a classic Western aircraft, easily registrable in any member state and with no complex import process, Bulgaria is the simpler choice, with the added benefit of an active tourism market that eases future resale and a currency pegged to the euro.

For a buyer drawn to Yugoslav-designed aircraft, gliders or historic training aerobatic types, and willing to plan for the non-EU import process, Serbia can offer interesting opportunities, provided customs and certification costs are precisely budgeted before buying, ideally with support from an aircraft import specialist from the negotiation stage onward.

Good to know — Whichever country is chosen, a pre-purchase inspection by a qualified independent mechanic remains essential before any commitment, and matters even more for an aircraft whose technical file will later need to be assessed by a foreign certification authority.

Ultimately, the choice between Serbia and Bulgaria depends less on a raw price difference than on your tolerance for administrative procedures and the intended future use of the aircraft. Taking the time to quantify the full acquisition cost, including transport and compliance work, remains the best way to avoid an unpleasant surprise after signing.
