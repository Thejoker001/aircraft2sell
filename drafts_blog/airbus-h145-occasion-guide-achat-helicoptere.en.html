Why the H145 became a benchmark among light twins

Descended from the EC145/BK117 lineage, the Airbus H145 is a light-to-medium twin-engine helicopter widely used for helicopter emergency medical services (HEMS), VIP transport, police work and utility missions. Its spacious cabin, twin-engine redundancy and mission versatility have made it a market reference, both among professional operators and among a few private owners who have the operational setup to support it.

On the used market, you'll find both first-generation EC145s with a conventional tail rotor and newer H145s fitted with a Fenestron and, on the latest model years, a five-bladed main rotor. This diversity of configurations means you need to clearly identify the exact version of an aircraft before comparing prices, since the two families are not strictly equivalent in terms of performance and operating costs.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used H145/EC145 depends heavily on the aircraft's generation (conventional-tail-rotor EC145 or Fenestron-equipped H145), total airframe and engine hours, remaining potential before the next major inspections, and mission configuration (standard civil, medicalised HEMS, VIP). An aircraft fitted out for medical evacuation with a full medical interior represents a noticeably larger investment than a basic utility version.

Beyond the purchase price, your budget should include ancillary costs: a pre-purchase inspection by an approved maintenance organisation qualified on the type, registration and transfer fees, helicopter-specific insurance, and possibly an avionics or cabin interior upgrade.

Arriel engines and remaining potential

The H145 is powered by two Safran (formerly Turbomeca) turbines from the Arriel family, the exact version depending on the aircraft's model year. This twin-engine configuration provides valuable redundancy for professional operations, but it also requires rigorous tracking of engine potential, the main transmission and life-limited components.

Before buying, it's essential to ask for the detailed remaining hours and cycles on each engine, on the main transmission and on life-limited parts, as well as the history of inspections carried out at a manufacturer-approved shop. An aircraft close to its potential limits should systematically translate into a lower price, to anticipate the cost of upcoming major inspections.

From EC145 to H145: Fenestron and airframe evolution

The most significant change between the early EC145 versions and the more recent H145 concerns the anti-torque system: the first models have a conventional tail rotor, while later H145 generations are fitted with a Fenestron, which is quieter and reputed to be more tolerant in crosswind conditions. The latest model years also feature a five-bladed main rotor, which further improves performance and vibration comfort.

These changes have a direct impact on resale value: an aircraft fitted with the Fenestron and the latest rotor improvements generally trades at a noticeably higher level than a first-generation EC145 with a conventional tail rotor, for a comparable age.

What to check before buying

As with any used aircraft, a pre-purchase inspection by an approved maintenance organisation qualified on the type is essential before finalising a transaction. For an H145, a few points deserve particular attention.

  • Complete history of both engines: remaining hours and cycles, compliance with the manufacturer's maintenance program, traceability of replaced parts.
  • Condition and remaining potential of the main transmission and life-limited parts (rotor, gearbox, dynamic components).
  • Condition of the Fenestron or conventional tail rotor depending on version, and of the tail structure.
  • Cabin fit-out configuration (standard, HEMS, VIP) and compliance of installed equipment with current certifications.
  • Complete logbook and technical file, with no grey areas in the aircraft's history and its Certificate of Airworthiness.
Good to know — Never rely solely on the seller's statements about overall condition: a written independent inspection report, carried out by a shop qualified on the type, remains the best protection against a bad surprise after the purchase.

Uses: EMS, VIP and utility missions

The H145 is particularly common in air ambulance fleets, thanks to its spacious cabin which can accommodate full medical equipment and a care team alongside the patient. Its twin-engine redundancy and performance also make it a popular choice for police work, surveillance and VIP transport missions.

For a private buyer or a small operating structure, the key is to clearly define the intended mission before choosing the cabin configuration and equipment level, since reconfiguring the aircraft after purchase can represent a significant cost.

Maintenance and operating costs

The operating cost of an H145 is that of a professional twin-engine helicopter: the main expense items are mandatory periodic inspections, engine and transmission maintenance, specific insurance and, in the longer term, major inspections once the hourly potentials are reached. These costs are clearly higher than those of a light single-turbine helicopter, in exchange for the redundancy and mission capability on offer.

Having the aircraft maintained at a manufacturer-approved shop familiar with the type helps limit unpleasant surprises and preserve its resale value. Rigorous, well-documented maintenance records are a strong argument at a future resale, particularly for an aircraft intended for intensive professional use.

Tip — Always ask for the detailed remaining potential on each life-limited component before making an offer: it gives an accurate picture of the real cost of ownership in the short and medium term.

Resale and the used market

The used H145/EC145 market remains mainly driven by professional operators (HEMS, police, VIP transport), making it a more specialised segment than that of light single-engine helicopters. A well-maintained aircraft, with a transparent history, a Fenestron and a sought-after cabin configuration, generally resells more easily and on better terms than one whose technical file has grey areas.

Comparing several listings of similar generation and configuration remains the best way to assess a fair price, both for buying and for preparing a future resale. Price differences between two aircraft of the same model year are almost always explained by remaining engine and transmission potential, as well as the cabin configuration installed.
