Buying a used aircraft in Cyprus or the United Kingdom: which country to choose

Market, prices, registration, VAT post-Brexit and pitfalls to avoid: a complete comparison to help you decide where to buy your used light aircraft, between Cyprus and the United Kingdom.

Two very different markets in size and profile

Cyprus and the United Kingdom carry very different weight on the European general aviation market. The United Kingdom is one of the largest general aviation markets in Europe, with a dense network of aerodromes and a very large installed fleet of touring single-engine aircraft, two-seaters, microlights and gliders. Cyprus, a much smaller market, operates more as a niche, driven in part by the tax appeal of its aircraft register for international owners and operators, with a notably smaller volume of listings than the United Kingdom.

Since Brexit, the United Kingdom has its own civil aviation authority independent of EASA for registration and airworthiness, the Civil Aviation Authority (CAA), while retaining procedures largely inspired by the European framework for light aircraft. Cyprus, as an EU member state, applies EASA airworthiness standards directly, which simplifies registration transfers with other member states.

For a buyer looking for a wide choice of comparable aircraft to inspect before deciding, the United Kingdom offers more options. For a buyer more interested in the Cypriot tax framework or a base for operations in the eastern Mediterranean, Cyprus can hold specific appeal despite a more limited supply.

Price comparison by aircraft type

Price levels depend above all on engine history (hours remaining before TBO), airframe condition and installed avionics rather than on the country of sale alone. The notably smaller volume of listings in Cyprus, however, makes a direct comparison between two aircraft harder than on the British market, where the density of listings makes it possible to find several similar examples to compare. The pound/euro exchange rate is also an extra factor to watch for a British listing.

Registration: the Cypriot register versus the CAA

In Cyprus, aircraft registration falls under the Cypriot Department of Civil Aviation, with the prefix 5B-. As an EU member state, Cyprus applies the common EASA framework, which makes a registration transfer from another EU country easier. In the United Kingdom, the competent authority is the Civil Aviation Authority (CAA), which assigns the prefix G-, under its own framework since the country left the European Union and EASA.

In both cases, a purchase between private individuals already registered locally simply requires updating the registered owner, while an aircraft imported from the other country first needs to be deregistered from the original register before being registered in the destination country.

  • A valid certificate of airworthiness (CofA) — issued under the EASA framework in Cyprus, under the CAA's own framework in the United Kingdom since Brexit.
  • Bill of sale — dated, signed by both parties, stating the serial number, required by both registers.
  • Proof of third-party liability insurance — required before first use under the new registration, in both Cyprus and the United Kingdom.
Good to know — A registration transfer between Cyprus and another EU member state follows a common EASA framework that is generally fast. A transfer between Cyprus and the United Kingdom now involves dealing with two separate authorities applying their own procedures since Brexit: plan for extra time and check the exact formalities with the CAA before committing.

VAT and the effect of Brexit

Cyprus, as an EU member state, applies the common EU VAT regime: a sale between EU-resident private individuals is generally VAT-free, while a sale by a business or an import from outside the EU remains subject to import VAT. The Cypriot aircraft register is also known for its tax appeal among certain international operators, a point worth validating case by case with a specialist tax advisor based on your situation.

The United Kingdom is no longer part of the European Union or its common customs regime since Brexit. An aircraft imported into the United Kingdom from the EU, or the other way round, may now be subject to customs formalities and British VAT calculated on the value of the aircraft, except in specific situations to be validated case by case. It is essential to have your tax and customs situation validated before acquiring an aircraft, especially one that has already changed registration country several times since Brexit.

Pre-purchase inspection and documents

Whether the aircraft is based in Cyprus or the United Kingdom, a pre-purchase inspection by a mechanic independent of the seller remains the best protection against unpleasant surprises. It covers airframe condition, the consistency of the engine logbook, maintenance history and compliance of the avionics with current requirements (transponder, 8.33 kHz radio).

  • Up-to-date airframe, engine and propeller logbooks, with no chronological gaps, whether kept in Cyprus or in the United Kingdom.
  • Maintenance invoice history for the last three years, from an approved shop in the relevant country.
  • No undeclared structural damage, verifiable through the maintenance file and a thorough visual inspection.
Point of caution — For an aircraft based in Cyprus for several years for tax reasons, always ask for written confirmation that maintenance was carried out at a recognised approved shop, rather than being skipped simply because the aircraft saw little use.

Which country to choose depending on your profile

The choice between Cyprus and the United Kingdom depends above all on your project and your tolerance for administrative procedures. The United Kingdom appeals to buyers who prioritise a large volume of listings, a very developed general aviation community and a wide choice of models to compare, at the cost of going through the post-Brexit customs and tax formalities for any cross-border import or export. Cyprus suits a more targeted project, linked to the tax appeal of the Cypriot register or to a base in the eastern Mediterranean, in exchange for a notably smaller used market and fewer comparable examples available.

In every case, the decisive criterion remains the quality of maintenance and the transparency of the aircraft's history, far more than the country of registration alone. Widening your search to the whole European market to establish a reliable benchmark, especially if local supply is limited, remains the best way to make an informed choice.

Tip — If you are considering an import between the two countries, have the customs and tax formalities applicable since Brexit, as well as the specific tax regime of the Cypriot register, validated beforehand by a specialist professional.
