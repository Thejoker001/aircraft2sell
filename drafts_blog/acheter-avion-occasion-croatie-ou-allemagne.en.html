Buying a Used Aircraft in Croatia or Germany: Which Country to Choose

Market, prices, registration, taxation and pitfalls to avoid: a complete comparison between Croatia and Germany for buying a used light aircraft with confidence.

Two very different markets

Croatia and Germany represent two very different sides of light aviation in Europe. Germany has an extremely dense network of airfields and an abundant used aircraft supply, driven by a strong flying club tradition and a particularly large fleet of touring singles. Croatia, by contrast, offers a smaller market in volume, but with an interesting range of aircraft suited to coastal and tourist flying, plus a growing number of light sport aircraft and small singles linked to the development of leisure flying along the Adriatic.

For a buyer, this difference in volume has a direct consequence: it is generally easier to compare several similar aircraft in Germany than in Croatia, where the choice remains more limited for certain specific types. On the other hand, the Croatian market can offer less contested opportunities, sometimes with less competition for an interesting listing.

Price ranges observed in both countries remain broadly aligned with the European average for comparable aircraft, with the gap mostly explained by local availability rather than a fundamentally different market.

Tip — An aircraft's price depends above all on engine history (hours remaining before overhaul), airframe condition and the avionics fitted, far more than its current country of registration. Always compare at equivalent engine potential, regardless of where the listing is based.

Registration: CCAA in Croatia, LBA in Germany

In Germany, any aircraft based and operated on German territory must be registered with the Luftfahrt-Bundesamt (LBA), under the D- prefix. For a purchase between private individuals where the aircraft is already registered in Germany, the transfer is a simple update of ownership. For an imported aircraft, de-registration from the original register is required beforehand.

In Croatia, registration falls under the Croatian Civil Aviation Agency (CCAA), under the 9A- prefix. The principle is similar: a simple ownership update for an aircraft already registered in Croatia, and mandatory prior de-registration for an aircraft imported from another register.

  • Valid certificate of airworthiness and ARC — check this before signing anything, in either country; the expiry date determines when you can first fly under your own name.
  • Bill of sale — dated, signed by both parties, stating the aircraft's serial number.
  • Proof of third-party liability insurance — required before first use under the new registration, in both Germany and Croatia.
Good to know — Common EASA rules govern transfers between EU member states and make them simpler than an import from outside the Union. In both countries, expect the process to generally take 2 to 8 weeks depending on how complete the submitted file is.

VAT and taxation in both countries

In both countries, a sale between private individuals is in principle not subject to local VAT (Umsatzsteuer in Germany, PDV in Croatia). A sale carried out by a professional or a company may, however, be subject to it, and an aircraft imported from a country outside the European Union remains liable for import VAT calculated on its customs value, at the national rate applicable in the country of entry.

SituationTax treatment
Sale between EU resident private individualsGenerally outside the scope of VAT, in both countries
Sale by a professionalVAT potentially applicable, in Germany as in Croatia
Import from outside the EU (e.g. USA)Import VAT, national rate of the EU country of entry

It is advisable to have your tax situation confirmed before acquiring an aircraft in either country, especially if the aircraft has already changed registration country several times within the European Union.

Pre-purchase inspection and documents

Whether buying in Germany or Croatia, a pre-purchase inspection by a mechanic independent of the seller remains the best protection against unpleasant surprises. The denser German market generally makes it easier to find type-experienced approved mechanics nearby; in Croatia, it can be worth planning ahead for an inspector to travel to the aircraft, or for the aircraft to be flown to a nearby approved workshop.

  • Up-to-date airframe, engine and propeller logbooks, with no chronological gaps, regardless of the current country of registration.
  • Maintenance invoice history for the last three years.
  • No undeclared structural damage, particularly for aircraft based near the coast in Croatia, where exposure to salt air warrants a closer look at corrosion.
Watch out for — Never finalise a purchase without seeing the original logbooks and having their consistency checked by a professional, whether the aircraft is based in Germany or Croatia.

Cost of ownership by country

Day-to-day operating costs (fuel, parking, insurance, annual inspection) remain broadly comparable between the two countries for a similar aircraft, but a few practical differences are worth anticipating. In Germany, the density of the approved workshop and spare parts network generally eases routine maintenance and limits turnaround times. In Croatia, the coastal climate calls for increased vigilance on corrosion and on maintaining the canopy and exposed metal parts, a cost item not to be underestimated over the ownership period.

Hangar parking, often recommended to limit exposure to the elements, can represent a variable budget depending on the airfield chosen in either country: it is worth checking locally before setting your final annual budget.

Pitfalls to avoid depending on the country

In Germany, the most common mistake remains neglecting to verify de-registration from the previous register for an imported aircraft, or underestimating certificate of airworthiness transfer costs. In Croatia, the most common pitfall is underestimating the impact of the marine climate on the airframe and failing to plan for the time and cost of travelling to an approved workshop if none is available close to the aircraft's home airfield.

Tip — Compare several similar listings on Aircraft2Sell before committing, in either country: the volume of available listings often provides a reliable benchmark for price and overall market condition.

Which country to choose for your profile

Germany is particularly well suited to a buyer who wants a wide choice of comparable aircraft, a dense maintenance network and an active resale market to secure a future exit. Croatia will appeal more to a buyer looking for an aircraft suited to coastal and tourist flying, willing to accept a more limited supply in exchange for a sometimes less contested market.

In either case, the reasoning stays the same: compare several listings at equivalent engine potential, have an independent pre-purchase inspection carried out, and verify the full consistency of the technical file before signing, regardless of the registration country of the aircraft you are considering.
