Wood-and-fabric vs metal vs composite structure on light aircraft

Wood-and-fabric, metal or composite structure on a light aircraft: maintenance, weight and repair cost differences, and how they affect a used purchase.

Why structure shapes the ownership experience

A used light aircraft can be built on one of three main structural families: wood-and-fabric, metal (riveted sheet) or composite (fiberglass or carbon fiber). This structural choice has very concrete consequences on day-to-day maintenance, the cost of any repair, and the aircraft's tolerance to storage conditions. Two aircraft of the same model can require very different maintenance budgets depending on the actual condition of their structure, far more than on manufacturing date alone.

Before comparing specific models against each other, it helps to know which structural family you're willing to commit to: this significantly narrows your search and avoids unpleasant surprises after the purchase.

The used market mixes very different generations: a 1950s-1960s wood-and-fabric aircraft, a conventional metal production single-engine, and a more recent composite light aircraft, sometimes built from an experimental kit, can appear side by side in the same classified listings at comparable prices, despite very different maintenance demands. Comparing only the advertised price or total flight hours without accounting for structure often leads to underestimating the real cost of ownership.

Wood-and-fabric structures on light aircraft

Many older light aircraft, particularly training or leisure types built in the mid-twentieth century, use a wooden structure (spars and ribs) covered with doped fabric. This construction remains attractive for its light weight and a purchase cost that is often lower for comparable performance.

Maintaining a wood-and-fabric structure requires particular vigilance: moisture and fungal decay affecting the wood, fabric aging under UV exposure requiring periodic re-covering, and checking the integrity of glued joints. Storage in a ventilated hangar, protected from moisture, is almost essential to preserve the structure over time.

  • Advantages: light weight, often a lower purchase cost, historical character for certain older models.
  • Constraints: sensitivity to moisture, periodic re-covering, check the availability of specialized workshops in your region.

Riveted metal structures

Most production light aircraft from the 1950s-1960s onward (flying club single-engine types in particular) use a riveted aluminum alloy structure. This construction offers good resistance to ground handling, a relatively accessible visual inspection, and wide availability of spare parts and mechanics trained on this type of structure.

The main point of vigilance remains corrosion, particularly in less visible areas (fuselage underside, wing root, around rivets) and on aircraft that have spent long periods outdoors or near the coast. A corrosion assessment is one of the essential steps of a serious pre-purchase inspection on this type of structure.

  • Advantages: ruggedness, a wide network of mechanics and parts, a good compromise for club or intensive rental use.
  • Constraints: corrosion to watch in hidden areas, aerodynamic finish sometimes less refined than composite.

Composite structures

Composite structures (fiberglass, carbon fiber for certain highly loaded parts) have developed on modern light aircraft and certain experimental kits, offering optimized aerodynamic shapes and a potential weight saving over metal at equal strength.

The trade-off is a specific inspection requirement: delamination, blistering from moisture ingress, micro-cracking of the gelcoat, and resin aging under prolonged UV exposure. These defects are not always detectable to the naked eye and may require acoustic tapping or the involvement of a composite-specialist workshop. A structural composite repair generally costs more than an equivalent repair on a conventional metal structure.

  • Advantages: optimized aerodynamic shapes, refined finish, the standard for many modern light aircraft and kits.
  • Constraints: delamination and blistering not always visible, costlier repairs, storage away from UV exposure required.
Watch out — On a light aircraft with a composite or mixed structure, check whether an airworthiness directive or manufacturer service bulletin related to structural aging applies to the model, and ask for proof it has been addressed in the maintenance logbook.

Which structure for which use

For flight school use or intensive rental with many different pilots, a metal structure is often the simplest to operate: ruggedness against ground-handling impacts, and a wide network of parts and mechanics. For travel or performance use, where weight savings and aerodynamic efficiency matter more, composite becomes a strong argument, provided you accept a more demanding inspection requirement.

Wood-and-fabric structures remain relevant for a tight acquisition budget or a search for an aircraft with historical character, provided you accept closer upkeep and confirm the availability of a competent workshop before buying.

Some light aircraft combine several materials within the same airframe (a metal fuselage with composite wings, for example, or a wood structure locally reinforced with metal parts). In these mixed cases, each area of the aircraft should be assessed against the criteria proper to its own material, and the technical file should clearly identify what was modified, when, and under which approval procedure.

What a pre-purchase inspection should target by structure

Whatever structure you choose, a pre-purchase inspection carried out by an independent licensed mechanic remains the best protection against unpleasant surprises. The priority points differ, however: moisture and fungal decay for wood, hidden corrosion for metal, delamination and blistering for composite. The maintenance logbook should document past work on these specific points, not just cumulative flight hours or engine hours since the last major overhaul.

Tip — Never let the seller's recommended mechanic carry out the inspection: choose an independent provider, ideally one familiar with the exact structure type of the aircraft you're targeting.

Impact on running costs and resale

Beyond the purchase, structure influences the running maintenance budget over the whole ownership period and ease of resale. Metal aircraft benefit from a larger market of parts and mechanics, making day-to-day maintenance easier over the years. Modern composite aircraft, often higher-performing, find steady demand on resale but require specialized maintenance that can limit the number of competent workshops depending on the region. Wood-and-fabric aircraft retain a niche market, driven by enthusiasts specifically seeking that type of construction.

Whatever choice you ultimately make, the consistency and completeness of the technical file remain the best indicator of the real quality of a used light aircraft. A buyer comparing two aircraft of the same model, one perfectly documented and the other with gaps in its history, should treat the second as significantly riskier, regardless of the structural material advertised in the listing.
