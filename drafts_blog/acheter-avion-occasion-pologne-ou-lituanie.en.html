Buying a Used Aircraft in Poland or Lithuania: Which Country to Choose

Market, prices, tax, registration and maintenance: a side-by-side look at Poland and Lithuania to help you decide where to focus your used aircraft search.

Two aviation markets with very different profiles

Poland and Lithuania are both EU and EASA member states, but their light aviation used markets differ considerably in size and history. Poland has a long-established aerospace industry (PZL, Margański & Mysłowski) and a highly developed network of flying clubs, with a strong tradition in aerobatics and gliding. Lithuania, with a much smaller population, is better known in aviation circles for its commercial aircraft leasing and trading industry centred on Vilnius than for a recreational light-aircraft fleet comparable to its Polish neighbour's.

For a buyer of a used light aircraft or microlight, this structural difference matters: Poland offers a markedly wider choice of touring aircraft, trainers and aerobatic types, while Lithuania presents a narrower but occasionally interesting supply of aircraft that have passed through the regional professional market or belong to private owners connected to neighbouring Scandinavian countries.

Size and character of the used market

The Polish used market is large and varied: microlights, trainers, touring single-engines, aerobatic aircraft and motor-gliders all circulate in meaningful numbers, with healthy listing turnover thanks to a sizeable pilot community and a still-active domestic manufacturing base. Poland remains one of the most active markets in Central Europe for recreational light aviation.

The Lithuanian market, much smaller in volume due to a far smaller pilot population, offers a more targeted supply. Listings are fewer, but a patient buyer can find well-maintained aircraft, sometimes on attractive terms, partly thanks to regular exchange with the neighbouring Latvian, Estonian and Polish markets.

Comparing the prices charged

For a comparable model in similar maintenance condition, prices in Poland generally sit close to the rest of Central Europe, with maintenance labour often cheaper than in Western Europe. In Lithuania, the narrower supply can cut both ways: some listings trade at attractive levels for lack of deep local demand, while rarer aircraft can command a premium simply because there is no domestic alternative on offer.

Lithuania adopted the euro back in 2015, which simplifies price comparisons with France or Germany. Poland has kept the zloty: always convert at the current exchange rate before comparing a Polish listing with a Lithuanian one, or with any other listing priced in euros.

Tax, VAT and currency

Since Poland and Lithuania are both EU member states, a transaction between the two countries falls under the intra-EU acquisition regime rather than an import from outside the EU, which simplifies customs procedures. VAT treatment depends above all on the seller's status (private individual or VAT-registered professional), not solely on the country where the aircraft happens to be: a sale between private individuals is generally not subject to VAT, while a sale by a professional may be.

Lithuania uses the euro as its official currency, which makes payments and listings easier to read for a buyer in the eurozone. Poland keeps the zloty (PLN), with each country applying its own national VAT rate under the harmonised European regime. For the specific case of an aircraft, it is advisable to have the applicable tax treatment confirmed with the relevant authority in each country (Krajowa Administracja Skarbowa in Poland, Valstybinė mokesčių inspekcija in Lithuania) before any cross-border transaction.

Registration: ULC versus CAA Lithuania

In Poland, the competent authority for aircraft registration is the Urząd Lotnictwa Cywilnego (ULC), which issues registrations under the SP- prefix. In Lithuania, the competent authority is the Civil Aviation Administration of Lithuania (Civilinės aviacijos administracija, CAA Lithuania), which issues registrations under the LY- prefix. An aircraft bought in either country can be re-registered in the other depending on the buyer's choice: keeping the registration of the purchase country is not mandatory.

  • For a transfer to either Poland or Lithuania, prior deregistration from the register of origin is generally required before the new registration.
  • The Certificate of Airworthiness and its airworthiness review certificate (ARC) must be valid and recognised by the destination authority.
  • A liability insurance certificate covering operation in the destination country is required before first use under the new registration.
Good to know — Common EASA rules govern the transfer between EU member states and make it simpler than an import from outside the EU. Generally allow 2 to 8 weeks depending on how complete the file submitted to both authorities is.

Maintenance network and parts availability

Poland has a maintenance workshop network spread across the whole country, with good parts availability for domestically built types (PZL, Margański & Mysłowski) thanks to industrial proximity, as well as relatively easy access to parts for common Western single-engines. Lithuania has a smaller number of approved workshops, concentrated around its main airfields (Vilnius, Kaunas, Šiauliai), which can occasionally stretch out turnaround times for a specialised job or for sourcing a rarer part.

For a Western-built aircraft (Cessna, Piper, Diamond), parts in both countries are generally sourced through Western European distributors, with broadly comparable lead times. Poland still keeps the edge in sheer numbers: more workshops means more available slots and an easier time finding a second technical opinion if something looks doubtful during a pre-purchase inspection.

The continental climate shared by both countries, with cold, damp winters, warrants particular attention to winter storage conditions and possible corrosion during the pre-purchase inspection, regardless of the aircraft's ultimate country of origin.

Language, paperwork and buying logistics

The language barrier can weigh into the choice of country: listings and technical documentation are most often written in Polish or Lithuanian, even though English is widely understood in professional aviation circles in both countries, particularly in Lithuania, where the international aircraft leasing sector has built a strong culture of technical English. Arranging translation of key documents (logbook, engine logbook, maintenance invoices) remains a useful precaution before negotiating.

Logistically, the distance between the two countries is more significant than between two direct neighbours: budget roughly ten hours by road between Warsaw and Vilnius, or under two hours by direct flight. For a pre-purchase inspection or ferry flight, it is worth planning this trip ahead and, where possible, grouping several aircraft viewings into the same journey to spread the cost of travel.

Which country to favour depending on your profile

For a buyer looking for a trainer, an aerobatic aircraft, a microlight or a classic single-engine with a wide choice, the Polish market generally remains the richest in Central Europe thanks to its domestic industry and extensive light aviation fleet. For a buyer willing to widen the search to a narrower but potentially less contested market, and comfortable dealing in euros, Lithuania can occasionally turn up a good opportunity, particularly for a well-maintained aircraft that has had a single owner.

Good to know — In all cases, a pre-purchase inspection by a qualified independent mechanic remains essential, whether the aircraft is located in Poland, Lithuania or elsewhere in Europe.

Ultimately, the country of purchase matters less than the actual condition of the aircraft and how well it has been maintained. Comparing listings across both markets widens your options and gives you a stronger negotiating position, whether on price or on the terms of the transaction.
