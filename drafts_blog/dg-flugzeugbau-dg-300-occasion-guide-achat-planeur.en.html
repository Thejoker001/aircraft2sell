The DG-300, a club glider benchmark

Designed by Glaser-Dirks and later produced under the DG Flugzeugbau brand, the DG-300 became one of the most widespread single-seat performance gliders in Europe from the mid-1980s onward. Drawing on the lineage of the DG-100 and DG-200, it uses an all-composite airframe (fibreglass, with carbon reinforcement on certain structural parts) paired with a wingspan of either 15 or 17.2 metres depending on which wingtip set is fitted. That flexibility — the same fuselage accepting 15 m wings for Standard Class competition or 17.2 m extensions to maximise glide performance — explains much of its commercial success and why so many examples are still flying today.

The DG-300 was built primarily for the club pilot or private owner looking for a capable performance glider that is straightforward to handle on the ground and in the air, with running costs that stay reasonable given its handling qualities. Its glide ratio, roughly 40:1 depending on configuration and conditions, places it among the more accessible performance gliders, well above a basic trainer but without the budget demands of a large-span Open Class machine.

For a buyer new to shopping for a performance glider, the DG-300 is often a pragmatic choice: the fleet in service is large, spare parts remain available through DG Flugzeugbau's dealer network, and clubs and approved workshops alike are familiar with the type, which makes routine maintenance and mandatory periodic inspections considerably easier to arrange.

The Elan and Club Elan versions

Two main variants show up on the used market. The DG-300 Elan is the most common version, fitted with a standard cockpit and designed for versatile use spanning club flying, cross-country tasks and regional competition. The DG-300 Club Elan is a simplified variant aimed at heavier club use, with slightly lighter equipment and finish than the Elan, which usually translates into a somewhat lower used price for a comparable condition.

Both versions are typically fitted with a retractable landing gear, an effective airbrake and the ability to carry water ballast in the wings to tune the glider's weight for the thermal conditions of the day — standard features for this class of performance glider. Before deciding, confirm precisely which version you are looking at, since that affects resale value, the availability of certain specific parts, and eligibility for some competition categories.

Some examples have also been modified over the years (alternative wingtips, modern instrumentation, a replaced canopy): these changes must be properly documented in the logbook and covered by the manufacturer's applicable technical bulletins, or a future airworthiness review can become complicated.

What budget to plan for

The price of a used DG-300 varies considerably depending on the age of the airframe, total flight hours, the condition of the canopy and landing gear, the avionics fitted (electronic variometer, FLARM, competition GPS) and whether the water ballast system is present and working. The oldest, least equipped examples trade well below well-maintained gliders carrying modern avionics and a complete, consistent maintenance history.

  • Compare several listings for a similar overall condition before fixing a realistic price range, as the performance glider market remains fairly specialised and international.
  • Factor the cost of a suitable road trailer into the total budget if one is not included in the sale, a line item first-time buyers often underestimate.
  • Set aside a reserve for canopy or paint refurbishment, two items that degrade over time even on a well-maintained aircraft.
  • Anticipate the cost of a full periodic inspection if one falls due shortly after purchase.
Good to know — A certified road trailer in good condition, recent avionics and a complete, consistent logbook usually justify a significant price gap over an example lacking these elements.

What to check before buying

A pre-purchase inspection of a DG-300 follows the same logic as for any composite glider, with a few points specific to this model. The logbook should show the full history of the airframe: total flight hours, dates and content of periodic inspections, any noted structural repairs, and manufacturer technical bulletins applied or still outstanding.

  • Inspect the canopy for cracks, yellowing or sealing issues, a costly item to replace on this model.
  • Check the play and smooth operation of the retractable landing gear and the condition of the bearings and tailwheel.
  • Confirm the water ballast system is sealed and functioning correctly, a frequent source of small leaks on older examples.
  • Inspect the wing root fittings and attachment pins, points subject to repeated stress over years of flying.
  • Have a professional experienced with composite gliders check for delamination or undeclared impact damage across the fuselage and wings.

As with any used aircraft, this inspection should be entrusted to an independent approved mechanic or workshop, ideally one already familiar with the DG-300, rather than relying solely on the seller or the buyer's own assessment.

Composite structure: ageing and repairs

The DG-300's airframe is built from composite materials, a technology that holds up well over time when the glider is stored correctly, but which remains sensitive to a few specific ageing factors: prolonged UV exposure without adequate protection, large humidity swings from unsuitable storage, or minor undeclared impacts during ground handling.

A repair history is not necessarily a reason to walk away: a structural repair that is properly documented, carried out by an approved workshop and compliant with the manufacturer's procedures can perfectly well guarantee the aircraft's long-term soundness. What should raise a flag instead is a visible anomaly with no supporting documentation, or signs of an undocumented, home-made repair that never made it into the logbook.

Always ask how the aircraft was stored over the years (closed hangar, trailer, outdoor exposure); the answer gives a useful indication of the likely condition of the structure, as a complement to the technical inspection itself.

Maintenance, periodic inspections and parts

Like any certified glider, the DG-300 is subject to a periodic inspection programme whose frequency and content depend on the applicable maintenance regulation and the manufacturer's recommendations. These inspections cover the structure, flight controls, landing gear and safety equipment, and must be carried out by qualified personnel in line with the regulations in force in the country of registration.

DG Flugzeugbau's dealer network across Europe keeps spare parts specific to the model available, which makes routine maintenance considerably easier than on older or very low-production gliders. Clubs already operating one or more DG-300s in their fleet are also an excellent source of practical, day-to-day maintenance experience.

Annual running costs for a DG-300 remain generally moderate compared with an Open Class glider or a motor glider, since there is no engine to maintain. The main cost items are the periodic inspection, routine upkeep of the landing gear and controls, and the occasional refurbishment of paint or canopy over the years.

Resale and the used market

The DG-300 enjoys an active, well-established resale market across Europe, supported by the large fleet still in service and its reputation as a reliable, capable performance glider for a manageable budget. A well-maintained example with a complete logbook, up-to-date avionics and, ideally, a suitable trailer generally sells without much difficulty to club pilots or private owners looking for a first performance glider.

To maximise resale value, keeping a rigorous record of every periodic inspection, every repair and every modification made to the aircraft is by far the most decisive factor, well ahead of the airframe's age on its own. Comparing similar listings before setting an asking price, just as before buying, helps establish a realistic range matched to the aircraft's actual condition.
