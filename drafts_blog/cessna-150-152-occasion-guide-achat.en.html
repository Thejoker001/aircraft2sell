Why the 150/152 remains a benchmark for beginners

The Cessna 150 and its successor the Cessna 152 rank among the most-produced and most widespread training aircraft in the world. Designed as simple, economical and rugged two-seaters, they have trained generations of private pilots since the 1950s. Their classic metal airframe, well-understood mechanics and the abundance of spare parts make them particularly accessible aircraft for a first aircraft ownership purchase or for entering a shared ownership arrangement.

On the used market, the 150/152 stands out with an acquisition budget noticeably lower than four-seat single-engine aircraft like the Cessna 172, while keeping some of the lowest operating costs in light aviation. It's often the preferred choice for flying regularly without breaking the bank, or for testing aircraft ownership before considering a larger aircraft.

150 or 152: what are the differences

The Cessna 150, produced until the mid-1970s, is fitted with a Continental O-200 engine of around 100 hp. The Cessna 152, which succeeded it, shares a very similar airframe but adopts a more powerful Lycoming O-235 engine, a slightly different propeller and a few structural and equipment updates. In practice, the two aircraft fly in a very similar way and share most of their mechanics, which makes maintenance and parts availability easier.

The 152 generally shows slightly better performance and a somewhat higher useful load, which explains a used price often a little higher than a comparable 150. The choice between the two mostly depends on local market availability and the actual condition of the aircraft in question, rather than any inherent preference for one model over the other.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used Cessna 150 or 152 depends above all on airframe condition, the number of engine hours remaining before the major overhaul (SMOH), the quality of installed avionics, and how well maintenance has been documented in the logbook. Aircraft close to their engine limit or requiring airframe rework trade at a very accessible entry level, while a recently overhauled, well-equipped example represents a larger investment, while remaining affordable relative to the light aviation market as a whole.

Beyond the purchase price, your budget should include ancillary costs: an independent mechanic's pre-purchase inspection, registration fees, insurance and, if needed, an avionics or cabin interior refresh.

What to check before buying

As with any used aircraft, a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic qualified on the type is essential before finalising a transaction. For a 150/152, whose airframe can be several decades old, a few points deserve particular attention.

  • Airframe corrosion, especially around the spars, floors and areas exposed to moisture, common on older aircraft.
  • Complete engine history: hours elapsed since the last major overhaul (SMOH), compliance with the maintenance program, and traceability of replaced parts.
  • Condition of the landing gear and tyres, regularly stressed by intensive flight school or club use.
  • Operation of the avionics and compliance with current regulatory requirements (transponder, radio communication equipment).
  • Up-to-date logbook and Certificate of Airworthiness (CofA), with no grey areas in the aircraft's history.
Good to know — A Cessna 150/152 that has flown a lot of hours in a flight school is not necessarily a bad purchase: intensive but well-documented, well-maintained use is often preferable to a lightly used but poorly tracked aircraft.

Maintenance and operating costs

One of the main strengths of the Cessna 150/152 remains its operating cost, among the lowest in light aviation: low fuel consumption, simple mechanics well understood by mechanics everywhere, and plentiful new and used spare parts on the market. The main expense items remain the mandatory annual inspection, insurance, parking and, in the longer term, the engine's major overhaul once the hourly potential is reached.

Having the aircraft maintained at a shop familiar with the type helps limit unpleasant surprises and preserve its resale value. Rigorous, well-documented maintenance records remain a strong argument at a future resale, even for an economical aircraft.

Tip — Always ask for the most recent maintenance invoices and the exact remaining engine potential before making an offer: these are the two factors that weigh most on the real price.

Club use, shared ownership or training

The Cessna 150/152 remains a top choice for private pilot training, thanks to its simple handling and moderate hourly cost. It's also well suited to shared ownership between several pilots, which spreads fixed costs (insurance, hangar, maintenance) while allowing regular flying.

For personal use, the 150/152 is first and foremost a two-seater suited to local or medium-distance flights, with a limited useful load that generally does not allow two adults plus baggage on long trips. This is worth factoring in if your intended use goes beyond proficiency flying or discovery flights.

Resale and the used market

The Cessna 150/152 benefits from a very active, international used market, supported by a considerable installed fleet and good parts availability. A well-maintained aircraft, with a complete logbook and a transparent history, generally resells easily, particularly to clubs, flight schools or future private owners looking for a first aircraft.

Comparing several listings of similar models and model years remains the best way to assess a fair price. In this entry-level segment, price differences between two comparable aircraft are almost always explained by remaining engine potential and actual airframe condition, rather than avionics or options.
