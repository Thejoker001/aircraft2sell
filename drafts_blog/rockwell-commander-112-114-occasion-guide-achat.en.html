The Commander 112/114: an underrated but solid single-engine

The Rockwell Commander 112 and its later development, the 114, hold a particular place in the used piston single-engine market: less common than the Cessna or Piper models in the same category, they nonetheless appeal to attentive buyers thanks to their wide, comfortable cabin, a sturdy retractable-gear structure, and flight handling often described as stable and pleasant. Originally designed by Rockwell International and carried on under other brands through successive changes in the program's ownership, these aircraft benefit from a carefully engineered design despite more limited production numbers than their main competitors.

That more limited production is a double-edged sword for a buyer: it often means a more accessible purchase price for comparable equipment, but it also calls for extra care regarding parts availability and how familiar the local maintenance network is with the type — two criteria worth assessing before getting started.

In terms of flying qualities, current and former Commander owners regularly highlight the cabin width, wider than many direct competitors in the same power category, along with good overall visibility from the cockpit. These qualities make it a particularly appreciated aircraft for family trips or journeys with four occupants over medium distances, where cruise comfort remains an important selection criterion for many buyers.

112, 114 and their variants: how to tell them apart

The Commander 112 was the original version, followed by the 112A and then reinforced versions, before the arrival of the 114, which brought a more powerful engine and better overall performance. Several sub-versions followed one another with differences in engine power, avionics and interior fit, creating a relatively varied used-market offering despite a limited total number of aircraft built.

Before starting any search, it's worth precisely identifying the version and sub-version of the aircraft in question: engine power, payload capacity and certain standard equipment vary meaningfully from one variant to another, which directly affects the budget and the use the aircraft is best suited for.

What budget should you plan for

The price of a used Commander 112/114 depends above all on the aircraft's age, the engine hours remaining before the next major overhaul, the avionics equipment level, and the overall condition of the airframe and interior. The earlier 112 versions, now older, generally trade at a more accessible entry level than the later, better-equipped 114s, which remain sought after for their superior performance.

Beyond the purchase price, your budget should also factor in additional costs: a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic, registration and transfer fees, insurance, and possibly an avionics or interior upgrade if the aircraft needs one.

Points to check before buying

As with any used aircraft, a pre-purchase inspection carried out by an independent mechanic qualified on the type is essential before closing any deal. For a Commander 112/114, certain points deserve particular attention.

  • Operation and overall condition of the retractable landing gear, a mechanical system that requires rigorous maintenance tracking.
  • Complete engine history: hours elapsed since the last overhaul, compliance with the manufacturer's maintenance program, traceability of replaced parts.
  • Checking for corrosion on the metal structure, particularly on older or lightly used examples.
  • Condition and updates of the installed avionics, as well as compliance with current regulatory requirements.
  • Complete logbook and technical file, with no grey areas in the aircraft's history.
Good to know — Never rely solely on the seller's statements about overall condition: a written independent inspection report remains the best protection against an unpleasant surprise after purchase.

Parts availability and the maintenance network

The more limited production of the Commander 112/114 compared with its main competitors means you should check, before any purchase, the real availability of spare parts and the existence of a maintenance shop familiar with the type in your area. While overall manufacturer support and parts stocks remain generally satisfactory for routine maintenance, some more specific parts may require longer lead times than on a more common model.

Before buying, it's advisable to contact a shop already familiar with the type or an owners' association, which can provide information on real parts availability and on any specific points of attention known for this model.

Maintenance and operating costs

The operating cost of a Commander 112/114 remains broadly comparable to other retractable-gear single-engine aircraft in its category, with particular attention needed for landing gear maintenance, which is more complex than a fixed gear. The main expense items remain the mandatory annual inspection, insurance, parking, and, over the longer term, engine overhaul once the hour potential is reached.

Having the aircraft maintained at an approved shop familiar with the type — even if it sometimes means travelling further than for a more common model — helps limit unpleasant surprises and preserve resale value.

Tip — Always ask for the last three maintenance invoices before making an offer: they give an accurate picture of how seriously the aircraft has been maintained, a point that matters even more on a less common model.

Resale and the used market

The used market for the Commander 112/114 is smaller than that of its main competitors, which can extend the time it takes to resell but also offers, at purchase, a price-to-equipment ratio that is often more favourable for a patient buyer. A well-maintained aircraft, with a transparent history, a clear engine record and up-to-date avionics, stands out clearly in this niche market and generally resells on better terms.

Comparing several available listings, including beyond your home market, remains the best way to assess a fair price for this less common model than its direct competitors, both for buying and for preparing a future resale.

Joining an owners' and pilots' association for the Commander, where one exists in your country or at a European level, is also a significant asset both for buying and for owning the aircraft: these networks often centralize good knowledge of known weak points of the type, maintenance best practices and trusted shop contacts, which can save a new buyer discovering this model for the first time a considerable amount of time.
