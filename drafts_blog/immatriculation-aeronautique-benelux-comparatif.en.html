Aircraft Registration in the Benelux: Belgium, Netherlands, Luxembourg Compared

Three countries, three authorities, three prefixes: how to decide where to register your aircraft within the Benelux area, and what to expect from each process.

Why compare the three Benelux systems

Belgium, the Netherlands and Luxembourg sit within a tight airspace, often only a short flight apart, yet each keeps its own civil aviation authority and its own registration paperwork. For a buyer based in one of these countries, or anyone weighing up registering outside their home jurisdiction, knowing the practical differences between the three registers up front avoids surprises on timing and cost once an aircraft deal is already underway.

All three apply the same EASA regulatory baseline, which makes moving an aircraft between Benelux registers far more straightforward than importing from outside the European Union. The real differences sit in local administrative setup, processing speed, and, to a lesser degree, the tax treatment that depends on the status of the seller rather than the country itself.

Registering in Belgium

In Belgium, civil aircraft registration is handled by the Direction Générale Transport Aérien (DGTA), operating under federal mobility authority. Aircraft registered in Belgium carry the OO- prefix followed by three letters. For its size, the country has a dense network of flying clubs and airfields, with meaningful general aviation and microlight activity around fields such as Spa-La Sauvenière, Saint-Hubert and Kiewit.

  • Transfer file — bill of sale, a valid certificate of airworthiness, and, for an aircraft coming from another country, proof of deregistration from its previous register.
  • Insurance certificate — valid third-party liability cover before the first flight under an OO- registration.
  • Compliance check — the aircraft must meet the EASA requirements applicable to its category to obtain or keep its certificate of airworthiness.

Registering in the Netherlands

In the Netherlands, the Inspectie Leefomgeving en Transport (ILT), part of the Dutch Ministry of Infrastructure, keeps the civil register and assigns the PH- prefix. The Dutch light aviation market is particularly active around airfields such as Lelystad, Teuge and Hilversum, with a strong share of touring single-engine aircraft and a lively microlight scene, partly a consequence of how tightly controlled and limited the country's airspace is.

The transfer process into the Dutch register follows the same logic as its neighbours: deregistration from the previous register for an imported aircraft, a complete file (bill of sale, certificate of airworthiness, insurance proof), then registration with the ILT. The density of controlled airspace around Schiphol and the military zones also means buyers should pay close attention to the avionics (transponder, radio) actually required to operate the aircraft once registered, not just to get it on the register.

Good to know — As with Belgium and Luxembourg, the shared EASA framework governs transfers between Benelux member states and makes them considerably simpler than an import from a country outside the European Union.

Registering in Luxembourg

In Luxembourg, the competent authority is the Direction de l'Aviation Civile (DAC), and aircraft registered in the country carry the LX- prefix. Given the country's size, the light aviation market is naturally smaller than in Belgium or the Netherlands, but Luxembourg retains notable business aviation activity and an active register for light aircraft based at its main airfield, Luxembourg-Findel, or at nearby Belgian and French fields close to the border.

  • Broadly the same file — a dated, signed bill of sale, a valid certificate of airworthiness, and a third-party liability insurance certificate.
  • Prior deregistration — required whenever the aircraft comes from another register, whether Benelux or not.

VAT and tax treatment across the three countries

In all three countries, the general tax logic follows the European VAT rules applicable to movable goods: a sale between private individuals resident in the European Union is generally exempt from local VAT, while a sale carried out by a professional or a company may be subject to VAT depending on the regime applicable to the transaction. An aircraft imported from outside the European Union remains subject to import VAT, calculated on its customs value, regardless of which Benelux country is chosen for registration.

Point of caution — The country where an aircraft is registered is not automatically the same as the buyer's tax residence or the VAT regime applicable to the sale: have your situation confirmed by a professional before signing, particularly if you live in a different Benelux country from the one where you plan to register the aircraft.

Paperwork common to all three

Beyond the specifics of each authority, a common set of documents is systematically requested in all three Benelux countries before any registration or transfer:

  • Bill of sale — dated, signed by both parties, stating the aircraft's serial number.
  • Certificate of airworthiness (CofA) and its associated airworthiness review certificate, both valid.
  • Consistent logbook with no chronological gaps, stating engine hours including SMOH since the last major overhaul.
  • Proof of deregistration from the previous register for any aircraft already registered elsewhere.
  • Third-party liability insurance certificate in force before first use under the new registration.

Which country fits your situation

The choice of registration country depends mainly on where you live, where the aircraft will actually be based day to day, and, in some cases, on tax considerations specific to your personal or professional situation. There is no single Benelux regime that is universally "better": all three administrations apply the same European regulatory baseline, and the practical differences mostly come down to proximity to your home airfield and local administrative responsiveness when your file is submitted.

Whichever Benelux country you settle on, an independent pre-purchase inspection and a careful review of the logbooks remain the strongest protection before finalising the registration of the aircraft.
