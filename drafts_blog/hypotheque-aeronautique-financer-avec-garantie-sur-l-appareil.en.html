Aircraft mortgage: financing with the aircraft as collateral

How an aircraft mortgage works, how it differs from an unsecured loan, and what to check before putting your aircraft up as collateral for financing.

What an aircraft mortgage is

An aircraft mortgage is a real security interest that lets a lender secure its loan against the aircraft itself in the event the borrower defaults. In practice, the financed aircraft (or sometimes another aircraft already owned by the buyer) serves as collateral: if repayments stop, the creditor can enforce its claim against the aircraft to recover the debt, under a procedure governed by the relevant registration authority.

This mechanism is conceptually close to a real-estate mortgage: ownership of the aircraft stays with the buyer, but a lien is recorded against the title and must be released once the loan has been fully repaid. For a buyer who has no other asset to pledge, or who wants to avoid tying up real estate to finance an aircraft purchase, this solution clearly separates the financing of the aircraft from the rest of their personal assets.

Aircraft mortgages can apply to the purchase of a new or used light aircraft just as much as a helicopter, a turboprop or a business jet, whenever the amount borrowed and the value of the aircraft justify, from the bank's or lender's point of view, putting in place a real security interest rather than a simple unsecured loan.

How the collateral on the aircraft works

In practice, setting up an aircraft mortgage follows several steps that differ clearly from a standard unsecured loan. Before releasing the funds, the lender generally commissions a valuation of the aircraft, to make sure its market value comfortably covers the amount borrowed throughout the financing term, taking expected depreciation into account.

Once the loan is approved in principle, the mortgage is recorded with the aircraft's registration authority, which differs depending on the country of registration chosen. This filing makes the security interest enforceable against third parties: a prospective buyer who checks the register before a resale would see the charge encumbering the aircraft, which is why releasing the mortgage is a mandatory step before any sale.

  • Valuation of the aircraft, usually at the borrower's expense.
  • Filing of the security interest with the relevant registration authority.
  • Insurance covering the value of the aircraft, with the lender named as beneficiary in the event of a total loss.
  • Release of the mortgage once the loan is fully repaid, formally recorded with the register.

Throughout the loan term, the aircraft remains usable as normal by its owner, subject to the contract's conditions (maintenance consistent with the logbook, insurance kept in force, notifying the lender of any change of base or main country of operation).

Eligibility and typical buyer profiles

An aircraft mortgage is primarily aimed at buyers financing a significant share of the aircraft's price, typically above a threshold where the bank wants a real security interest rather than a simple personal commitment. Typical profiles range from the private individual buying a light touring aircraft to a professional operator renewing its fleet.

Lenders review several criteria before granting this type of financing: whether the estimated value of the aircraft is consistent with the amount borrowed, the aircraft's age and remaining engine potential (an aircraft close to a major overhaul carries a different depreciation risk), the intended use (personal, club, rental, commercial operation), and of course the borrower's financial standing.

The down payment required varies considerably depending on the profile and aircraft type, but it is rare for collateral-backed financing to cover the full purchase price: a down payment limits the lender's exposure to the rapid depreciation that can follow entry into service or a hasty resale.

Costs, rates and related fees

The total cost of an aircraft mortgage includes several components that need to be added together before comparing offers. On top of the loan's interest rate come the fees for filing the security interest with the register, the cost of the aircraft valuation, any arrangement fees charged by the lender, and the cost of the insurance required to maintain the coverage level demanded by the loan agreement.

Cost itemNature
Loan interest rateVaries with term, borrower profile and amount financed

Comparing several lenders on the basis of the total cost of credit, not just the advertised rate, remains the best way to assess how competitive a collateral-backed financing offer really is.

Documents and paperwork to prepare

Putting together a solid file speeds up the loan approval process. Lenders generally ask for a detailed presentation of the purchase project, precise identification of the aircraft (registration, serial number, maintenance logbook), plus the usual financial documentation required for a large loan.

  • Quote or purchase agreement detailing the aircraft and its price.
  • Flight logbook and engine logbook for a used aircraft.
  • Currently valid certificate of airworthiness (CofA).
  • Pre-purchase inspection report, often required by the lender itself.
  • Proof of income and financial standing of the borrower.
  • Insurance certificate covering the aircraft's value as soon as it is pledged.

Preparing these documents in advance can save several weeks on the overall timeline between signing the purchase agreement and the actual delivery of the aircraft.

Risks and pitfalls to avoid

From the borrower's point of view, the main risk of an aircraft mortgage lies in depreciation of the aircraft. If market value drops faster than expected — following a regulatory change affecting a given aircraft type, an imminent major overhaul not factored into the valuation, or a used market downturn — the borrower can end up owing more on the loan than the aircraft is actually worth.

Word of caution — Always check the terms for early repayment and resale before the loan ends: some contracts impose penalties or require the lender's prior consent for any sale as long as the mortgage has not been released.

Another common pitfall is underestimating the time it takes to file and release a mortgage with the registration authority, which varies by country and can delay a sale or a refinancing. It is also essential to check that no prior mortgage already encumbers a used aircraft before purchasing it: a lien search with the relevant registry should always precede signing.

Alternatives to an aircraft mortgage

An aircraft mortgage is just one of several financing options available for buying a light aircraft, a helicopter or a business jet. Finance leasing, an unsecured personal loan for smaller amounts, or private-party financing can all be more suitable solutions depending on the buyer's profile and the amount involved.

Before settling on an option, it is worth comparing the total cost of each solution, the flexibility offered in case of early resale, and the consequences of default, which differ significantly between a real security interest on the aircraft and a simple personal commitment.

Tip — Get several financing quotes in parallel (mortgage, leasing, standard loan) before signing a purchase agreement, so you can negotiate from a position of strength with both the seller and the lender.
