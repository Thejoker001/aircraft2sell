Buying a Used Aircraft in Malta or the United Kingdom: Which Country to Choose

Market, prices, registration, taxation and pre-purchase inspection: a comparison between Malta and the United Kingdom to help you buy a used light aircraft with full knowledge of the facts.

Two very different markets

Malta and the United Kingdom present two very contrasting profiles for a buyer of a used aircraft. The United Kingdom has one of the most developed light aviation markets in Europe, with a dense network of airfields, a long-standing general aviation tradition and a large volume of listings covering every type of aircraft, from two-seat trainers to business jets.

Malta, on the other hand, is a much smaller market in terms of aircraft actually based on the island, but the Maltese register has established itself as a reference for aircraft registration, including for aircraft that are never physically based in the archipelago. Many buyers choose Malta primarily for the flexibility of its register and its tax framework, rather than to find the aircraft itself locally.

This distinction matters: buying "in Malta" or "in the United Kingdom" does not describe the same practical reality. In the United Kingdom, you are generally buying an aircraft physically present on UK territory. In Malta, you are often primarily choosing a flag of registration, while the aircraft may remain based elsewhere in Europe.

Price comparison by aircraft type

Prices in the United Kingdom for comparable aircraft remain broadly aligned with European standards, with an abundant supply that makes comparison easier. In Malta, the local market of aircraft physically based on the island is too small to establish reliable, specific price ranges: transactions associated with the Maltese register concern aircraft whose price is negotiated on the European or international market, independently of the chosen flag of registration.

Registration: the Maltese register versus the UK register

The UK register is managed by the Civil Aviation Authority (CAA), under the G- prefix. Since Brexit, the United Kingdom is no longer an EASA member for certification, but it retains a regulatory framework broadly harmonised with European standards for light aviation, with well-established registration transfer procedures for an aircraft already based on UK territory.

The Maltese register, under the 9H- prefix, is managed by the Malta Transport Authority (formerly Transport Malta). As Malta is a member of the European Union and of EASA, aircraft registered under this flag enjoy full recognition within the EASA area. The Maltese register is particularly known for its flexibility in accommodating complex ownership structures (companies, trusts, fractional ownership), which explains its appeal for certain business jets and higher-value aircraft, even when they are not based on the island.

  • UK register — suited to an aircraft physically based and operated in the United Kingdom, within a framework well known to local maintenance shops.
  • Maltese register — often chosen for its legal flexibility and EASA recognition, regardless of where the aircraft is actually based.
  • In both cases, prior deregistration from the register of origin remains necessary if the aircraft was previously registered elsewhere.
Good to know — Choosing the Maltese register for an aircraft based outside Malta is a common and fully legal practice within the EASA framework, but it requires a clear understanding of the resulting continuing-airworthiness oversight obligations (competent authority, approved continuing airworthiness management organisations).

VAT and taxation

A sale between private individuals in the United Kingdom is generally not subject to UK VAT, while a sale by a professional or a company may be subject to it under the applicable rules. An aircraft imported from the European Union or a third country remains subject to the duties and import VAT applicable on UK territory, a point that has become more complex since Brexit and deserves case-by-case verification.

Malta is known for a tax framework generally considered attractive for the ownership and operation of aircraft by certain professional structures, which partly explains the appeal of its register for business jets. The precise terms, however, depend heavily on the ownership structure chosen (company, fractional ownership, private or commercial use) and evolve with the regulations: it is essential to have your situation confirmed by a tax adviser specialised in aviation before any decision, rather than relying on generalities.

Pre-purchase inspection and documents

Whatever country of registration is ultimately chosen, a pre-purchase inspection by a mechanic or approved shop independent of the seller remains the best protection against a bad surprise, whether the aircraft is physically based in the United Kingdom or destined for the Maltese register from another country.

  • Up-to-date airframe, engine and propeller logbooks, with no chronological gaps, regardless of the intended flag of registration.
  • Valid certificate of airworthiness and consistency of the technical file with the history declared by the seller.
  • For an aircraft destined for the Maltese register, checking in advance the continuing airworthiness management organisation that will handle regulatory follow-up after the change of register.
Point of caution — Never finalise a purchase without having seen the original logbooks and having their consistency checked by a professional, and clarify in writing, before signing, what the aircraft's final country of registration will be.

Pitfalls to avoid on both sides

In the United Kingdom, the most common mistake since Brexit is underestimating the customs and tax formalities for an aircraft coming from the European Union, which are no longer treated as a simple intra-EU transfer. It is also worth checking the avionics' compliance with current navigation and traffic-surveillance requirements, which can differ slightly from those applicable within the European Union.

In Malta, the most common pitfall is underestimating the administrative complexity involved in choosing a suitable ownership structure (company, trust, fractional ownership), or neglecting the choice of the continuing airworthiness management organisation that will handle the aircraft's day-to-day follow-up. A poorly prepared Maltese registration can generate unexpected delays and additional costs.

Tip — Compare several similar listings on Aircraft2Sell before committing, and get support from a professional familiar with the chosen register (CAA or the Maltese authority) to secure the choice of country of registration ahead of signing.

Which country to choose depending on your project

For a buyer primarily looking for an aircraft that is physically available, with a large volume of listings and an active local market, the United Kingdom remains a destination of choice, particularly for light touring aircraft. For a buyer whose priority is the ownership structure, EASA recognition or legal flexibility, especially in the case of a business jet or an aircraft held through a company, the Maltese register deserves careful consideration, regardless of where the aircraft will actually be based.

In every case, the final decision should rest on a combined analysis of the aircraft's technical inspection and the ownership and tax structure best suited to your use, not on the flag of registration alone.
