Buying a used aircraft in France or Malta: which country to choose

Market size, prices, DGAC vs AAM registration, tax treatment and operating costs: a full comparison to help you choose between France and Malta for your used aircraft purchase.

Two markets of very different sizes

France has one of the most active and best-structured general aviation markets in Europe, with a dense network of airfields, hundreds of flying clubs, and an abundant supply of used aircraft across every category, from two-seat microlights to twin-engine touring aircraft. Buyers find a wide choice there and plenty of comparable listings to help assess a fair price.

Malta, by comparison, is a niche market. The island's small size and the limited number of civil airfields translate into a much smaller supply of used aircraft. Malta has, however, attracted growing aircraft registration activity over the past years (notably for business jets and commercially operated aircraft) thanks to a regulatory and tax framework considered attractive — making it a relevant market mainly for specific profiles rather than for buying a typical light touring aircraft.

Prices compared by aircraft type

In France, used-market price ranges are broadly aligned with the rest of the European Union for comparable aircraft, with enough supply to allow informed negotiation. In Malta, the low volume of listings makes a direct comparison harder: asking prices there depend more on local scarcity of supply than on a market dynamic comparable to France's.

Always compare at equivalent engine potential, including between a French aircraft and one based in Malta.

Registration: DGAC vs. AAM

In France, aircraft registration falls under the Direction générale de l'Aviation civile (DGAC), with the prefix F-. For a sale between individuals already registered in France, the transfer is simply an update of ownership. For an imported aircraft, deregistration from the original registry is required before the new registration.

In Malta, registration is handled by the Authority for Transport in Malta (the AAM registry), with the prefix 9H-. The Maltese registry has built a reputation for flexibility and generally fast processing times, which explains its appeal to some private and commercial operators, particularly in the business jet segment. For a simple touring aircraft intended to fly mainly in France, however, this registry offers little practical benefit over a standard DGAC registration.

  • Valid Certificate of Airworthiness (CofA) — check this before signing anything, regardless of the aircraft's original registry.
  • Bill of sale — dated, signed by both parties, mentioning the serial number.
  • Liability insurance certificate — required before first use under the new registration.
Good to know — Common EASA rules govern transfers between EU member states and make them simpler than importing from outside the EU, whether the aircraft ends up on the French or the Maltese register.

VAT and tax treatment compared

In France, a sale between private individuals is generally not subject to VAT; a sale by a professional or a company can be. Malta has built a particular reputation in aviation taxation, with specific schemes that have historically attracted private and business jet operating structures. These arrangements mainly concern companies and high-value aircraft operated in a professional or semi-commercial setting — they are not relevant for a standard purchase of a light leisure aircraft between individuals.

SituationTax treatment
Sale between EU resident individuals (France)Generally VAT-exempt
Sale by a professionalVAT potentially applicable

Caution — Never decide on a Maltese registration or tax structure based on a rumored "tax advantage": always have your situation validated by a specialized aviation lawyer, as the rules change regularly.

Climate, bases and operating costs

France offers a wide variety of climates and airfields, from the Atlantic coast to mountain ranges, with hangarage and maintenance costs that vary significantly by region. A hangar near Paris or on the Côte d'Azur typically costs more than one in a rural area.

Malta enjoys a mild Mediterranean climate favorable to year-round operation, but the very limited number of civil airfields (Malta International Airport is the main point of entry) severely restricts basing options for a light touring aircraft. Hangarage and routine maintenance costs there are generally higher than in France, due to the island's insularity and a thinner network of maintenance shops for light general aviation.

Pre-purchase inspection and paperwork

Regardless of the aircraft's country of origin, a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic unaffiliated with the seller remains the best protection against unpleasant surprises. It covers airframe condition, logbook consistency, maintenance history, and avionics compliance with current European requirements.

  • Airframe, engine and propeller logbooks up to date, with no chronological gaps, regardless of the original registry.
  • Maintenance invoices for the past three years.
  • For an aircraft based in Malta: check the real availability of an approved maintenance shop on-site for future upkeep, a point buyers sometimes underestimate.

Which country to choose based on your profile

For the vast majority of individual buyers looking for a light touring aircraft to be flown mainly in continental Europe, France remains the simplest and most economical choice: abundant supply, a dense network of maintenance shops, a well-known DGAC registration process, and controlled operating costs in most regions.

Malta makes sense mainly for specific profiles: owners or companies operating a high-value aircraft (particularly a business jet) seeking a particular regulatory and tax framework, or buyers specifically looking for a Mediterranean base. In every case, advice from a specialized aviation lawyer is recommended before any registration project in Malta.
