Aircraft Registration in the Baltic States: a Comparison

Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania: a comparison of the ES, YL and LY registers, procedures, timelines, costs and taxation for registering a used aircraft in the Baltic states.

The three Baltic registers at a glance

Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania share the same European regulatory framework (EASA) while each maintaining its own national register and civil aviation authority. All three markets remain modest in size compared with Germany or France, but they attract a growing number of owners of light aircraft, microlights and experimental aircraft, partly thanks to structural costs that tend to be lower than in Western Europe.

Each country applies its own registration prefix: ES- for Estonia, YL- for Latvia and LY- for Lithuania. All three authorities process registration applications, airworthiness certificates and ownership transfers on a similar basis, inherited from the same common European regulations, but with administrative practices and timelines that can vary noticeably from one country to another.

For a buyer based in Western Europe, the idea of registering an aircraft in one of these three countries often remains unfamiliar, for lack of visibility into these markets compared with Germany, France or the United Kingdom. Yet the cultural proximity between the three Baltic states, their shared membership of the Schengen area and the European Union, and the consistency of their regulatory framework with the rest of EASA make them entirely accessible options for an owner based locally or for an aircraft intended to operate in the region.

Registering in Estonia (ES- prefix)

In Estonia, aircraft registration falls under the Estonian Transport Administration (Transpordiamet), the authority that took over the duties of the former Estonian civil aviation administration. Estonia is known in the region for a particularly advanced digital administration, a legacy of its national e-government policy: a large share of administrative procedures, including aeronautical ones, can be initiated through the state's digital portals.

  • Bill of sale and proof of ownership — dated, signed, stating the aircraft's serial number.
  • Valid certificate of airworthiness — or a complete technical file for a first registration.
  • Liability insurance certificate — required before any flight under the new prefix.
Good to know — Estonia is also an active country for welcoming experimental and amateur-built aircraft, a segment in which the local administration has built recognised experience within the Baltic region.

Registering in Latvia (YL- prefix)

Latvia entrusts its aviation register to the Civil Aviation Agency of Latvia. The file to prepare follows the same logic as in other EASA member states: proof of ownership, a document confirming airworthiness or an equivalent file for a new or experimental aircraft, and proof of insurance. Riga remains the country's main administrative and aeronautical hub, and most procedures related to the register are centralised there.

  • Ownership file and aircraft history — examined closely for an aircraft already registered elsewhere.
  • Compliance with applicable EASA requirements — depending on the aircraft category (certified, ELA1/ELA2, experimental).
  • Proof of currently valid insurance.
Good to know — The Latvian fleet remains dominated by leisure light aviation and microlights; a foreign buyer should plan ahead for translating certain documents into Latvian or English, depending on the file's requirements.

Registering in Lithuania (LY- prefix)

In Lithuania, the Civil Aviation Administration of the Republic of Lithuania manages the national register under the LY- prefix. Lithuania has the most active aviation market of the three Baltic states, driven by a dynamic network of flying clubs and a relatively substantial supply of used light aircraft and microlights for the region. As elsewhere in the European Union, transferring an aircraft already registered in another member state requires prior deregistration from the register of origin.

  • Deregistration certificate from the previous register — essential for any aircraft already registered elsewhere in the EU.
  • Up-to-date airframe and engine logbooks — checked during the review of the file.
  • Currently valid liability insurance certificate.
Good to know — Lithuania is regularly cited as the most dynamic Baltic country for general aviation, with a greater number of airfields and clubs than its two neighbours.

Comparing timelines and costs

In all three countries, processing a complete file that meets EASA requirements generally takes from a few weeks to two or three months, a timeline comparable to that observed elsewhere in the European Union. Differences between Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania are mainly down to local administrative responsiveness and how complete the submitted file is, rather than fundamental regulatory differences, since all three registers apply the same common European rules.

Tip — Whichever country you choose, a complete file submitted from the very first application (ownership, airworthiness, insurance, deregistration from the origin register if needed) remains the most effective way to shorten the overall processing time.

VAT and taxation across the three countries

As elsewhere in the European Union, a sale between EU resident private individuals is generally not subject to local VAT in the three Baltic states, while a sale carried out by a professional or a company may be subject to it. An aircraft imported from outside the EU remains subject to import VAT, calculated on its customs value and applied at the national rate in force in the country of first release for free circulation.

SituationTax treatment
Sale between EU resident private individualsGenerally VAT-exempt
Sale by a professionalVAT potentially applicable
Import from outside the EUImport VAT, national rate in force

Standard VAT rates differ from one Baltic country to another and change periodically: it is recommended to have the applicable rate and precise tax regime confirmed with local counsel before any acquisition, particularly for an aircraft that has already changed country of registration several times.

Transferring from another EU country

Transferring an aircraft already registered in another member state to one of the three Baltic registers follows the standard procedure applicable across the Union: obtaining a deregistration certificate from the register of origin, then submitting the complete file (proof of ownership, airworthiness, insurance) to the new authority. Common EASA rules govern this transfer and make it considerably simpler than an import from outside the European Union.

  • Request deregistration from the register of origin before submitting the new file, never after.
  • Check the compatibility of the existing certificate of airworthiness with the new register's requirements.
  • Arrange liability insurance under the new prefix before the first flight.
Point of caution — An aircraft cannot legally fly without a valid registration: avoid any gap between deregistration from the previous register and confirmation of the new one.

Which register to choose for your profile

For a buyer based in or near the Baltic region, the choice of country of registration depends above all on where the aircraft will actually be operated rather than on tax optimisation, since the three regimes remain close on that front. Lithuania offers the largest used-aircraft market and the densest club network; Estonia stands out for the smoothness of its online procedures, an advantage for a file handled remotely; Latvia remains a solid option, particularly for an aircraft based near Riga.

Tip — Compare the listings available in all three countries on Aircraft2Sell before deciding: the volume of offers per country gives a concrete indication of how active each national market really is.

In any case, take the time to contact the relevant national authority directly before starting a registration process: the common European regulations do not rule out certain local specifics, particularly regarding the forms to use, the supporting documents required in the national language, or how filing fees are to be paid. A preliminary email often avoids an administrative back-and-forth that would needlessly extend the overall processing time.
