Converting an FAA Licence into an EASA Licence

Hold a pilot licence issued by the FAA and want to fly an EU-registered aircraft? Here is the step-by-step process for converting it into an EASA licence.

Why convert an FAA licence into an EASA licence

A licence issued by the United States Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) lets you fly a US-registered aircraft (N-reg), including in Europe, but it grants no rights on an aircraft registered in an EU member state. To fly an EASA-registered aircraft (F-, D-, G-, etc.), you need a licence recognised by the European Union Aviation Safety Agency (EASA), or one issued by a member state's national authority under common EASA rules.

This is a common situation for pilots who trained in the United States — often for cost or weather-availability reasons — and then move to Europe, or for owners who buy a US-registered aircraft and want to re-register it under a European flag.

Other pilots find themselves in this situation after gaining professional experience overseas, or simply because they chose to train entirely in the United States before returning to fly in Europe. In every case, converting the licence becomes a mandatory step before legally exercising privileges on an EU-registered aircraft, whether for private or professional use.

The principle: validation or full conversion

EASA regulation (Part-FCL, for "Flight Crew Licensing") sets out a structured mechanism for converting third-country licences, including FAA licences. It is not a simple administrative formality: the holder must demonstrate a level of theoretical knowledge and practical skill equivalent to that required of a candidate applying directly for an EASA licence.

In practice, the national civil aviation authority of the member state where you file your application (such as the DGAC in France) assesses your flight experience and FAA ratings, and determines the number of additional theory exams and flight hours required. The process is therefore never fully automatic, nor identical from one pilot to the next.

Good to know — Conversion is done aircraft category by category: an FAA PPL(A) leads to an EASA PPL(A), but type ratings and special endorsements (night flying, IFR...) often need to be revalidated or demonstrated separately according to recognised equivalences.

Theory exams you may need to retake

Candidates for conversion generally have to retake a series of EASA theory exams, with the exact number depending on the licence sought (PPL, CPL, ATPL) and the competent national authority. These subjects typically cover:

  • Air law — European air law, distinct from the US FAR framework.
  • Human performance — human factors and physiological limitations under the EASA syllabus.
  • Meteorology — concepts and formats specific to European airspace.
  • Navigation — procedures and charts conforming to European standards.
  • Operational procedures — operations and airspace specific to Europe.

These exams are usually prepared through an Approved Training Organisation (ATO), which can also provide the preparation certificate required to register for the theory tests with the national authority.

The skill test and required experience

Beyond the theory, a skill test with an EASA-approved examiner is in principle required to validate the conversion. This flight check verifies that the pilot masters procedures and handling to European standards, including the specifics of air traffic and radio phraseology used in EASA airspace.

The national authority may also require a minimum of recent flight hours or experience on an aircraft type comparable to the one covered by the EASA licence. The candidate's file (FAA logbook, licence and ratings held, qualifications) is therefore reviewed in detail before any validation.

Word of caution — The exact level of equivalence (number of theory exams waived, flight hours recognised) depends on an individual assessment of the file by the competent authority: there is no single guaranteed scale, and it can change as rules evolve. Check directly with the national authority and an ATO before committing time or money.

The EASA medical certificate

An EASA licence must be backed by a medical certificate issued under EASA rules (Part-MED) by an Aeromedical Examiner (AME) recognised by a European civil aviation authority. An FAA medical certificate, even if current, is not automatically accepted to exercise the privileges of an EASA licence: a new medical examination with a European AME is generally required.

It is advisable to schedule this medical exam in parallel with the theory requirements, since a deferral or restriction can have a direct impact on the conversion timeline.

Administrative steps with the national authority

The conversion of an FAA licence is filed with the national civil aviation authority of the member state where the pilot wishes to be based (for example the DGAC in France). The file generally includes:

  • A copy of the FAA licence and its associated ratings.
  • The complete flight logbook, broken down by aircraft type and flight.
  • Proof of identity and, in some cases, proof of residence in the member state concerned.
  • A current EASA medical certificate.
  • Pass certificates for the theory exams and skill test required by the authority.

An Approved Training Organisation (ATO) can support the whole process, from the initial file assessment to the final submission to the national authority, which considerably simplifies matters for a pilot unfamiliar with European administrative procedures.

Common pitfalls and timelines to plan for

Converting an FAA licence into an EASA licence is an administrative process that takes time: expect several months between filing, preparing for and passing the theory exams, the skill test and actually receiving the licence. The real workload depends heavily on the candidate's profile: a pilot with many recent flight hours and advanced ratings (IFR, multi-engine) will generally have a faster path than a pilot holding only a basic licence with few hours. A few mistakes keep coming up among pilots who underestimate this process:

  • Waiting until after buying the European aircraft to start the conversion, when the process can take several months.
  • Neglecting the EASA medical exam, which can reveal an unexpected restriction.
  • Not checking in advance with the national authority and an ATO the exact number of exams and hours required for their specific profile.
  • Forgetting that type ratings or special endorsements (night flying, IFR, instructor) often follow equivalence rules separate from the base licence.
Tip — Contact an Approved Training Organisation (ATO) based in the target member state as early as possible: it can assess your file individually and give you a realistic timeline before any financial commitment.
