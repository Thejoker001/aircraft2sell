Buying a used aircraft in France or the UK: which country to choose

Market, prices, Brexit, CAA registration and tax: a comparison between France and the UK to help you decide where to search for your used aircraft.

Two historic markets, now separated by Brexit

France and the United Kingdom are both among the most active light aviation markets in Europe, with a long tradition of flying clubs, a sizeable fleet of aircraft and a dense network of airfields. Until Brexit, the two markets operated under a largely shared regulatory framework under EASA. Since the UK's departure from the European Union, the UK has its own civil aviation authority, the Civil Aviation Authority (CAA), and applies its own certification rules, distinct from the EASA rules applied in France.

This regulatory separation does not make a cross-border purchase impossible, far from it, but it does add extra steps to plan for compared with a purchase between two EU member states. A French buyer can perfectly well acquire a used aircraft in the UK, and vice versa, provided they understand these specifics before getting started.

Size and activity of the used market

France has a very large fleet of light aircraft, supported by a particularly dense network of flying clubs and a long-standing tradition of popular aviation. Supply on the used market is abundant, especially for the most common single-engine piston aircraft such as Cessnas, Pipers or Robins.

The UK also has a substantial fleet of light aircraft and a highly developed general aviation culture, with many private airfields and a dynamic used market, particularly for touring aircraft and British classics (Pipers, Cessnas, but also some types less common on the continent). Driving on the left obviously has no bearing on flying, but the British market does retain certain equipment specifics tied to local habits (avionics, paint schemes, fit-out).

Comparing the prices charged

Price levels remain broadly comparable between the two countries for the same model and the same maintenance condition, with observed differences owing mainly to local supply and demand at any given time. Since Brexit, however, the euro/sterling exchange rate adds an extra variable: an aircraft priced in pounds can become more or less attractive to a European buyer depending on how the exchange rate moves during the transaction.

For a cross-border transaction, you should also factor the exchange rate movement into your total budget calculation, as it can change the final price converted into euros over the course of a negotiation.

The impact of Brexit on a cross-border transaction

Brexit has changed the administrative nature of a purchase between France and the UK: what used to be a simple intra-EU transfer is now, from a customs and regulatory standpoint, comparable to an import or export between the European Union and a third country. This does not mean the transaction is blocked, but it does involve extra formalities that need to be planned for in advance.

  • Customs formalities — an aircraft crossing the Channel in either direction may now be subject to customs procedures that did not exist between the two countries before Brexit.
  • Recognition of certificates — a Certificate of Airworthiness (CofA) issued under the EASA regime is not automatically equivalent to a UK CAA certificate, and vice versa; a compliance check is required when changing the country of registration.
  • VAT — a transaction between France and the UK is no longer treated as an intra-EU exchange; the applicable VAT treatment must be checked case by case depending on the status of the buyer and seller.
Point of caution — Always have your file reviewed by a professional familiar with post-Brexit transactions (broker or specialised aviation lawyer) before committing to a cross-border purchase between France and the UK.

Registration: EASA in France, the CAA in the UK

An aircraft registered in France falls under the EASA system, with a registration under the F- prefix managed by the DGAC. An aircraft registered in the UK now falls exclusively under the UK CAA, with a G- prefix, and has its own Certificate of Airworthiness procedures and its own maintenance rules, which have progressively diverged from EASA's since the UK left the European Union.

Moving an aircraft from one register to the other therefore requires a fuller file than a simple transfer between two EASA countries: a technical compliance check against the new regulatory framework, submission of the complete maintenance file and, in some cases, a compliance inspection by the destination authority.

  • Check which regime (EASA or UK CAA) the aircraft is currently certified under before considering a change of country.
  • Plan for administrative lead times that are often longer than for a standard intra-EU transfer.
  • Seek advice from a Continuing Airworthiness Management Organisation (CAMO) familiar with cross-Channel files.

Maintenance network and parts availability

Both countries have a dense network of approved maintenance workshops for the most common models. In France, workshops remain certified Part-145 / Part-CAO under EASA oversight; in the UK, workshops now operate under the UK's own equivalent of these approvals, defined and overseen by the CAA. For an aircraft that regularly flies between both countries, it is worth checking that the workshop chosen for routine maintenance is indeed recognised in the aircraft's country of registration.

For rarer models or specific engines, parts availability may vary slightly between the two markets since Brexit, as some suppliers have adjusted their logistics between the EU and the UK. It is therefore advisable to check in advance how easily parts can be sourced for the aircraft type under consideration, regardless of the country of purchase.

Which country to favour depending on your profile

For a buyer who wants to stay within a simple regulatory framework, with no customs formalities or certification-regime checks, favouring the French market, or more broadly a market that remained under EASA, remains the most straightforward solution. For a buyer willing to accept a few extra steps in exchange for a wider choice, particularly on certain models more common in the UK, widening the search across the Channel can be worthwhile, provided the cost and time of post-Brexit formalities are factored into the calculation.

Good to know — In all cases, a pre-purchase inspection by a qualified independent mechanic remains essential, whether the aircraft is located in France, the UK or elsewhere in Europe.

Ultimately, the country of purchase matters less than the actual condition of the aircraft, the consistency of its technical file and correctly anticipating the administrative formalities specific to a cross-Channel transaction since Brexit. Comparing listings from both markets widens your options, provided you budget correctly for the time and cost of a registration transfer if you change country.
