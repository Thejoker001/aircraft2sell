A 100,000-euro budget opens the used market up to capable retractable-gear singles and the first entry-level light twins. Here's how to make the best choice.

What €100,000 changes compared with a tighter budget

Doubling the budget compared with €50,000 doesn't just mean "the same aircraft, in better condition" — it opens the door to structurally different categories of aircraft. Under €50,000, buyers typically look at older fixed-gear singles, well-equipped microlights, or gliders. With €100,000, the market offers faster retractable-gear singles, modern composite airframes with integrated avionics, and even the first entry-level light twins.

This budget range still demands real trade-offs: a capable but older retractable-gear single, a modern composite airframe with fewer hours logged on long cross-country trips, or an economical twin with higher maintenance bills don't serve the same purpose. The right choice depends first on the actual flying profile sought — cruise speed, payload, engine redundancy — rather than on the available budget alone.

As at any budget level, the listed price is only part of the real acquisition cost. Ferry flights, avionics upgrades, sorting out an incomplete logbook, or funding the next engine overhaul all need to be planned for before you set your maximum purchase price.

Capable retractable-gear singles

At this budget level, retractable-gear singles such as the Mooney or the Beechcraft Bonanza become accessible, with a meaningful jump in cruise speed compared with a classic fixed-gear single. These aircraft, often older but well maintained, appeal to buyers who fly regular cross-country trips over medium to long distances, where the extra speed translates directly into less time in the air.

The trade-off for this performance is a more complex airframe to maintain: the retractable gear, the variable-pitch propeller, and, on the Mooney, the laminar-flow wing all demand extra attention during the pre-purchase inspection. A complete and consistent maintenance history matters even more here than on a fixed-gear single, since the cost of reworking a poorly maintained retractable gear can eat up a significant share of the purchase budget.

Modern composite singles with efficient engines

With a €100,000 budget, some younger composite singles become realistic options, including entry-level generations of the Diamond DA40 or earlier model years of the Cirrus SR20, depending on market conditions and avionics fit. These aircraft combine a modern airframe, integrated digital avionics, and generally well-controlled fuel burn, with simpler upkeep than a classic retractable-gear design.

The compromise here is mostly about age and remaining engine life available at this budget: a recent example with the latest avionics is rare under €100,000, while an older example with less remaining engine life requires planning for a mid-term overhaul. Comparing several listings of the same model and year lets you weigh this trade-off precisely before committing.

The first accessible light twins

€100,000 can also be enough to reach a first used light twin, typically older and with reduced remaining life on each of the two engines, but offering the engine redundancy sought by buyers who plan regular flights over mountains, water, or long distances. This category demands extra care, since maintenance covers two complete engine and propeller sets rather than one.

An entry-level twin at this budget generally means accepting an older aircraft, with limited remaining engine life on one or both powerplants, and building the foreseeable cost of an upcoming overhaul into the purchase decision from day one. It's a sound choice for a buyer who values the safety margin of redundancy over outright performance, and who has enough annual operating budget to absorb higher maintenance costs than a single.

Criteria that matter more than the asking price

At this budget level, several criteria should outweigh the listed price when identifying the real best choice.

  • Condition and operation of the retractable gear and variable-pitch propeller — major inspection points in these categories.
  • Remaining engine hours before the next overhaul, weighed against the estimated cost of that work — double it for a twin.
  • Consistency between the maintenance logbook, flight logbook, and stated hours for every engine and propeller.
  • Avionics equipment level, and the cost of any upgrade needed to stay compliant with changing regulations.
  • Insurance cost, noticeably higher for a twin or a retractable-gear aircraft than for a simple fixed-gear single.
Warning — On a retractable-gear aircraft or a twin, always have the complete landing and gear-incident history checked during the pre-purchase inspection: an undetected fault in this area can cost far more than any saving made on the purchase price.

How to pick the best aircraft for your own use

The "best" aircraft under €100,000 depends entirely on the flying profile you're after. For a pilot who values cruise speed and regular cross-country trips while keeping maintenance costs under control, a well-documented retractable-gear single is often the most rational choice. For a buyer who prioritises a modern airframe and integrated avionics with simpler upkeep, a recent entry-level composite single is an excellent compromise.

For a pilot who regularly flies demanding routes — over mountains, over water, long distances — and who can accept a higher annual operating budget, a first used light twin can justify its extra cost through the safety margin provided by engine redundancy.

In every case, have an independent pre-purchase inspection carried out, compare several listings of the same model and year, and build the foreseeable cost of the next engine overhaul into your calculation from the start, rather than looking only at the listed purchase price.

Advice — Set aside 10 to 15% of your total budget for related costs (inspection, ferry flight, avionics compliance) before fixing your maximum purchase price — and more still if you're considering a twin or a retractable-gear aircraft.
