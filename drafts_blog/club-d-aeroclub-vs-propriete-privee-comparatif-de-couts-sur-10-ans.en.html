Flying club vs private ownership: a 10-year cost comparison

Membership, flying hours, maintenance, insurance, resale: what flying club membership really costs compared with owning your own aircraft, over a decade.

Two models, two philosophies

Flying with a club and owning your own aircraft follow two very different logics. A club pools the cost of one or more aircraft across many members, which lowers the entry ticket but means sharing aircraft availability and following the club's internal rules. Private ownership, alone or in a group, gives full control over the aircraft, its availability and its maintenance, in exchange for a significantly larger financial commitment and management workload.

Comparing the two purely on an hourly basis is misleading: the real cost depends mainly on how many hours you fly per year, the type of aircraft targeted, and the intended holding period. A 10-year comparison smooths out cost spikes — a major overhaul, an engine rebuild — and gives a fairer picture than the headline hourly rate alone.

Entry cost: membership vs purchase

In a flying club, the entry cost is usually limited to an annual membership fee and sometimes a modest joining fee or shares. In private ownership, you need to finance the purchase of the aircraft itself (cash, loan, or shared ownership), plus related costs: pre-purchase inspection, initial insurance, and any registration or transfer fees.

  • Flying club — modest annual fee, immediate access to an already-maintained fleet.
  • Sole private ownership — capital tied up in the aircraft's price, varying widely with model and condition.
  • Group ownership — capital shared among several owners, a middle-ground option financially.

Recurring costs in a flying club

In a club, recurring costs essentially boil down to the annual membership fee and the hourly rental rate, which usually bundles most maintenance, insurance and hangarage. The main advantage is predictability: the pilot doesn't directly absorb cost spikes from a major overhaul or a serious failure — the club's structure does.

The trade-off is shared availability: the aircraft you want may be booked by another member, and some clubs impose minimum or maximum hour quotas per pilot. The hourly cost also stays strictly proportional to hours flown, with no favourable threshold effect beyond a certain annual volume.

Recurring costs in private ownership

In private ownership, recurring costs split between fixed charges (insurance, hangarage, scheduled maintenance, airworthiness and navigation subscriptions) and variable charges tied to hours flown (fuel, wear, overhaul reserve). Fixed charges remain due even if the aircraft flies little in a given year, which penalises occasional use compared with club membership.

  • Annual fixed charges: insurance, hangarage, continued airworthiness, regardless of flying activity.
  • Engine overhaul reserve: to be set aside hour by hour to avoid an unplanned expense near the end of the engine's life.
  • Unplanned maintenance: unscheduled failures and repairs, to be budgeted alongside scheduled maintenance.
Tip — Beyond a certain number of annual hours, the hourly cost of private ownership generally becomes more competitive than club rental, precisely because fixed charges are spread over more hours.

Availability and flexibility

A club means sharing the fleet: booking the aircraft you want at the time you want isn't always possible, especially during peak demand (weekends, holidays). Private ownership offers near-total availability, subject to scheduled maintenance — a decisive advantage for regular use or trips planned well in advance.

In exchange, the owner alone carries the administrative and technical management of the aircraft (tracking airworthiness directives, planning inspections, choosing maintenance providers), a burden the club absorbs collectively for its members.

Resale at the end of the period

At the end of a 10-year period, a flying club leaves no residual value for the member: fees paid financed usage, not an asset. A private owner, by contrast, holds an aircraft that can be resold, with a value depending on its overall condition, remaining engine life and market conditions at the time of sale.

This residual value must be factored into the overall comparison: a well-maintained aircraft with a sound airframe can offset a significant share of holding costs over time, while a poorly maintained aircraft or one with unresolved airworthiness directives can instead mean a steep discount at resale.

How to choose based on your profile

The choice mainly depends on the hours you expect to fly, how much you need guaranteed availability, and your appetite for the technical and administrative side of owning an aircraft. A pilot flying few hours per year, without strong scheduling constraints, generally finds a flying club more economical and less demanding: they benefit from an already-maintained fleet without carrying the risk of a costly failure. A pilot flying a lot, with strong availability needs or recurring travel plans, tends to recoup a private ownership investment — solo or shared — more quickly, since fixed charges are spread over more hours.

Group ownership is often a sensible middle ground between the two models: it keeps most of the benefits of private ownership (a dedicated aircraft, less sharing than in a club, resale value) while reducing the capital tied up and some fixed charges through sharing among a handful of partners. Before deciding, it is worth building your own 10-year simulation with real quotes — insurance, hangarage, scheduled maintenance from an approved shop — rather than relying on generic averages, since the gap between two airfields or two aircraft models can be substantial.

Caution — Never decide based on the headline hourly rate alone: always factor in fixed charges, the overhaul reserve and the expected residual resale value to compare both models on equal footing.
