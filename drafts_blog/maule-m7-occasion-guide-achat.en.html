Why the M-7 is a bushplane benchmark

The Maule M-7 is one of the most popular bushplanes among owner-pilots looking for a rugged aircraft capable of operating off short, unprepared strips. Built by American manufacturer Maule Air in Georgia, this taildragger single-engine piston stands out for its very short takeoff and landing distances, a trait baked into the design from the start for rough fields, grass strips and mountain approaches.

On the used market, the M-7 attracts a wide range of buyers: private pilots seeking a versatile leisure aircraft, operators serving small remote strips, and backcountry flying and mountain-touring enthusiasts. That versatility, paired with a simple and easily repairable airframe, explains the model's lasting popularity since its introduction in the 1980s, with the broader Maule lineage dating back to the 1960s.

M-7 variants and what sets them apart

The M-7 has been produced in several variants that differ mainly in engine choice and landing gear configuration. You'll find different horsepower flat engines, some fuel-injected and others carbureted, as well as classic taildragger versions and float or ski variants for specific missions. A tricycle-gear layout also exists elsewhere in the Maule lineup, but the M-7 is best known in its taildragger configuration, the one most sought after by bush-flying enthusiasts.

Before buying, it is essential to identify precisely which variant is on offer: engine power, payload capacity and short-field performance vary noticeably from one version to another, which directly affects what you can do with the aircraft and its resale value.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used M-7 depends above all on the age of the aircraft, engine hours remaining before the next major overhaul, airframe condition, and the level of avionics fitted. Older aircraft with reduced engine potential trade at a noticeably more accessible level than recent, well-maintained examples fitted with modern avionics.

Beyond the purchase price, your budget should include ancillary costs: an independent mechanic's pre-purchase inspection, registration and transfer fees, insurance suited to backcountry or mountain operations, and possibly a fabric or avionics refresh if the aircraft needs one.

What to check before buying

As with any used aircraft, a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic qualified on the type remains essential before finalising a transaction. For an M-7, a few points deserve particular attention given its typical use on rough airstrips.

  • Condition of the fabric covering (on covered variants): look for UV damage, wear or previous repairs that may signal an upcoming recover job.
  • Complete engine history: hours elapsed since the last major overhaul (SMOH), compliance with the manufacturer's maintenance program, traceability of replaced parts.
  • Condition of the taildragger gear and shock struts, often heavily stressed by repeated operation on uneven terrain.
  • Presence and condition of any specialty equipment (floats, skis, STOL kit, tundra tires) along with their associated documentation.
  • Complete logbook and technical file, with no grey areas in the aircraft's history or its certificate of airworthiness (CofA).
Good to know — An aircraft with heavy rough-field flying time deserves closer attention on the landing gear and structure: a written independent inspection report remains the best protection against a bad surprise after the purchase.

Taildragger gear and tundra tires: operating specifics

The M-7's taildragger gear, combined with tundra tires on many examples, is precisely what lets it operate from short, grass or dirt strips, sometimes rough ones. This configuration, however, calls for specific flying technique, particularly during taxi and landing, and demands closer attention to maintaining the gear, wheels and tires, which see more stress than on a tricycle-gear aircraft used on paved runways.

A pilot unfamiliar with taildragger handling should plan for suitable training before taking on this type of aircraft: the transition is usually done with an instructor qualified on type, and this skill should be factored into the buying project alongside the acquisition budget.

Engine maintenance and operating costs

The operating cost of an M-7 remains broadly comparable to that of other light single-engine aircraft in its category, with a few specifics tied to its backcountry use. The main expense items remain the mandatory annual inspection, insurance (often higher for operations on unprepared strips), parking and, in the longer term, engine overhaul once the hourly potential is reached.

Having the aircraft maintained at an approved shop familiar with the type, and particularly with taildragger and bush-equipment specifics, helps limit unpleasant surprises and preserve its resale value. Rigorous, well-documented maintenance records are a strong argument at a future resale.

Tip — Always ask for the last three maintenance invoices before making an offer: they give an accurate picture of how seriously the aircraft has been maintained, particularly on the gear and structure.

Bush mission gear worth considering

Beyond the base airframe, many M-7s are fitted with accessories designed for bush missions: oversized low-pressure tundra tires, a STOL kit with leading-edge cuffs, a propeller optimised for short field work, or amphibious floats for water operations. Each of these options affects performance, empty weight and maintenance costs, and should be evaluated against your actual intended use rather than its visual appeal alone.

If you are considering mixed use (land and water, or land and snow), check the compatibility of any interchangeable gear offered with the aircraft and the real cost of conversion kits, which can represent a significant investment on top of the aircraft's purchase price.

Resale and the used market

The M-7 benefits from an active used market, supported by a loyal community of bush pilots and good parts availability from the manufacturer. A well-maintained aircraft, with a transparent history, a complete logbook and bush equipment in good condition, generally resells more easily and on better terms than one whose technical file has grey areas.

Comparing several listings of similar models, factoring in remaining engine hours and installed specialty equipment, remains the best way to assess a fair price, both for buying and for preparing a future resale.

Finally, keep in mind that an M-7's value also depends on geography and the seasonality of its equipment: a ski-equipped example will be particularly sought after in snowy regions during winter, while a wheel-equipped version will more easily find buyers among mountain and bush pilots operating year-round on rough strips. Taking the time to view several aircraft before deciding remains the best guarantee of a successful purchase.
