The P337: a push-pull twin-boom twin

The Cessna 337 Skymaster, and its pressurised P337 variant, stand out through an unusual layout in light aviation: two engines aligned on the fuselage centreline, one at the front pulling and one at the rear pushing, with the fuselage extended by twin booms running back to the tail surfaces. This so-called centerline-thrust configuration puts both engines on the same axis, unlike a conventional twin with engines mounted on the wings.

The P337 variant adds a pressurised cabin to this base airframe, allowing flight at higher altitudes with better comfort for occupants, while keeping the engine-redundancy benefits that come with any twin. On the used market, the P337 remains a niche aircraft, less common than wing-mounted twins, but sought after by buyers who understand its particular characteristics.

Why this layout appeals to some buyers

The argument Skymaster owners cite most often is how much simpler it is to handle an engine failure. On a conventional twin, losing a wing-mounted engine creates a strong yawing moment that must be corrected immediately, which calls for rigorous, type-specific training. On a centerline-thrust aircraft like the P337, with both engines on the same axis, an engine failure does not produce that same pronounced yaw, which makes the immediate handling of the failure easier for a pilot who is less experienced on twins.

For some buyers, this characteristic makes the Skymaster a more approachable entry point into twin-engine flying than conventional layouts, while still keeping the main benefit of engine redundancy for flights over mountains, water or long distances.

Pressurisation: an asset that demands extra maintenance

The P337's pressurised cabin allows flight higher up, above some of the turbulence and weather, with breathing comfort for occupants superior to that of a non-pressurised aircraft flying at the same altitude. That benefit comes at a cost: the pressurisation system adds specific components — door seals, valves, ducting — that must be inspected and maintained on a dedicated schedule, on top of routine maintenance for the rest of the aircraft.

Good to know — Before buying a P337, always ask for the complete maintenance history of the pressurisation system and have its actual operation checked by an independent mechanic familiar with the type, not just the rest of the airframe and engines.

A poorly maintained pressurisation system can turn into a significant source of unexpected expense after purchase. This is a specific point of vigilance for the P337 that does not apply to the non-pressurised 337 variant, or to most other light twins.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used P337 depends heavily on the condition and history of both engines, the status of the pressurisation system, the avionics fitted, and the airframe hours remaining before the next major overhaul. As a niche aircraft built in far smaller numbers than the most common wing-mounted twins, the used-market supply is thinner, which can stretch the search for a well-maintained example.

Beyond the purchase price, the budget should include a pre-purchase inspection by a mechanic specialised in the type, insurance for a pressurised twin, and a reserve for a major overhaul on both engines if one is approaching.

What to check before buying

On top of a standard pre-purchase inspection, the P337 requires checking points specific to its twin-boom twin layout and its pressurisation.

  • Separate history and engine hours for the front engine and the rear engine: both must be assessed independently, not just as an average.
  • Condition of the fuselage booms and their junctions with the tail surfaces, structural areas specific to this layout.
  • Actual operation and seal integrity of the pressurisation system, not just its presence on the spec sheet.
  • Complete logbook for both engines and for the pressurisation system, with traceability of replaced parts.
  • Status of the certificate of airworthiness (CofA) and consistency between the declared history and the hours shown on the gauges.

Using a mechanic or maintenance centre with specific Skymaster experience is particularly recommended for this model, more so than for a more common twin that most workshops already know well.

Routine maintenance and operating costs

Routine maintenance on a P337 combines the costs of any twin — two engines, two propellers to track separately — with the extra costs specific to pressurisation. Finding a workshop genuinely competent on this particular model can take more searching than for a more common aircraft, which should be planned for before purchase rather than discovered afterwards.

Parts specific to the centerline-thrust layout or to the pressurisation system can also be harder to source than those for a more widely produced model, which can occasionally extend downtime during maintenance work.

Tip — Before buying, identify a workshop or independent mechanic with documented Skymaster experience in your region, rather than searching under pressure after the first problem.

Training and licensing to fly a pressurised twin-boom twin

Flying a P337 requires a multi-engine rating, as with any twin, plus specific familiarisation with managing the pressurisation system. While the centerline-thrust layout simplifies the immediate handling of an engine failure compared with a conventional twin, it remains a full multi-engine aircraft that calls for rigorous training and regular proficiency upkeep.

Type-specific transition training, delivered by an instructor or a centre with Skymaster experience, is strongly recommended before taking on the aircraft as an owner, particularly for managing the pressurisation system in flight.

Resale and the used market

The used P337 market remains a niche market, with a smaller installed fleet than the most common wing-mounted twins. This scarcity can work in a seller's favour for a well-maintained, well-documented example, since buyers interested in this specific layout often have few equivalent alternatives available at the same time.

Conversely, an aircraft with grey areas in its pressurisation or engine maintenance history can prove harder to resell than a more common conventional twin, simply because fewer buyers are willing to absorb the perceived risk. Comparing several listings of similar Skymasters and P337s remains the best way to judge a fair price, both when buying and when preparing a future resale.
