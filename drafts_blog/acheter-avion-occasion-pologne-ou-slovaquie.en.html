Buying a Used Aircraft in Poland or Slovakia: Which Country to Choose

Market, prices, registration, taxation and pitfalls to avoid: a complete comparison to help you decide where to buy your used light aircraft between Poland and Slovakia.

Two very different markets in size and maturity

Poland and Slovakia are both EU members and apply the EASA regulatory framework, but their used light aircraft markets have very different profiles. Poland has one of the densest and most dynamic markets in Central Europe, driven by a long aeronautical tradition (historic glider and light aircraft manufacturers, numerous active flying clubs) and a wide supply: gliders, microlights, motor gliders, touring single-engines and a significant installed fleet of turboprops and business jets around Warsaw, Kraków and Poznań.

Slovakia has a much smaller market in volume terms, concentrated around a handful of key airfields (Bratislava, Piešťany, Nitra, Žilina), with supply dominated by gliders, microlights and entry-level single-engines, often coming from former flying clubs. Slovak buyers frequently widen their search to neighbouring countries, particularly Poland and the Czech Republic, which limits the depth of the local market for more specific or high-end aircraft.

For a buyer who values choice and depth of supply, Poland generally remains the richer destination in Central Europe. For a buyer looking above all for good value on a simple aircraft, Slovakia can offer good opportunities, provided the search is widened to the whole region.

Price comparison by aircraft type

Prices in Slovakia are on average slightly lower than those seen in Poland for comparable entry-level aircraft, but the gap narrows sharply, or even reverses, as soon as you compare well-equipped or newer aircraft. As always, engine history (hours remaining before major overhaul) and the state of the avionics weigh far more on the final price than the country of sale alone.

Registration: ULC versus Dopravný úrad

In Poland, civil aircraft registration is handled by the ULC (Urząd Lotnictwa Cywilnego, the Civil Aviation Authority), which assigns the SP- prefix. In Slovakia, it is the Dopravný úrad (Transport Authority of the Slovak Republic) that keeps the national register, under the OM- prefix. In both countries, a purchase between private individuals where the aircraft is already registered locally simply requires updating the registered owner, while an aircraft imported from another country must first be deregistered from its register of origin.

  • Valid certificate of airworthiness and ARC — check this before signing, in both countries.
  • Bill of sale — dated, signed by both parties, stating the serial number, required by both the ULC and the Dopravný úrad.
  • Liability insurance certificate — required before first use under the new registration, in both Poland and Slovakia.
Good to know — Both countries apply the same common EASA rules for registration transfers between EU member states: expect 2 to 8 weeks depending on how complete the file submitted to the ULC or the Dopravný úrad is.

VAT and taxation: two similar intra-EU regimes

Since Poland and Slovakia are both EU members, VAT treatment follows the same common logic in both countries: a sale between EU resident private individuals is generally VAT-exempt, while a sale by a professional or a company may be subject to it. An aircraft imported from a non-EU country remains subject to import VAT in both cases, calculated on its customs value at the applicable national rate.

The main practical difference between the two countries is therefore not the VAT regime itself, but the ease of finding a professional used to cross-border formalities: the larger, more mature Polish market has more brokers and accountants specialised in international aircraft acquisition than the Slovak market.

Maintenance, workshops and parts availability

This is one of the areas where the gap between the two countries is clearest. Poland has a dense network of Part-145 and Part-CAO approved workshops, well distributed across the country, with good parts availability for most common models, particularly for gliders and motor gliders given the local aeronautical manufacturing tradition. Slovakia, with a smaller market, has a more limited workshop network, mainly concentrated around the main airfields (Bratislava, Piešťany): for some specific models or rare parts, it is not uncommon to need a technician to travel, or to send the aircraft to Poland, the Czech Republic or Austria for a major overhaul.

This factor should be anticipated before buying, especially if you plan to base the aircraft in Slovakia long term: ask about the workshop that has historically looked after the aircraft, and check the distance to the nearest approved maintenance centre.

Pre-purchase inspection and documents

Whether the aircraft is Polish or Slovak, a pre-purchase inspection by a certified mechanic independent of the seller remains the best protection against unpleasant surprises. It covers the condition of the airframe, the consistency of the engine logbook, maintenance history and the compliance of the avionics with current European requirements (transponder, 8.33 kHz radio), common to both countries thanks to the application of EASA standards.

  • Up-to-date airframe, engine and propeller logbooks, with no chronological gaps, whether kept in Poland or Slovakia.
  • History of maintenance invoices from the last three years; for a Polish or Slovak aircraft, plan for a translation if the logbooks are written in Polish or Slovak.
  • No undisclosed structural damage, verifiable via the maintenance file and a thorough visual inspection.
Point of caution — Never finalise a purchase, in Poland or in Slovakia, without having seen the original logbooks and having their consistency checked by a professional independent of the seller.

Which country to choose depending on your profile

The choice between Poland and Slovakia depends above all on your project and your budget. Poland appeals to buyers looking for a large, varied market, a dense maintenance network and good resale liquidity, with a purchase budget often comparable to the Central European average. Slovakia can suit a buyer looking for a simple aircraft at an attractive entry price, accepting a more limited supply and a less dense maintenance network, which may involve travelling to Poland, the Czech Republic or Austria for some maintenance work.

In all cases, the decisive factor remains the quality of maintenance and the transparency of the aircraft's history, far more than the country of registration alone. Comparing several listings in both countries, at an equivalent budget and category, remains the best way to make an informed choice.

Tip — If you are considering an import between the two countries or from another EU country, have the administrative formalities confirmed in advance by a specialist professional: this avoids unpleasant surprises after signing.
