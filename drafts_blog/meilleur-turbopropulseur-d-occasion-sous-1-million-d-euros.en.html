The used turboprop market under €1M

With a budget under 1 million euros, a buyer looking for a used turboprop almost always turns to one of three well-established families on the European market: the single-engine Pilatus PC-12, the fast single-engine Daher TBM (in its 700, 850 or 900 variants, depending on age and budget), and the twin-engine Beechcraft King Air, particularly the older C90 or B200 versions. These three families cover different missions: versatile transport with strong payload, high cruise speed, or twin-engine redundancy for certain professional missions.

Below this budget threshold, the aircraft's age, the engine hours already flown, and the remaining potential before the next major overhaul usually weigh more heavily on the final choice than the model itself. A newer example of an entry-level model can be a better investment than an older example of a more prestigious model that is close to a major overhaul.

Pilatus PC-12: the versatile all-rounder

The Pilatus PC-12 has established itself as a benchmark single-engine turboprop thanks to its spacious cabin, versatility (passenger transport, cargo, medevac missions) and reputation for ruggedness. Earlier generations, now available on the used market at budgets compatible with the 1-million-euro threshold, remain sought after for their payload and range, even though their avionics are sometimes less modern than on newer versions.

On the used market, an older-generation PC-12 with significant engine hours and limited remaining potential before the major overhaul can still be a sound purchase, provided the price correctly reflects that remaining potential and the logbook shows a rigorous maintenance history.

Daher TBM 700/850/900: speed above all

The Daher TBM family (originally the Socata TBM, then Daher-Socata) stands out for its cruise speed, markedly higher than the PC-12's, at the cost of more limited payload. The TBM 700, the oldest and therefore the most affordable, remains an excellent compromise for personal use focused on fast long-distance travel, with a single pilot able to operate it alone.

The newer 850 and 900 versions offer upgraded engines and avionics but are generally traded at budgets closer to, or above, the 1-million-euro threshold depending on age and hours remaining before the major overhaul. To stay under this budget, buyers most often focus on well-maintained TBM 700s or older TBM 850s.

Beechcraft King Air C90/B200: the tough twin

The Beechcraft King Air holds a distinct place in this comparison: it is the only twin-engine aircraft in the selection, which gives it an engine redundancy valued for certain professional missions or for flights over terrain that would be hard to reach in the event of a failure. The more compact, older C90 versions are found on the used market at budgets often lower than those of the PC-12 or TBM, while the more capable B200 generally commands a higher budget at a comparable age.

The trade-off for this redundancy is a higher operating cost: two engines to maintain, two propellers to monitor, and higher fuel consumption. For a buyer whose priority is twin-engine versatility rather than pure speed or maximum payload, the King Air nonetheless remains a solid option under this budget threshold, particularly on older C90 examples.

Which aircraft for which use

The choice between these three families should first be based on the intended mission rather than the model's prestige. For personal or family use with several passengers and bulky luggage, the PC-12's payload is a real advantage. For a pilot who prioritises fast long-distance trips with a minimal crew, the TBM remains hard to beat at this budget level.

  • Pilatus PC-12: ideal for family or professional transport with strong payload.
  • Daher TBM: relevant for a pilot seeking the highest cruise speed in this segment.
  • Beechcraft King Air: the preferred choice for twin-engine redundancy, particularly on missions over difficult terrain.
  • In every case, remaining engine potential should be compared before the advertised list price.

Conversely, a buyer for whom engine redundancy is a non-negotiable safety criterion — for example for regular flights over mountains or sea — will find the King Air a decisive argument, despite the higher operating cost tied to maintaining two engines.

Buying a used turboprop: points to watch

Whichever model is chosen, buying a used turboprop requires greater vigilance than a piston-engine aircraft. The logbook must accurately trace the engine's complete history: total time since new, time since the last major overhaul (SMOH), and every major intervention documented. The certificate of airworthiness (CofA) must be currently valid and consistent with the history presented.

Word of caution — Always have an independent mechanic approved for the specific type verify the number of hours remaining before the next major overhaul: this figure directly determines the aircraft's real value, far more than its advertised price.

For a King Air, the history of each engine and each propeller must be checked separately, since the two powerplants can have very different remaining potential. A complete pre-purchase inspection, including a ground engine run and ideally a test flight, remains essential before committing financially to this type of aircraft.

Which turboprop to choose under 1 million euros

Under this budget threshold, none of the three models is universally better: each answers a different priority. The Pilatus PC-12 remains the most versatile choice for family or professional use requiring strong payload. The Daher TBM, mainly in its 700 version at this budget level, is aimed at pilots for whom cruise speed matters above everything else. The Beechcraft King Air, particularly in its C90 version, remains the reference option for anyone seeking twin-engine redundancy without exceeding 1 million euros.

In every case, comparing several listings of similar model years, checking the remaining engine potential, and commissioning an independent pre-purchase inspection remain the best ways to secure this significant investment.
