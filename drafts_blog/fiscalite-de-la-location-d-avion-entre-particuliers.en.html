Tax treatment of private aircraft rental

Declared income, VAT, deductible costs: what you need to know before renting your aircraft out to other pilots, or renting one from a private owner.

Why private aircraft rental raises specific tax questions

More and more light aircraft owners are looking to offset the cost of ownership by making their aircraft available to other pilots, outside the usual framework of a flying club or management company. This private rental activity, whether occasional or regular, generates income that must be declared, and depending on the arrangement chosen, can push the owner into a different tax regime than that of simple private use.

The topic is all the more sensitive because the applicable rules vary significantly from one European country to another, and depend on the owner's country of tax residence, the aircraft's country of registration, and its legal status (private individual, company, co-ownership). This guide sets out the general landmarks worth knowing before getting started, without replacing the advice of an accountant or tax lawyer specialising in aviation, who alone can accurately assess your specific situation.

The main legal structures

Before even discussing tax, it is worth distinguishing between the arrangements that frame the private rental of an aircraft, since the applicable tax regime follows directly from the structure chosen.

  • One-off simple rental — the owner makes the aircraft available to another pilot for one or more flights, in exchange for a financial contribution covering some or all of the hourly costs.
  • Regular organised rental — the aircraft is offered repeatedly through a platform, a mailing list or a flying club, with a fixed hourly rate and recurring invoicing.
  • Co-ownership or joint ownership — several individuals jointly own the aircraft and split the costs according to flight hours, without there being, strictly speaking, a rental between a single owner and a tenant.
  • Management through a company — the aircraft is contributed or leased to a structure (company, club) that handles invoicing to the renting pilots, with the owner then receiving a fee or dividends.

Each of these arrangements has different implications for income reporting, VAT and liability, which is why the chosen structure should be formalised in writing before the first rental takes place.

Rental income: which reporting regime applies

When a private individual receives payments in exchange for making their aircraft available, these payments generally constitute taxable income, even if the activity remains occasional. Depending on the country, this income may fall under a category of property or rental income, non-commercial activity income, or may require setting up a dedicated structure beyond a certain volume of transactions.

The line between a simple cost-sharing arrangement between co-owners — often treated differently, since it is not a service rendered to a third party — and a genuine rental activity is decisive: it determines the applicable regime and must be examined case by case with a local tax adviser, since the precise thresholds and categories differ according to national legislation.

Word of caution — Never assume that occasional rental between private individuals escapes reporting obligations: most European tax authorities treat any regular income as taxable, regardless of the amount involved.

VAT and aircraft rental between individuals

The VAT treatment of renting an aircraft between individuals depends in particular on the owner's VAT status (registered or not), the country where the transaction is deemed to take place, and the nature of the flight (private leisure use or carriage for payment under a specific authorisation). Aviation VAT rules include particular regimes depending on the aircraft's use and weight, which do not apply uniformly to every private rental arrangement.

A private individual who occasionally rents out their aircraft without otherwise being VAT-registered as a professional may fall under a different regime than a company that invoices flight hours on a habitual basis. Here again, the precise configuration — country of residence, country of registration, frequency of transactions — must be validated with a professional before setting an hourly rate, in order to avoid a tax reassessment down the line.

Deductible costs and depreciation

Once rental income is declared, the owner can generally deduct a portion of the costs related to operating the aircraft, in proportion to rental use versus personal use. The most commonly considered items include:

  • Routine maintenance and the major overhaul, apportioned according to the engine hours flown on behalf of renters.
  • Insurance premiums, when the policy explicitly covers rental use by third-party pilots.
  • Parking fees, fuel not re-invoiced, and regulatory compliance costs.
  • Depreciation of the aircraft, under rules and timeframes that vary significantly from one country to another and depending on the owner's status (private individual or company).

Keeping a rigorously up-to-date logbook, with a precise record of flight hours invoiced to renters as distinct from personal use, is essential to justify these deductions in the event of an audit.

The most common tax pitfalls

Several mistakes come up repeatedly among owners who start renting out their aircraft between individuals without tax planning:

  • Invoicing only a cost-sharing contribution without a written contract, which makes it harder to justify the tax regime applied in the event of an audit.
  • Forgetting to declare income received by direct bank transfer between individuals, on the assumption that it does not pass through a commercial structure.
  • Overlooking the impact on insurance: a claim arising during a rented flight may be refused if the policy only covered the owner's personal use.
  • Applying another European country's tax regime by analogy, when each national legislation has its own thresholds and income categories.
Tip — Always formalise a rental agreement, even a simplified one, specifying the hourly rate, cost allocation, applicable insurance, and the engine hours tracked for each rental flight.

Insurance and contractual documents

Beyond tax considerations, private rental requires checking that the aircraft's insurance explicitly covers this use, since a policy limited to the owner's personal use may exclude any claim arising during a flight invoiced to a third party. The aircraft's certificate of airworthiness (CofA) must also remain valid for the intended use, and the logbook must allow personal flight hours to be distinguished from hours flown on behalf of renters — information that may be requested by the insurer or by the tax authorities.

A standard rental agreement should specify at minimum: the identity of the parties, the hourly rate and payment terms, the allocation of engine hours, cancellation terms and liability in the event of an incident, as well as the documents the renter must present (licence, ratings, recent flight experience).

When to consult a specialist tax expert

As soon as private rental becomes regular, generates significant income, or involves several countries — an owner resident in one country, an aircraft registered in another, renters of various nationalities — it becomes essential to consult an accountant or tax lawyer specialising in general aviation. This professional can confirm the applicable tax regime, the correct VAT position, and the most suitable legal structure (direct ownership, company, co-ownership), thereby avoiding a tax reassessment that is often far more costly than the advisory fees.

Before offering your aircraft for rent or renting one from another private individual, take the time to compare the available structures, formalise a written agreement, and confirm your tax position with a professional: these simple precautions avoid the vast majority of disputes seen in this type of transaction.
