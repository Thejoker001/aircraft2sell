The Light Aviation Market in Central Europe: A Comparison

Poland, Czechia, Slovakia, Hungary: a side-by-side look at supply, prices, taxation and registration procedures for buying a used light aircraft in Central Europe in 2026.

A fast-growing market

Central Europe — Poland, Czechia, Slovakia and Hungary — has become, over the past fifteen years, one of the most dynamic hubs for light aviation and microlights in Europe. A more moderate cost of living, a dense network of local airfields and a long-standing aeronautical industrial tradition (notably in Czechia and Poland) explain this vitality. Several microlight and light two-seater manufacturers are based in the region, a proximity that feeds an abundant used-aircraft market, often more accessible than in Western Europe.

For a buyer based in Western Europe, these markets represent a real opportunity: well-maintained aircraft, sometimes cheaper than in France or Germany for equivalent engine potential, but with register, language and taxation specifics that need to be anticipated before committing.

Poland: the region's largest market

Poland concentrates the largest supply of used light aircraft and microlights in Central Europe. The country hosts several active manufacturers of two-seaters and ultralight machines, which feeds a substantial secondary market and often competitive prices. Leisure flying is also supported by a very active network of flying clubs, particularly around Warsaw, Kraków and Poznań.

Polish listings cover the full range: recent two-seat microlights, used touring single-engines such as the Cessna 172 or Piper PA-28, as well as older aircraft at attractive entry prices. Registration falls under the Polish civil aviation authority (Urząd Lotnictwa Cywilnego, ULC), with the SP- prefix.

Czechia: the cradle of microlights and light aircraft

The Czech Republic enjoys a particular reputation in European light aviation: it hosts a high number of well-known microlight and light two-seater manufacturers, whose output is largely exported. This industrial density translates into a very rich domestic used-aircraft market, with recent, well-equipped aircraft.

The Czech civil aviation authority (Úřad pro civilní letectví, ÚCL) manages registration under the OK- prefix. The Czech market is also a classic stopover for buyers from other Central and Western European countries, thanks to the overall quality of the fleet and the dynamism of local maintenance shops.

Slovakia: a smaller but active market

Smaller than its Polish and Czech neighbours, the Slovak market nonetheless remains active, supported by a well-established leisure flying culture and strong geographical and cultural proximity to Czechia (many aircraft exchanges between the two countries). Used supply is concentrated on microlights, light two-seaters and a handful of classic touring single-engines.

Registration falls under the Slovak civil aviation authority (Dopravný úrad), with the OM- prefix. The smaller size of the market often means a somewhat longer search to find the exact aircraft sought, offset by less upward pressure on prices.

Hungary: attractive prices and growing supply

The Hungarian light aviation market has grown markedly in recent years, driven by an expanding sport and leisure flying culture and by operating costs generally lower than in Western Europe. Used supply remains somewhat less abundant than in neighbouring Poland or Czechia, but prices are often very competitive for comparable aircraft.

Registration is managed by the Hungarian civil aviation authority, under the HA- prefix. With an ageing fleet in some segments, the Hungarian market is also seeing a growing supply of modern microlights, in line with regional demand.

Price and tax comparison

The figures below give an indicative view of prices observed in Central Europe for aircraft comparable to those found elsewhere in the European Union, generally with a slight discount compared with Western Europe for equivalent engine potential.

Two-seat microlight€10,000 to €45,000
Light single-engine (Cessna 172, Piper PA-28)€40,000 to €100,000
High-performance single-engine (glass cockpit)€120,000 to €250,000

On the tax side, the general EU VAT principles apply in all four countries: a sale between EU resident private individuals is generally VAT-exempt, while a sale by a professional or a company may be subject to it. National VAT rates, however, differ noticeably from one country to another, which can matter for a professional buyer or a leasing company.

Tip — Always compare prices at equivalent engine potential (hours remaining before TBO, avionics condition) rather than build year alone: the price gap between Central and Western Europe is often explained more by this factor than by the country of sale itself.

Registration and cross-border procedures

As with any registration transfer within the European Union, buying an aircraft in Poland, Czechia, Slovakia or Hungary for operation in another member state requires deregistration from the register of origin before the new registration. Common EASA rules govern this transfer and make it generally simpler than an import from outside the European Union, although administrative timelines and the responsiveness of local authorities can vary from one country to another.

  • Certificate of airworthiness and airworthiness review certificate (ARC) — check these first, including their mutual recognition within EASA.
  • Airframe, engine and propeller logbooks — often written in the local language: plan for translation or the help of a bilingual contact for the inspection.
  • Bill of sale and proof of deregistration from the register of origin — essential to start the new registration in your country of operation.

Practical tips for buying in Central Europe

Beyond the regulatory aspects, a few practical precautions help secure a cross-border purchase in Central Europe. The language barrier remains the most frequently underestimated point: listings and maintenance logbooks are often written in the local language, and it is recommended to use a mechanic or broker who speaks the seller's language for the pre-purchase inspection and for reviewing the documents.

Ferrying the aircraft to its destination country must also be budgeted and planned in advance, along with temporary insurance covering the ferry flight. Finally, as with any used purchase in Europe, a pre-purchase inspection by a certified mechanic independent of the seller remains the best protection against unpleasant surprises, whatever the aircraft's country of origin.

It is also worth checking the local availability of spare parts and approved workshops for the model under consideration: some aircraft types that are very common in Central Europe benefit from a good local maintenance network, while rarer models may require support from a workshop located in a neighbouring country. Asking a local club or regional distributor in advance often helps avoid unpleasant surprises after the purchase.

Finally, allowing a realistic negotiation margin remains relevant in these markets as elsewhere in Europe: an aircraft that has been for sale for a long time, or whose seller wants to close the deal quickly before a change of register, often leaves more room for negotiation than on a tighter Western European market.

Key takeaway — Central European markets often offer good value for money, but document due diligence and ferry-flight logistics require a bit more preparation than a local purchase.
