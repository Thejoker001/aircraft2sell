Why owner-pilots choose the TBM 850

The Daher TBM 850 (previously sold under the Socata brand, now part of the Daher group) is a fast single-engine turboprop designed to cover long distances in far less time than a conventional piston single. Its pressurised cabin allows cruising at high altitude above most turbulence and weather, making it a favourite travel tool for owner-pilots and small business operators alike.

On the used market, the TBM 850 stands out for its high cruise performance within its category, solid payload capacity and avionics that evolved significantly over the production run. This range of configurations means buyers should clearly define the model year and equipment level they want before comparing listings.

The type draws both pilots upgrading from a capable piston single into a turboprop and owners of jets looking for a cheaper aircraft to operate on short-to-medium legs. The ability to use shorter runways than those typically required by a business jet is another point buyers often mention when considering this category of aircraft.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used TBM 850 depends mainly on the age of the aircraft, accumulated airframe and engine hours, the engine's remaining potential before overhaul, and the avionics fitted. Older examples with less remaining engine potential trade at a noticeably more accessible entry level than newer, better-equipped versions, which represent a considerably larger investment.

Beyond the purchase price, your budget should include ancillary costs: a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic qualified on the type, registration and transfer fees, insurance suited to a turboprop, and possibly an avionics upgrade if the aircraft isn't fitted with the latest-generation systems.

What to check before buying

As with any used aircraft, a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic qualified on the type is essential before finalising a transaction. For a TBM 850, a few points deserve particular attention given the technical complexity of a turboprop.

  • Complete engine history: hours elapsed since the last overhaul, compliance with the manufacturer's maintenance program, traceability of replaced parts.
  • Condition of the cabin pressurisation system and its seals, which must operate without leaks to guarantee comfort and endurance at altitude.
  • Propeller operation and compliance with its periodic inspections, a component directly tied to flight performance and safety.
  • Condition and updates of the integrated avionics, as well as compliance with current regulatory requirements (transponder, traffic-monitoring systems).
  • Complete logbook and technical file, with no grey areas in the aircraft's history or any past incidents.
Good to know — Never rely solely on the seller's statements about overall condition: a written independent inspection report from a shop familiar with the type remains the best protection against a bad surprise after the purchase.

The PT6 engine: monitoring and overhaul

The TBM 850 is powered by a turboprop engine from the PT6 family, well known for its robustness and longevity when maintained according to the manufacturer's program. Like any turboprop, this engine must undergo a major overhaul after a number of hours set by the engine manufacturer, an operation that represents a significant expense to plan for over the life of ownership.

Before buying, it's essential to ask the seller for the number of hours remaining before the next overhaul, along with the complete history of work performed on the powerplant. An engine close to its limit directly affects the negotiated price, since the buyer will need to budget for this major overhaul.

Avionics and cabin equipment

Different model years of the TBM 850 were delivered with varying avionics levels, ranging from older suites to fully integrated digital cockpits on the most recent examples. The equipment level has a direct impact on day-to-day ease of use, compliance with current regulatory requirements, and resale value.

It's worth precisely checking which avionics generation is installed, whether the necessary updates are present, and whether the aircraft is compatible with the traffic-monitoring equipment now required in many European airspaces.

Maintenance and operating costs

The operating cost of a TBM 850 remains higher than that of a piston single, owing to the complexity of the turboprop and the fuel it uses. The main expense items remain the mandatory annual inspection, insurance, parking, fuel and, in the longer term, the engine overhaul once the hourly potential is reached.

Having the aircraft maintained at an approved shop familiar with the type helps limit unpleasant surprises and preserve its resale value. Rigorous, well-documented maintenance records are a strong argument at a future resale.

Tip — Always ask for the latest maintenance invoices as well as the report from the last engine overhaul before making an offer: these documents give an accurate picture of how seriously the aircraft has been maintained.

Type training and insurance

Flying a TBM 850 requires specific type training, usually delivered by approved training organisations, given the high performance and complexity of the turboprop compared with a conventional piston single. This training is also a factor insurers take into account when assessing a buyer's application.

Insurance for a TBM 850 depends heavily on the pilot's experience on type, total flight hours and recency of experience. It's worth checking insurance terms before finalising the purchase, as they can materially affect the overall cost of ownership.

Resale and the used market

The TBM 850 benefits from an active used market, supported by a sizeable installed fleet and good parts availability through the Daher network. A well-maintained aircraft with a transparent history, comfortable remaining engine potential and up-to-date avionics generally resells more easily and on better terms than one whose technical file has grey areas.

Comparing several listings of similar models remains the best way to assess a fair price, both for buying and for preparing a future resale. Price differences between two aircraft of the same model year are almost always explained by remaining engine potential and the quality of the avionics fitted.

Finally, it's worth keeping in mind that selling a turboprop like the TBM 850 generally involves more experienced buyers than a conventional piston single does: a complete technical file, quality photos and full transparency about the aircraft's history make negotiation markedly easier and speed up the time to close a sale.
