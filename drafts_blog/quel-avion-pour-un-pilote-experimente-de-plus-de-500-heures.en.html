Which Aircraft for a Pilot with 500+ Hours of Experience

Once you cross 500 flight hours, your needs shift: cruise speed, range, technical demands and running costs become the real decision factors. A comparison to guide your next purchase.

Why experience changes the equation

A pilot who has logged more than 500 flight hours no longer has the same constraints or the same ambitions as someone just starting out in aircraft ownership. Initial training and the first few hundred hours are usually flown on simple, fixed-gear, fixed-pitch aircraft designed to teach safely rather than to perform. With experience comes the desire for an aircraft that flies faster, carries further and can genuinely be used as a transportation tool, not just for local leisure flying.

Crossing 500 hours often reflects a real jump in skill: tighter procedure management, better anticipation, and a more comfortable handle on cockpit workload. That foundation is exactly what allows a pilot to confidently step up to more complex aircraft — retractable gear, variable-pitch propeller, denser avionics, pressurization or a turboprop — that would otherwise overload a pilot still in the learning phase.

Choosing the right aircraft at this stage comes down to three main factors: the actual mission (business trips, long-distance family travel, pure flying enjoyment), the total budget available — purchase and operating costs combined — and willingness to invest time in additional ratings and type-specific training.

Fast singles: Mooney, Bonanza, Cirrus SR22

For an experienced pilot who wants more speed and comfort without jumping straight to a twin, fast retractable-gear singles are a natural next step. The Mooney, known for its efficient laminar-flow wing, delivers a high cruise speed for moderate fuel burn, at the cost of a narrower cabin and extra vigilance on the retractable gear and flight controls during the pre-purchase inspection.

The Beechcraft Bonanza, with its reputation for ruggedness and, on certain generations, its distinctive tail design, offers an attractive balance of speed, payload and cabin comfort, making it a frequent choice for long-distance family travel. The Cirrus SR22, for its part, combines a composite structure, factory-standard modern avionics and an integrated emergency parachute — a safety argument that particularly appeals to pilots moving up from a trainer to a more capable personal aircraft.

  • Mooney: high cruise speed, moderate fuel burn, narrower cabin.
  • Beechcraft Bonanza: ruggedness, solid speed-to-payload balance, large installed used-market base.
  • Cirrus SR22: modern standard avionics, built-in parachute system, specific maintenance to plan for.

Flying far and fast: TB-class and complex retractable-gear aircraft

For an experienced pilot whose main use is long-distance family or business travel, stepping up to a complex aircraft — retractable gear, variable-pitch propeller, sometimes turbocharging — meaningfully changes the flying experience. Turbocharged variants like the SR22T allow you to fly higher, above some of the weather that causes trouble at lower altitudes, and to take advantage of favourable winds aloft to boost ground speed.

Socata TB-family aircraft, older but still sought after on the used market, also offer a good balance of cabin comfort, payload and reasonable running costs, for a pilot who isn't necessarily chasing maximum performance but wants a comfortable cabin for multi-hour flights.

This category of aircraft requires, in return, finer engine management (mixture, temperatures, monitoring engine parameters) and additional type-specific training, usually provided by a qualified instructor or the seller as part of the transaction.

Stepping up to a twin: redundancy and new constraints

With experience, some pilots consider moving up to a light twin, mainly for the engine redundancy it provides when flying over mountains, open water or terrain that would be difficult to reach in the event of a failure. That redundancy comes at a cost: two engines to maintain, generally higher insurance, and increased workload in the event of an actual engine failure, which requires regular specific training to stay proficient.

Moving to a twin only makes sense if the actual flight mission justifies it — long trips, recurring flights over water or mountains, professional use — because the extra operating cost compared with a fast single is significant and needs to be weighed against the real safety benefit being sought.

Good to know — A light twin's engine redundancy doesn't eliminate all risk: it requires regular training on asymmetric engine failure to be a genuine safety asset rather than a theoretical one.

The turboprop: the next step up

For the most experienced pilots, with a substantial budget and a regular long-distance flying mission, a single- or twin-engine turboprop is a further step up. Cruise speed and altitude well above a piston aircraft, a reputation for engine reliability, and increased payload make it a remarkably efficient travel tool, at the cost of an acquisition and operating budget far above that of a piston single.

This choice is usually made by pilots who fly intensively, for professional use or very frequent trips, and who have progressively built their experience on increasingly capable piston aircraft before taking this step.

Real running costs by aircraft profile

Annual running costs vary considerably depending on the type of aircraft chosen, and this line item — more than the initial purchase price — often determines whether a choice makes sense over the long run. A fixed-gear single remains the cheapest aircraft to maintain; a retractable-gear single adds a specific maintenance item; a twin roughly doubles most powerplant-related costs; a turboprop involves much higher engine overhaul costs but often more predictable reliability and maintenance intervals.

Before deciding between these categories, it's worth working out the actual expected annual cost — insurance, hangarage, annual inspection, engine reserve — rather than focusing solely on the aircraft's list price.

Ratings and insurance to plan for

Moving up to a new aircraft category almost always requires additional training: a class rating for retractable gear or a variable-pitch propeller, a multi-engine rating for a twin, a type rating for a turboprop or jet. These ratings cost time and money that need to be factored into the overall project budget, just like the aircraft's purchase price.

Insurance follows the same logic: an experienced pilot generally gets more favourable terms, but moving to a more complex or more powerful aircraft can push the premium up, particularly during the first hours on type, before the insurer considers the pilot's experience on that specific model sufficient.

  • Check the insurer's required hours on type before committing to a purchase.
  • Budget for additional training (class, multi-engine, type) on top of the purchase price.
  • Plan for a supervised familiarization period, even as an experienced pilot moving to a new type.

Buying used: what really matters at this level

For an experienced pilot buying a more complex aircraft, maintenance history and the consistency of engine hours matter even more than for a simple first purchase. A complete logbook, clearly traceable overhauls and a pre-purchase inspection carried out by an independent mechanic qualified on the type remain the best protections against an unpleasant surprise, regardless of the aircraft's complexity.

Comparing several listings for equivalent models, focusing on remaining engine potential, avionics level and well-documented maintenance quality, remains the best way to decide between two aircraft in the same category and avoid overpaying for technical potential that has already largely been used up.
