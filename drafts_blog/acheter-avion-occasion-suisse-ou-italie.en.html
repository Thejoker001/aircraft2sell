Buying a Used Aircraft in Switzerland or Italy: Which Country to Choose

Market, prices, taxation, registration and maintenance: a comparison between Switzerland and Italy to help you decide where to look for your used aircraft.

Two neighbouring Alpine markets, two very different frameworks

Switzerland and Italy share a common border and part of their Alpine terrain, but their light-aviation markets rest on very different frameworks. Switzerland, outside the European Union but integrated into the European airspace through bilateral agreements with the EU and EASA, benefits from high purchasing power, a particularly well-maintained aircraft fleet and high-quality aviation infrastructure, notably in the mountains. Italy, an EU member state, has a larger market by volume, concentrated mainly in the north of the country, with a varied supply ranging from classic light aircraft to microlights.

A buyer based in Switzerland can perfectly well find an interesting aircraft in Italy, and vice versa, provided they understand the administrative and customs specifics of each country before entering a cross-border transaction, given that Switzerland is not an EU member.

Size and dynamism of the used-aircraft market

The Swiss used-aircraft market is relatively small in volume, reflecting the size of the country, but it is characterised by a generally very high standard of maintenance and a supply skewed towards well-equipped touring and mountain-capable aircraft. The density of airfields and mountain airstrips in Switzerland also makes it an active market for aircraft suited to mountain flying.

The Italian used-aircraft market is larger in absolute volume, particularly in the north of the country (Lombardy, Veneto, Piedmont) where most Italian general aviation is concentrated. Supply there tends to lean a little more towards microlights and recently certified or amateur-built light aircraft, with a notable appetite for economical types to operate.

Comparing prices

Price levels between the two markets differ noticeably: the Swiss market generally shows higher prices for a comparable model, in line with the cost of living and the often very high average maintenance standard of Swiss aircraft. The Italian market generally offers more accessible entry prices for equivalent models, though it calls for extra care checking each example's maintenance history, as standards of upkeep can vary more widely from one aircraft to another.

In both cases, comparing several listings of similar models remains the best way to assess a fair price before committing, wherever the coveted aircraft happens to be.

Seasonality also plays a role: the Italian market often sees a peak in listings at the end of the flying season, whereas the Swiss market, more stable across the year thanks to mountain flying extending over several seasons, varies a little less sharply from month to month. Widening your search to both countries therefore often provides access to a wider, more stable choice throughout the year.

Taxation and purchase-related costs

Since Switzerland is not an EU member, a transaction between Switzerland and Italy constitutes an import or export operation for customs purposes, rather than a simple intra-community exchange as would be the case between two EU member states. This means checking precisely the applicable customs formalities, as well as Swiss or Italian VAT depending on the aircraft's destination country and the buyer's status (private individual or professional).

Using a professional familiar with cross-border transactions involving Switzerland (broker, aviation lawyer) helps avoid procedural mistakes that can prove costly, particularly regarding VAT, customs documentation and the specific formalities tied to Switzerland's particular status outside the European Union.

Registration and administrative procedures

In Switzerland, civil aircraft registration is handled by FOCA (the Federal Office of Civil Aviation), under the HB- prefix. In Italy, it is ENAC (Ente Nazionale per l'Aviazione Civile) that maintains the national register, under the I- prefix. An aircraft bought in one of the two countries can be registered in the other, depending on the buyer's choice: keeping the registration of the country of purchase is not mandatory. This change, however, involves specific procedures with the destination authority, including submitting the complete technical file, checking the validity of the certificate of airworthiness and, where applicable, a compliance inspection.

  • Check that the logbook, technical file and the aircraft's actual condition are consistent before any change of registration.
  • Plan ahead for administrative timelines, which can vary noticeably between FOCA and ENAC depending on their current workload.
  • Check the specific customs formalities tied to Switzerland's status outside the European Union.
  • Budget for ferry-flight costs if an inspection or compliance work is needed before the transfer.

Maintenance network and parts availability

Switzerland has a network of approved maintenance workshops known for their rigour, particularly for touring aircraft and types suited to mountain flying, generally offering a high-end level of service. Italy also has a network of competent workshops, concentrated mainly in the north, but which can be sparser in some central and southern regions for less common types.

For rarer models or specific engines, it is worth checking in advance the availability of parts and the proximity of a specialist workshop before finalising the purchase, regardless of the aircraft's country of origin. The distance between the two countries remains modest from southern Switzerland (Ticino) and northern Italy, which makes ferrying an aircraft for maintenance between the two countries relatively easy when needed.

The language factor plays a particular role here: Ticino, the Italian-speaking part of Switzerland, makes it easier to deal with workshops in northern Italy, while a buyer from another Swiss region will often favour a German- or French-speaking workshop. In both cases, technical documentation in English is generally accepted by approved workshops in both countries.

Which country to favour depending on your profile

For a buyer looking for a touring or mountain-flying aircraft with impeccable maintenance and willing to pay the corresponding price, the Swiss market often remains the more reassuring option. For a more budget-conscious buyer willing to widen their search to find an aircraft at the best price, with extra care taken over maintenance history, the larger Italian market offers a wider choice.

Good to know — In all cases, a pre-purchase inspection by a qualified independent mechanic remains essential, whether the aircraft is located in Switzerland, Italy or elsewhere in Europe.

Climate can also steer the choice depending on intended use: a buyer mainly looking for a touring aircraft to fly year-round in mild conditions will often lean towards the Italian market, particularly in the south of the country, while a buyer who values mountain flying and a very high maintenance standard will often stay loyal to the Swiss market. The two approaches are not mutually exclusive: there is nothing stopping you from searching for an aircraft in Italy while planning to have it maintained in Switzerland, or vice versa, as long as ferrying distances remain reasonable.

Ultimately, the country of purchase matters less than the actual quality of the aircraft and how well it has been maintained, even though Switzerland's status outside the European Union adds a layer of formalities to anticipate. Comparing listings from both markets widens your options and gives you a better negotiating position, whether on price or on the terms of the transaction.
