Why the Evektor EV-97 Eurostar appeals to so many pilots

Born in the Czech Republic in the 1990s, the Evektor EV-97 Eurostar quickly established itself as a reference among low-wing, three-axis microlights. Its modern lines, wide two-seat cabin and reputedly easy handling have made it a favourite for training, leisure flying and the first steps of many pilots across Europe.

On the used market, the Eurostar benefits from a large fleet spread across Europe, which makes it easier to find spare parts, technical documentation and workshops familiar with the model. Its versatility - from pure leisure flying to intensive club training - explains a steady interest from buyers, whether they're looking for their first aircraft or a more comfortable second microlight than an entry-level model.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used EV-97 Eurostar mainly depends on the year built, total airframe and engine hours, the exact version (Eurostar, Eurostar SL or later evolutions) and the level of avionics fitted. An older example with basic analogue avionics trades at an accessible entry level, while a recent aircraft with a modern digital cockpit represents a significantly higher budget.

Beyond the purchase price, factor in an independent pre-purchase inspection by a mechanic experienced with the type, registration fees, microlight insurance and, if needed, an avionics or cabin interior refresh.

Versions and engine options to know

The EV-97 Eurostar has gone through several evolutions since its launch, with improvements to aerodynamics, composite materials and standard equipment. The more recent SL version stands out with airframe refinements and generally a more polished finish than the very first examples. It's important to identify exactly which version you're looking at during a search, since this affects performance, parts availability and the expected level of finish.

On the engine side, the vast majority of Eurostars in service are fitted with Rotax 900-series engines, whose reliability and maintenance network across Europe are well established. Before buying, check that the installed engine, the engine logbook and the declared SMOH hours are consistent, so you can anticipate the cost of a future major overhaul.

Since many Eurostars have served at flying schools or clubs, it's also worth looking into the aircraft's past usage intensity: an aircraft that has flown many hours in training is not necessarily a bad buy, provided maintenance has been rigorous and well documented throughout.

What to check before buying

As with any used microlight, a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic qualified on the type is essential before finalising a deal. For a Eurostar, a few points deserve particular attention.

  • Overall condition of the composite airframe and flight controls, especially if the aircraft has been used intensively at a flying school.
  • Complete engine history: engine hours, SMOH since the last major overhaul, and traceability of replaced parts.
  • Quality and consistency of the logbook, showing regular tracking at an approved workshop rather than occasional, poorly documented work.
  • Condition of the canopy, cabin sealing and safety equipment (harnesses, fire extinguisher), which must remain in good working order.
  • Compliance of the microlight identification record and the aircraft's administrative file, with no grey areas in its ownership history.
Good to know — A Eurostar that has served at a club isn't necessarily more worn than a lightly used private aircraft: rigorous, regular maintenance matters far more than the hours shown on the clock.

Microlight status and formalities in Europe

The EV-97 Eurostar falls under microlight status in most European countries, with registration, licensing and inspection rules that differ from one country to another. Before buying, check with your national aviation federation or competent authority to understand exactly which formalities apply to this type of aircraft and to your intended use.

Always verify that the aircraft's administrative file (identification record, proof of conformity to type, registration history) is consistent with the regulations of the country where you intend to operate it. If you have doubts about a specific regulatory point, have the file validated by a competent body or federation before committing, rather than relying solely on the seller's statements.

Maintenance and operating costs

The operating cost of a Eurostar remains moderate compared to a certified light aircraft of the same category, which is one of its main strengths. The main expense items are the periodic inspection under the applicable maintenance programme, microlight insurance, parking, fuel and, over the longer term, engine overhaul once the hourly potential is reached.

Having the aircraft maintained at an approved workshop familiar with the type helps limit unpleasant surprises and preserve its resale value. Rigorous, well-documented maintenance records - especially for an aircraft that has seen school use - are a strong argument at a future resale.

Tip — Always ask for the latest maintenance invoices and the details of the maintenance programme followed before making an offer: they give an accurate picture of how seriously the aircraft has been maintained.

Resale and the used market

The Eurostar benefits from an active used market in Europe, supported by a large installed fleet and good parts availability. A well-maintained aircraft with a transparent history and a complete logbook generally resells more easily and on better terms than one whose technical file has grey areas.

Comparing several listings of aircraft from a similar generation and engine remains the best way to assess a fair price, both for buying and for preparing a future resale. Price differences between two similar Eurostars are most often explained by remaining engine potential, the quality of the avionics and how rigorously maintenance has been tracked.

Before signing, take the time to arrange a test flight if circumstances allow, and to meet the seller or current operator to discuss the aircraft's real history. These informal conversations usefully complement the logbook and often reveal details that don't always appear in the technical file, such as how regularly the aircraft was flown or minor incidents that were never logged.
