Choosing your home airfield: practical criteria

Proximity, infrastructure, runway type, costs, community and opening hours: the concrete criteria for deciding where to base your aircraft.

Why choosing a home airfield matters so much

Your home airfield is not a minor administrative detail: it's where you will spend most of your time with your aircraft, outside of flying itself. Pre-flight preparation, maintenance, conversations with other pilots, storage — a poorly chosen airfield can turn every outing into a hassle, while a good choice makes regular flying easier and removes everyday friction.

The right choice depends heavily on your profile: occasional leisure flying, intensive club activity, a travel-oriented project, or an aircraft that needs specific infrastructure (a hard runway, a minimum length, particular fuel). There is no "ideal" airfield in the abstract — only an airfield suited to how you actually plan to use it.

Geographic proximity and accessibility

The distance between home and airfield directly shapes how often you fly. A 20-minute drive encourages spontaneous end-of-day outings; an hour or more naturally restricts flying to weekends and dedicated days. Be honest about your real availability before choosing a more distant field for cost or slot-availability reasons.

  • Actual drive time at the hours you typically fly, not just mid-day on a weekday.
  • Road access and on-site parking, especially if you carry gear (covers, tools, luggage for a trip).
  • Public transport links if you don't always have a car available.

Infrastructure and services available

Beyond the runway itself, on-site services shape much of your day-to-day comfort. A well-equipped airfield saves time on every visit; a minimal one requires more personal organisation.

  • On-site fuel — available during opening hours, with or without a self-service card outside those hours.
  • Approved maintenance workshop — having a maintenance organisation on site avoids ferry flights for every periodic check.
  • Hangars and parking slots — check real availability, not just theoretical; ask about the current waiting time.
  • Flight planning room and internet access — useful for checking weather or filing a flight plan before a trip.
  • Café or clubhouse — a secondary criterion, but one that affects how much time you'll want to spend at the field outside of flying.

Runway type and operational constraints

Runway surface (hard or grass) and length directly determine which aircraft can use it comfortably, especially in a crosswind or on a waterlogged surface. A light, low-mass aircraft often copes fine on a well-maintained grass strip; a heavier aircraft, or one less tolerant of soft ground, will need a longer hard runway.

  • Available runway length relative to your aircraft's takeoff and landing distances, with a comfortable safety margin.
  • Surface type and how well it's maintained, especially a grass strip after periods of rain.
  • Runway orientation relative to local prevailing winds, and whether there are crossing runways.
  • Obstacles on approach or departure (terrain, power lines, built-up areas) that can constrain certain flight paths.
  • Airfield status (controlled, AFIS, uncontrolled) and typical traffic level during the hours you fly.
Tip — Before committing, fly several arrivals and departures at the airfield you're considering, ideally in different weather conditions, to judge the real constraints for yourself.

Costs: parking, fuel and fees

The overall cost of basing at an airfield goes well beyond the advertised parking fee. Compare every line item before deciding, as differences between fields can add up significantly over a year.

  • Parking fee — annual or monthly rate, outdoor or hangared, with or without a minimum commitment.
  • Fuel price — differences of several cents per litre between nearby fields can weigh heavily on an annual budget for an aircraft flying many hours.
  • Landing fees — applied on every movement at some fields, including for based aircraft, depending on local policy.
  • Flying club membership or usage fees — where access to the field requires compulsory club membership.

Flying club atmosphere and community

A lively airfield, with an active pilot community and a vibrant flying club, makes ongoing learning easier, encourages sharing good practice and helps organise group outings. For a newer pilot, this environment has real educational value beyond simply parking the aircraft.

By contrast, a quieter field with less traffic suits pilots who value peace and the immediate availability of a runway without queuing or congestion. Neither atmosphere is better in itself: visit the field on a busy day (a sunny weekend) to form a realistic impression before choosing.

Opening hours and availability

An airfield's actual opening hours, and those of its fuel station and related services, vary widely from one field to another and don't always match the hours shown on aeronautical charts or official websites. Some fields offer permanent free access with self-service fuel, others close outside set hours, which can significantly constrain evening or early-morning flying.

Word of caution — Confirm actual hours with the operator or flying club rather than relying on a single documentary source, and ask explicitly about the procedure outside official hours if you plan on evening flights.

How to make your final decision

List your two or three non-negotiable criteria (maximum budget, maximum distance, minimum runway length) and use them to quickly rule out fields that don't fit, before carefully comparing the remaining candidates on secondary criteria. Visit in person every field you're seriously considering rather than relying solely on information found online: atmosphere, the real condition of facilities and how welcoming the staff are cannot be judged remotely.

Finally, keep in mind that the choice isn't necessarily permanent: many owners change their home airfield over time, as their needs, budget or place of residence evolve.
