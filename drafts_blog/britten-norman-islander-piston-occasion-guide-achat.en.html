Why the Islander is still sought after after 50+ years

The Britten-Norman BN-2 Islander is a utility piston twin designed for ruggedness and short take-off and landing (STOL) performance. In production since the late 1960s, it has gone through several versions over the decades, with updates to its engines and equipment, but a deliberately simple overall architecture: high wing, fixed gear, and a metal structure that is easy to repair in a workshop. This intentional simplicity explains why the type remains in active service across many utility fleets worldwide, from inter-island links to surveillance missions.

On the used market, the Islander appeals to a fairly specific type of buyer: operators of small regional feeder airlines, skydiving schools looking for a platform able to carry several jumpers per rotation, or private owners wanting a twin capable of landing on short, unprepared strips. It is not a comfortable touring aircraft in the sense of a modern single-engine tourer, but a working tool whose value is judged above all on its reliability and parts availability.

Before committing, it is therefore essential to clearly define the intended use: an Islander bought purely for occasional family touring rarely makes economic sense, whereas intensive professional or club use makes it far easier to amortise the acquisition and operating cost of a twin in this category.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used Islander varies significantly depending on the age of the airframe, the status of both engines (remaining potential before the next overhaul), the avionics fitted, and the operating history — an aircraft that has flown intensive commercial operations has a very different profile from one owned by a private individual with lighter use. The oldest examples, with reduced potential on both powerplants, trade at a noticeably more accessible level than newer or refurbished versions, which represent a far larger investment.

As with any twin, the purchase price is only part of the real budget. Your financing plan should include ferry costs (often significant for a utility aircraft based far from its future home base), an independent pre-purchase inspection, registration and transfer fees, and a provision for bringing the avionics up to standard if they are dated.

Point of caution — On a twin of this generation, an attractive asking price almost always hides reduced potential on one or both powerplants: always check this figure before comparing two listings against each other.

Engines and remaining engine potential

The Islander is fitted with two piston engines, and the status of each must be checked independently: it is not uncommon for an example to show very different potential between the left and right engine, depending on the history of replacements or overhauls carried out over the years. Each engine's logbook should therefore be examined separately, paying particular attention to the SMOH hours (hours since the last overhaul) of each one.

On a utility twin of this type, the cost of an engine overhaul represents a significant part of the operating budget and should be anticipated at the time of purchase if the remaining potential is low. Asking for full traceability of parts replaced during previous work, as well as the list of airworthiness directives (mandatory safety instructions) already complied with, helps you better estimate upcoming costs before committing.

  • SMOH hours for each engine, checked separately and weighed against the estimated cost of the next overhaul.
  • Consistency between the two engine logbooks and the aircraft's overall logbook.
  • History of rotating parts replacement (propellers, magnetos, accessories) on each powerplant.

Pre-purchase inspection points to watch

A pre-purchase inspection carried out by an independent mechanic qualified on the type remains essential, all the more so because the Islander has often seen demanding utility use (short strips, unprepared runways, frequent rotations) that puts more strain on the airframe than typical touring use.

  • Condition of the structure and landing gear, particularly stressed by use on short or unpaved strips.
  • Signs of corrosion, common on examples that have operated in coastal or island environments.
  • Complete logbook and technical file, with clear traceability if the aircraft has changed registration or country of operation.
  • Validity of the certificate of airworthiness (CofA) and compliance with the latest airworthiness directives applicable to the type.
  • Condition of the avionics and mission equipment if the aircraft has been configured for a specific use (skydiving, surveillance, cargo).
Good to know — An Islander that has operated exclusively in private use with few daily rotations generally shows a less fatigued airframe than an example from intensive commercial operations, even at comparable total hours.

Maintenance and operating costs

Operating a piston twin like the Islander involves higher fixed costs than an equivalent single-engine aircraft: two sets of engines to maintain, higher fuel consumption, and generally higher insurance given the category of aircraft. On the other hand, the classic metal structure and the type's wide use in the utility world make it easier to source spare parts and find mechanics trained on the type, notably through operator networks and workshops specialising in utility aviation.

Rigorous, well-documented maintenance records for both engines and the airframe remain the strongest argument for justifying a higher asking price — and conversely, the main point of negotiation for a buyer facing an incomplete file.

Typical missions: island hops, skydiving, cargo

The Islander is above all a mission aircraft. Its short-field capability, modular cabin configuration and ruggedness make it a frequent choice for inter-island feeder links, light cargo transport, or skydiving clubs and schools that value its ability to carry several jumpers per rotation through a wide door that makes in-flight exit easier.

For private use, the aircraft can also suit an owner looking for a twin able to reach strips that other aircraft cannot use, provided they accept the more spartan cabin comfort and higher operating costs compared with a touring single-engine aircraft.

Resale market and liquidity

The Islander's resale market remains smaller and more specialised than that of the most widespread touring singles: potential buyers are essentially professional operators, skydiving schools or enthusiasts of utility twins. This lower liquidity generally means a longer resale timeline and greater sensitivity to a well-documented, good condition aircraft at the time of listing.

An example with a transparent history, comfortable remaining potential on both powerplants and a complete technical file will nonetheless resell on far better terms than an aircraft whose history has grey areas, particularly in a niche market where trust between professionals plays a significant role in the purchase decision.
