Buying a Used Aircraft in Greece or Italy: Which Country to Choose

Two Mediterranean markets, two very different profiles

Greece and Italy both offer a climate well suited to visual flying almost all year round, but their used-aircraft markets are not comparable in size. Italy's market is one of the most active in southern Europe: a long-standing flying club tradition, a strong gliding culture in the north of the country, a substantial microlight fleet and a large number of airfields spread across the whole territory. This density translates into an abundant supply of classic single-engine aircraft (Cessna, Piper, Robin) and a real opportunity to compare several similar aircraft before deciding.

The Greek market is significantly smaller. The number of airfields open to light aviation is more limited, concentrated around Athens, Thessaloniki and a handful of islands, and the number of used aircraft available at any given time is naturally lower. In exchange, an aircraft based in Greece benefits from a particularly long flying season, a real advantage for intensive leisure use. A buyer looking for the widest possible choice will naturally lean towards Italy; a buyer already based in Greece, or specifically looking for an aircraft already based locally, won't really have a local alternative.

Price comparison by aircraft type

Price ranges depend above all on the aircraft's condition and remaining engine potential, far more than on the country where it is based. But market size affects negotiating room: a more abundant supply, as in Italy, makes comparison and therefore negotiation easier, while a smaller market, as in Greece, can leave less leverage for the buyer on an aircraft that is rare in its category.

Registration: HCAA in Greece, ENAC in Italy

In Greece, aircraft registration falls under the Hellenic Civil Aviation Authority (HCAA), under the SX- prefix. In Italy, it is ENAC (Ente Nazionale per l'Aviazione Civile) that maintains the register, under the I- prefix. Since both countries apply the common EASA framework, the principle of a transfer between private individuals remains the same as elsewhere in the European Union: an update of the registered owner if the aircraft is already registered locally, or deregistration from the register of origin followed by new registration for an imported aircraft.

  • Valid certificate of airworthiness (CofA) and ARC — check this before signing in both countries; the expiry date determines the first flight under your name.
  • Bill of sale — dated, signed by both parties, stating the aircraft's serial number.
  • Liability insurance certificate — required before any use under the new registration, in both Greece and Italy.
Good to know — Original Greek documents (certificate of airworthiness, logbook) may need an official translation for a non-Greek-speaking buyer, a point rarely needed for an Italian aircraft whose paperwork is often already partly available in English.

VAT and taxation compared

In both countries, a sale between EU resident private individuals is generally exempt from local VAT, while a sale carried out by a professional or a company may be subject to it. An aircraft imported from outside the European Union remains subject to import VAT, calculated on its customs value, at the standard national rate applicable in the country of import. The principles are therefore identical in Greece and Italy; what differs in practice is how familiar local professionals are with aviation customs procedures, which tends to be more developed in Italy given the volume of transactions.

Maintenance, parts and network availability

Italy has a much denser network of approved maintenance workshops, a direct consequence of the size of its light aviation fleet: common parts are available faster, more mechanics are trained on the most widespread types, and hangar space is more widely available in most regions. Greece, with a smaller fleet, has a maintenance network that is more geographically concentrated around the main mainland airfields; an aircraft based on a more remote island can mean longer waits for a specific part or for a qualified mechanic, something worth planning for before the purchase if you intend to base the aircraft far from the mainland.

Tip — If you are considering basing your aircraft on a Greek island, check beforehand that an approved workshop is nearby and what the usual lead times for parts delivery are before finalising the purchase.

Pre-purchase inspection: country-specific points

The foundation of a pre-purchase inspection is the same everywhere in Europe: consistency of the airframe, engine and propeller logbooks, engine hours history since the last major overhaul, recent maintenance invoices, and the absence of undisclosed structural damage. Two points deserve extra attention depending on the country. In Greece, an aircraft based near the coast or on an island is more exposed to corrosion linked to salty air: a careful inspection of the airframe and flight controls is recommended, even on a relatively young aircraft. In Italy, the diversity of uses (flying club, gliding, private leisure) means you should carefully check the aircraft's actual usage profile: an aircraft that has spent a long time in flying school or club use often accumulates more hours and cycles than its build year would suggest.

  • Up-to-date airframe, engine and propeller logbooks, with no chronological gaps, in both countries.
  • History of maintenance invoices from the last three years.
  • Targeted corrosion inspection for any aircraft based near the coast or on a Greek island.
Point of caution — Never finalise a purchase without having seen the original logbooks and having their consistency checked by a certified mechanic independent of the seller, whether the aircraft is Greek or Italian.

Pitfalls to avoid depending on the country

In Greece, the most common mistake is underestimating the time needed to obtain an official translation of the documents or to arrange for a qualified mechanic to visit a remote island, which can delay returning the aircraft to service after purchase. In Italy, the most common pitfall is the opposite: the density of supply can create a false sense of ease and lead buyers to overlook the history of an aircraft that has had several owners or seen intensive club use. In both cases, neglecting to check deregistration from the previous register for an imported aircraft remains a classic mistake, as does signing without having the exact condition of the optional equipment listed in the technical sheet confirmed in writing.

Tip — Compare several similar listings on Aircraft2Sell before committing, regardless of the country: the volume of offers available gives a reliable benchmark for price and overall market condition.

Greece or Italy: which country to choose for your profile

The choice depends above all on your usage project rather than on the country itself. If you are looking for the widest possible choice, a dense maintenance network and the ability to easily compare several similar aircraft, Italy remains the more comfortable option for a first purchase. If you have a plan to settle in Greece, a leisure use oriented towards a long flying season, or a specific interest in an aircraft already based locally, Greece remains a perfectly viable market as long as you plan for the logistical delays inherent to a smaller network. In all cases, a pre-purchase inspection by a professional independent of the seller remains the best protection, whichever registration country you choose.
