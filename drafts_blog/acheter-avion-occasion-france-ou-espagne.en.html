Buying a Used Aircraft in France or Spain: Which Country to Choose?

Market, prices, DGAC or AESA registration, taxation, climate and operating costs: a complete comparison to help you choose between France and Spain for your next used light aircraft purchase.

Two active markets, two different dynamics

France and Spain are among the most active light aviation markets in southern Europe, but with noticeably different profiles. France benefits from an extremely dense network of airfields and one of the continent's most developed flying club traditions, which translates into a large volume of listings and a very broad range of aircraft, from microlights to light twins, including the classics of touring aviation such as the Cessna 172, Robin and Piper PA-28.

Spain, for its part, offers a somewhat tighter market, but one driven by a climate favourable to flying year-round and by substantial flight-school activity, particularly in the south of the country. Supply there is a little less dense than in France, but aircraft based in Spain often show an intensive usage history linked to training, a point worth checking carefully before buying.

Compared prices by aircraft type

Price ranges remain broadly comparable between the two countries for an aircraft of equivalent type and condition, since the European used market largely behaves as a single connected system. A few nuances remain, however: in France, the density of supply sometimes allows slightly better-negotiated opportunities on the most common models, while in Spain, aircraft with heavy flight-school use can show a discount linked to higher flight hours, offset by the generally rigorous maintenance that training operations impose.

Registration: DGAC versus AESA

In France, aircraft registration falls under the Direction générale de l'Aviation civile (DGAC), with the F- prefix. In Spain, the competent authority is the Agencia Estatal de Seguridad Aérea (AESA), which issues registrations under the EC- prefix. In both cases, a purchase between private individuals where the aircraft is already registered in the country simply requires updating the registered owner, while an aircraft imported from another country requires prior deregistration from the register of origin.

  • Valid certificate of airworthiness and ARC — check this before signing, in both countries.
  • Bill of sale — dated, signed by both parties, stating the serial number.
  • Liability insurance certificate — required before first use under the new registration, in both France and Spain.
Good to know — Common EASA rules govern transfers between EU member states, which simplifies the process between France and Spain. Generally allow 2 to 8 weeks depending on how complete the file is, in either direction.

VAT and taxation compared

In both countries, a sale between private individuals is in principle not subject to VAT. A sale carried out by a professional or a company may, however, be subject to it, and an aircraft imported from a country outside the European Union remains liable for import VAT, calculated on its customs value and applied at the national rate of the importing country.

Beyond VAT, it is worth checking the local taxation specific to aircraft ownership (annual taxes, airport fees), which can vary considerably from one region to another within each country, particularly between a rural French airfield and a busier Spanish airport.

Climate and flying conditions

Climate is often the strongest argument made in favour of Spain: a noticeably higher number of flyable days across much of the country, particularly in the south and on the islands, allows more regular use of the aircraft throughout the year and reduces downtime caused by weather. France offers more pronounced climatic variation by region, with the north more often subject to limiting winter conditions, against a south and Mediterranean coastline with conditions close to those found in Spain.

This climate factor also has an indirect effect on aircraft condition: prolonged exposure to sun and heat (common in Spain when parked outside) can accelerate the ageing of certain interior equipment and paintwork, while the higher humidity found in some French regions can encourage corrosion on a poorly maintained or outdoor-stored aircraft. In both cases, hangar storage remains the best protection, regardless of country.

Cost of ownership: hangarage, fuel, maintenance

Overall ownership costs remain broadly similar between the two countries, with differences depending mostly on the chosen airfield rather than the country as a whole. A hangar near Paris or on the French Riviera can, for example, cost noticeably more than a comparable spot at a less central Spanish airfield, while some highly sought-after Spanish airports (Mallorca, Costa del Sol) charge rates close to, or even above, those of major French facilities.

  • Fuel: broadly comparable prices, with occasional differences depending on the airfield and its proximity to a major commercial airport.
  • Hangarage or shelter: wide variation by region and proximity to an urban or tourist hub, in both countries.
  • Maintenance: a dense network of approved shops in both countries for common models, with sometimes more limited availability for less common types.
  • Insurance: generally similar rates, varying mainly with pilot profile, declared use and insured value.

Which country suits your profile

There is no universal answer: the choice between France and Spain depends above all on the intended usage profile. A buyer who prioritises the density of supply, proximity to a very developed flying-club network and a wide choice of models will do well to look at the French market first. A buyer who above all wants regular, year-round flying backed by favourable weather may find more flexible operating conditions in Spain, particularly in the south of the country.

In both cases, a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic remains an essential step, regardless of the aircraft's country of registration or residence, as does a rigorous check of the maintenance logbook and usage history before signing anything.

Tip — Compare French and Spanish listings for aircraft of equivalent type and condition: this often gives the most accurate picture of the real market, beyond preconceived ideas about either country.
