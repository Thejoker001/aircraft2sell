Seaplane float maintenance: the complete guide

Corrosion, periodic inspection, dedicated logbook, winter storage and budget: everything you need to know to maintain — or buy used — a seaplane fitted with floats.

Why floats need maintenance of their own

A floatplane combines the usual mechanics of a light aircraft with the constraints of a boat hull. The floats are in permanent contact with fresh or salt water, subjected to repeated impacts on landing, abrasion from sand or gravel, and constant humidity inside their internal compartments. Without specific maintenance, separate from that of the airframe and engine, the floats often become the aircraft's weak point long before the powerplant shows any sign of wear.

Unlike a conventional landing gear, floats include several watertight compartments, bilge pumps, partially submerged control cables, and a metal or composite structure directly exposed to corrosion. Neglecting this maintenance doesn't just risk costly repairs: an undetected leak can unbalance the aircraft during landing or while taxiing on water, creating a genuine safety risk.

Corrosion: the floats' number one enemy

Corrosion is by far the leading cause of degradation on metal floats, usually aluminium. In salt water the process accelerates considerably: pitting corrosion, galvanic corrosion where steel or brass parts come into contact (screws, hinges, cables), and progressive attack on welds and internal bulkheads. In fresh water the risk also exists, especially in the presence of pollutants or pH variations, but it generally progresses more slowly.

A systematic fresh-water rinse after every salt-water flight remains the most effective and least costly preventive measure. It should cover the outside of the floats as well as the internal compartments accessible through the inspection hatches, where stagnant salt water accelerates corrosion out of sight.

  • Rinse the outside and inside of the compartments after every salt-water outing.
  • Apply an anti-corrosion treatment to hard-to-reach areas.
  • Regularly check the condition of sacrificial anodes where the aircraft is fitted with them.
  • Pay particular attention to contact points between different metals, the usual seat of galvanic corrosion.
Word of caution — Through-corrosion on a float is almost never an isolated incident: if one bulkhead is affected, neighbouring bulkheads must be inspected just as thoroughly, since humidity and salt often travel from one compartment to another.

Visual inspection and periodic checks

Beyond routine upkeep, floats are subject to periodic checks defined by the manufacturer and the aircraft's maintenance manual, generally more frequent than those for the airframe. The inspection covers the watertightness of each compartment, the condition of the bulkheads, the fittings attaching the floats to the airframe (struts, braces, shock mounts), the water rudders and their control cables.

A compartment-by-compartment watertightness test, often called a "pump out check", detects a leak before it becomes visible from the outside. Any water found in a compartment meant to stay dry must be documented in the logbook, measured, and its cause investigated before the next flight.

Checks to perform at every inspection

  • Water present in each compartment (pump out check) and traceability in the logbook.
  • Condition of the float-to-airframe fittings and braces, no play or cracking.
  • Operation and play of the water rudders and their cables.
  • Condition of the anti-corrosion coating and absence of paint blistering.
  • No deformation after a hard landing or an impact with a floating obstacle.

The float-specific logbook

When floats are not original equipment but installed under a certified conversion kit (STC), they often have their own dedicated logbook, separate from the airframe's, with their own time-in-service limits and applicable directives. When buying used, it is essential to check consistency between the engine hours recorded in the flight logbook and the floats' hours in service, as well as compliance with every airworthiness directive specific to the installed float kit.

An incomplete history, or a missing logbook for the floats, should be treated as a warning sign in exactly the same way as an inconsistent engine logbook: it makes it impossible to verify that scheduled major overhauls and prior repairs were actually carried out, and makes negotiating the resale price far harder.

Mooring, anchoring and day-to-day upkeep

How the aircraft is moored or anchored between flights has a direct impact on float lifespan. Poorly adjusted mooring lines create repeated friction points that wear the coating prematurely and encourage localized corrosion. Prolonged unattended anchoring exposes the floats to fouling by algae and mollusks, which accelerate corrosion and disrupt the hydrodynamics needed for takeoff.

Good day-to-day operating practices considerably limit the amount of heavy maintenance required later on.

  • Prefer a trailer or dry dock between uses rather than permanent anchoring.
  • Inspect and clean the waterline after every extended stay on the water.
  • Avoid mooring over rocky bottoms or in areas with heavy sand abrasion.

Winter storage and off-season lay-up

Off-season storage, especially in regions where freezing is possible, requires specific preparation. Residual water inside the compartments must be fully drained before any risk of freezing, otherwise the expansion of frozen water can crack an internal bulkhead. Inspection hatches should remain accessible to allow regular ventilation throughout storage, preventing condensation from building up inside the floats.

A check before returning to service after several months of inactivity is recommended, even if nothing abnormal was visible when the aircraft went into storage: corrosion sometimes keeps progressing slowly throughout lay-up with no outward warning sign.

Maintenance budget and costs to anticipate

Float maintenance is a budget line of its own that should be factored in from the moment the aircraft is purchased. On top of the usual maintenance cost of a light aircraft come periodic anti-corrosion treatment, possible replacement of water-rudder cables, bulkhead repairs in cases of advanced corrosion, and, in the most severe cases, a full major overhaul of the floats including their removal from the airframe.

Good to know — A seaplane operated mainly in salt water should budget significantly more for float maintenance than one used exclusively in fresh water, given how much faster corrosion progresses.

Buying a used seaplane: checking the floats

Before buying any used aircraft fitted with floats, a dedicated pre-purchase inspection, carried out by a mechanic familiar with this type of installation, is essential in addition to the usual airframe and engine inspection. It should include a compartment-by-compartment watertightness test, a thorough check of the most common corrosion-prone areas, and a full review of the dedicated float logbook, alongside the aircraft's certificate of airworthiness.

An aircraft that has operated mainly in salt water without a documented regular rinsing routine deserves extra scrutiny, even if the visual inspection reveals nothing abnormal at first glance: internal corrosion often progresses with no outward sign before reaching an advanced and costly stage.
