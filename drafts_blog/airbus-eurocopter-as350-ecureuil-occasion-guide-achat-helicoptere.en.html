The AS350 Ecureuil, a classic light single-engine helicopter

The Aerospatiale, later Eurocopter, AS350 "Ecureuil" (Squirrel) is one of the most widely produced and operated light single-engine turbine helicopters in the world, with a lineage going back to the late 1970s. Originally designed by Aerospatiale and later developed under Eurocopter and today Airbus Helicopters, it has become a reference for aerial work, light transport and surveillance missions, thanks to its ruggedness, relatively simple maintenance and a large installed fleet that keeps spare parts readily available.

On the used market, the Ecureuil comes in several successive generations, which differ mainly in engine and maximum certified weight. This range of variants explains a wide price spread, from fairly accessible older airframes to much more expensive recent examples, which makes it essential to pin down the exact variant you are after before comparing listings.

Ecureuil variants and their differences

Over the decades the AS350 has gone through several notable evolutions, mainly on the powerplant side. The most common variants found used are the B, BA, B1 and B2, fitted with successive generations of the Turbomeca (now Safran Helicopter Engines) Arriel engine, with increasing power and maximum weight across model years. The most recent and most powerful members of the family, rebranded H125 some years ago, sit on a noticeably higher price tier and are built for more demanding missions (high altitude, heavier loads).

Before comparing listings, it is essential to correctly identify the exact variant on offer: two Ecureuil aircraft that look similar can have very different market values depending on their engine and certification.

Typical uses on the used market

An AS350's previous operating history has a direct impact on its overall condition and resale value. The main uses encountered on the European used market are:

  • Aerial work: sling-loading equipment, support for forestry sites, patrol and monitoring of power lines and infrastructure.
  • Search, rescue and medical missions, depending on configuration and onboard equipment.
  • Light transport and tourism, in particular scenic flights in mountain or tourist areas.
  • Advanced flight training, for pilots transitioning to turbine aircraft.
  • Private ownership, a rarer but existing profile, especially for older variants.

An example that has seen heavy aerial-work use generally does not show the same wear pattern as one used mainly for occasional light transport: the full operating history is therefore one of the first things to request from the seller before any viewing.

Inspection points to watch

Beyond the standard checks that apply to any used helicopter (complete logbook, compliance with airworthiness directives, overall airframe condition), a few points deserve particular attention on an AS350.

  • Precise operating history (aerial work, training, private use) and consistency with the declared flight hours and logbook.
  • Condition and remaining life of the main rotor blades, rotor head and transmission components, life-limited parts whose replacement is a significant expense.
  • Time remaining before the next Arriel engine overhaul and consistency with the engine logbook and maintenance invoices.
  • Signs of corrosion or accelerated wear, particularly for aircraft operated in mountain, coastal or agricultural environments.
  • History of any incidents (hard landing, transmission damage, engine FOD) logged in the logbook or with the registration authority.
  • Compliance of onboard equipment (stretcher, winch, cargo hook) with the use the buyer intends.
Good to know — A pre-purchase inspection carried out by a shop approved for the type is strongly recommended before any firm offer, in particular to check the transmission, rotor head and the complete engine file, three costly items if the potential is close to its limit.

Maintenance and the Arriel engine

Like any turbine helicopter, the AS350 requires a sustained periodic maintenance schedule, with life-limited components (blades, rotor head, certain transmission parts) that must be replaced after a defined number of hours or calendar time, regardless of apparent condition.

The Arriel engine overhaul is the heaviest maintenance item to plan for: its cost and due date should be clearly documented in the aircraft's technical file before any purchase, since an example close to that threshold will logically be priced with a discount reflecting the upcoming overhaul cost. It is also worth checking the availability of an approved maintenance shop near the intended base of operation, an important factor for an aircraft flown frequently.

Tip — Always ask for the airframe, engine and transmission logbooks as well as the last three maintenance invoices before making an offer: they give an accurate picture of how seriously the aircraft has been maintained.

Purchase and operating budget

The price of a used AS350 depends above all on the variant (B, BA, B1, B2 or B3), the age of the airframe, airframe and engine hours, the remaining potential before overhaul, and cabin configuration and equipment. The oldest variants, with reduced engine potential, trade at a noticeably more accessible level than well-equipped recent variants.

Before negotiating, compare several listings for comparable configurations (variant, year, hours, previous use) to gauge the asking price against the real market, rather than relying on a single isolated reference.

Market and resale

The AS350 Ecureuil benefits from an active, international resale market, driven by a particularly large installed fleet and good parts availability, even for the oldest variants. Resale is generally easier with a complete and transparent maintenance record, a clearly documented operating history, and life-limited components either recently replaced or with a comfortable margin before their due date.

Comparing several listings of the same variant remains the best way to assess a fair price, whether buying now or preparing a future resale. Price differences between two aircraft of the same model year are almost always explained by remaining engine and transmission potential and by the quality of the documented maintenance.
