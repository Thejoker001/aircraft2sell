Why the Nimbus remains an Open Class benchmark

The Schempp-Hirth Nimbus is a family of Open Class gliders designed by the German manufacturer Schempp-Hirth Flugzeugbau, known for its high aerodynamic efficiency and generous wingspan. Unlike Standard or Club Class gliders, the Nimbus was designed for long cross-country flying and pure performance, with a long, slender wing that lets it exploit weak thermals efficiently over long distances.

Since it first appeared, this focus has made the Nimbus a favourite among ambitious pilots targeting long cross-country flights, records and Open Class competition, as well as pilots simply looking for a glider able to cover long distances with minimal energy loss. Its staying power on the market, through several generations, speaks to the lasting relevance of its design.

On the used market, the Nimbus comes in several successive versions (Nimbus 2, Nimbus 3 and Nimbus 4) as well as two-seat and motorised variants, giving buyers a fairly wide range of choice depending on budget and intended use. This diversity also explains a broad price range, from older, more accessible examples to recent, very well-equipped versions.

The Nimbus's reputation also rests on the quality of its composite construction and a European network of pilots and mechanics familiar with the type, which makes maintenance, experience-sharing and, ultimately, resale of a well-maintained example easier.

Nimbus 2, Nimbus 3 and Nimbus 4: what's different

The Nimbus 2 is the first generation in the line, a single-seat, long-wingspan glider that laid the groundwork for the manufacturer's Open Class philosophy. It is the oldest version available on the used market, generally with a more accessible entry price, but buyers also need to factor in the more advanced age of these airframes. A motorised variant (Nimbus 2M), fitted with a retractable sustainer engine, also exists and stands out for its extended range should thermals fail.

The Nimbus 3 introduced a further-refined wing, with span extensions allowing the wing area to be adapted to flying conditions, as well as a two-seat version (Nimbus 3D) built for dual-control long-distance flying. It is a highly sought-after version on the European used market, particularly the two-seat examples, which let two crews share performance flying.

The Nimbus 4 represents the latest evolution of the line, with a further-optimised aerodynamic profile and, again, single-seat and two-seat (Nimbus 4D) variants, plus a motorised two-seat version (Nimbus 4DM) fitted with a sustainer engine. Nimbus 4 examples on the resale market are generally newer and are priced accordingly higher than earlier generations.

Before targeting a specific version, it is worth clearly defining the intended use — solo cross-country flying, dual-control flying, or the need for a sustainer engine to secure long flights — since the gap in budget and technical complexity between motorised and non-motorised versions, or between single and two-seaters, is significant.

What budget should you plan for

The price of a used Nimbus depends mainly on the version (2, 3 or 4), whether it is a single-seater or two-seater, whether it is fitted with a sustainer engine, the age and total airframe hours, and the condition of the composite gelcoat and onboard instruments. The oldest first-generation examples trade at a noticeably more accessible entry level than newer, well-equipped, low-hours Nimbus 4D or 4DM examples.

Beyond the purchase price, your budget should also cover pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic qualified on type, registration and transfer fees, insurance, a road trailer sized for the aircraft's long wingspan, and a hangar spot, often needed given the size of the airframe and its wing extensions.

What to check before buying

As with any used glider, a pre-purchase inspection carried out by an independent mechanic qualified on type is essential before closing a deal. For a Nimbus, a few points deserve particular attention given its long wingspan and specific design.

  • Composite gelcoat condition across the whole wing, particularly on the removable span extensions, which are often handled during rigging and derigging on the field.
  • Total airframe hours and periodic inspection history, with a complete, chronologically consistent logbook.
  • Operation of the water ballast system, common on this type of performance glider, and absence of leaks from the wing tanks.
  • On motorised versions (M, 2M, 4DM), condition and operation of the retractable sustainer engine and its deployment mechanism, a sensitive technical point that calls for a thorough check by a specialist on type.
  • Condition of the retractable landing gear and its locking mechanism, as well as play in the flight controls on a long, slender airframe.
  • Presence and condition of the road trailer, sized for the aircraft's long wingspan, and compatibility with wing extensions where applicable.
Good to know — Never rely solely on the seller's description of overall condition: a written independent inspection report, ideally from a workshop approved for the type and familiar with Open Class gliders, remains the best protection against unpleasant surprises after purchase, especially on versions fitted with a sustainer engine.

Maintenance and running costs

The running cost of a Nimbus is broadly comparable to that of other performance gliders, except that its long wingspan and, where applicable, its sustainer engine involve extra maintenance items compared with a Standard Class glider. The main cost items remain the periodic airworthiness inspection, insurance, storage (a hangar is usually recommended given the wingspan) and routine maintenance of the gelcoat and flight controls.

On motorised versions, maintaining the sustainer engine and its system (starter, fuel tank, deployment mechanism) is an extra cost and attention item that justifies using a workshop approved for and familiar with this type of installation. Rigorous, well-documented maintenance is a strong selling point at any future resale, all the more so for an aircraft fitted with a sustainer engine.

Insurance for an Open Class glider is generally a little higher than for a Standard Class glider of comparable value, owing to the often higher value of the aircraft and its technical complexity, particularly on motorised or two-seat versions.

Tip — Always ask for the complete logbook and the latest periodic inspection reports before making an offer, and on a motorised version, specifically check the sustainer engine's maintenance history: it gives a faithful picture of how seriously the whole aircraft has been maintained.

Resale and the used market

The Nimbus benefits from an active used market in Europe among pilots looking for cross-country performance beyond what Standard Class gliders offer, even though supply naturally remains more limited than for more common models. A well-maintained aircraft, with a transparent history, gelcoat in good condition and, on motorised versions, a working, well-documented sustainer engine, generally resells more easily and on better terms.

Comparing several listings for similar examples remains the best way to judge a fair price, whether you're buying now or preparing a future resale. Price differences between two examples of the same version almost always come down to airframe hours, gelcoat condition, whether it is a single or two-seater, the presence of a sustainer engine and documented maintenance quality.
