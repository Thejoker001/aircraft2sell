Reading Airworthiness Directives (AD) before buying

What an Airworthiness Directive is, where to find it, and how to check it has actually been complied with on the aircraft you are considering: the guide to avoiding unpleasant surprises.

What an Airworthiness Directive (AD) is

An Airworthiness Directive (AD) is a binding document issued by a certification authority (EASA in Europe, the FAA in the United States for US-designed aircraft) when a safety defect is identified on a given aircraft type, engine, propeller or specific piece of equipment. It mandates a corrective action — inspection, modification, parts replacement or an operating limitation — within a given deadline or at a given recurring interval.

Unlike a manufacturer's service bulletin, which is often optional, an AD is a regulatory obligation. An aircraft that does not comply with an applicable AD is simply not in an airworthy condition, even if it looks perfectly fine on visual inspection.

ADs can cover any part of the aircraft: the airframe structure (cracks, corrosion in specific identified areas), the powerplant (a defect in an internal engine part, ageing of a propeller component), or specific equipment such as landing gear, a fuel tank or an electrical system. Some ADs apply to an entire aircraft type, others only to a specific range of serial numbers or a particular equipment configuration.

Where to find the ADs applicable to an aircraft

For an EASA-certified aircraft, the list of ADs applicable to a given type is published on the EASA website and can be freely searched by type certificate. For a US-designed aircraft (Cessna, Piper, Beechcraft, Cirrus...), it is also necessary to check the ADs issued by the FAA, which may be made applicable in Europe through a mirror EASA AD or recognised directly under the bilateral agreements in force.

  • EASA database — search by type certificate (airframe, engine, propeller) in the official Airworthiness Directive register.
  • FAA database — useful as a complement for US-designed aircraft, especially for the most recent ADs.
  • Aircraft maintenance logbook — must list the ADs applied, with the date and reference of each intervention.
  • Manufacturer service bulletins — to be cross-checked with ADs, as some directives explicitly reference a specific service bulletin.
Tip — Always ask the seller for the complete list of ADs applicable to the aircraft's type, with the individual compliance status of each, even before travelling to view it.

How to read and understand an AD

An AD generally contains several key elements you need to be able to identify: the reference number, the scope of applicability (which serial numbers or configurations are affected), the nature of the defect found, the required corrective action and the compliance deadline, expressed in flight hours, cycles or calendar time depending on the case.

What to look for first

  • The exact scope of applicability — the same model may have several variants, and not all of them are necessarily affected by a given AD.
  • Whether the action is one-off or recurring — some ADs require a single inspection, others a repetitive inspection at every overhaul.
  • The compliance deadline — once past, the aircraft is no longer airworthy until the action has been carried out.
  • Any approved alternative methods of compliance (AMOC) that may exist for a given directive.
Point of caution — A recurring AD must appear at every overhaul in the maintenance logbook. Its absence over several years is a serious warning sign about how well the aircraft has actually been tracked.

Checking compliance before buying

Before signing anything, ask the seller or their mechanic for the exhaustive list of ADs applicable to the aircraft's type, with proof of compliance for each one: the date, engine or airframe hours at the time of the intervention, the part reference if a part was replaced, and the signature of the approved mechanic who carried out the work. This check is an integral part of the pre-purchase inspection and should be entrusted to a mechanic independent of the seller, just like the rest of the technical file.

For an aircraft that has been imported or has changed country of registration several times, be especially careful: ADs applicable in the country of origin (FAA, for example) sometimes need to be cross-checked against EASA ADs to make sure no obligation was missed during successive transfers.

Common pitfalls related to ADs

The most common mistake is to rely solely on the phrase "ADs up to date" in a listing without asking for documented proof. An incomplete maintenance logbook or an unexplained gap in the history is often a sign that an AD may have been missed at some point, without this being obvious during a simple visual viewing.

Another frequent pitfall is overlooking recurring ADs whose deadline falls shortly after the purchase. The cost of the next inspection or parts replacement required by a recurring AD should be factored into your acquisition budget, just like registration or insurance costs.

Tip — Have the mechanic carrying out the pre-purchase inspection produce a written, dated list of all ADs applicable to the type, with their individual status verified against the physical parts. This document should remain attached to the aircraft's technical file after the sale.

Impact of a recurring AD on value and future maintenance

The presence of costly recurring ADs on a given aircraft type can significantly affect its resale value and overall cost of ownership over time. Two aircraft of the same model, outwardly similar, can have very different ownership costs depending on whether one of the applicable ADs requires a costly part to be replaced periodically.

Before comparing several listings of the same model, it is therefore worth knowing the main recurring ADs known for that aircraft type and engine, in order to assess their impact on the maintenance budget for the coming years, in addition to the listed purchase price.

This check is particularly useful when comparing aircraft of the same model but different build years: an older serial number may be affected by more historical ADs, while a more recent version of the same model may be exempt thanks to a modification made at the factory. Always asking for the exact serial number before checking the applicable ADs avoids any unpleasant surprise during final negotiations.

Frequently asked questions

Is an AD always mandatory?

Yes, whenever it applies to the exact configuration of the aircraft in question. It is never an optional recommendation, unlike a manufacturer's service bulletin, which may remain optional depending on its classification.

What happens if an AD has not been complied with?

The aircraft is not airworthy until the corrective action has been carried out by an approved workshop. This should be negotiated with the seller before the sale, either through compliance at the seller's expense or through a price reduction equal to the cost of the work.

Are ADs the same everywhere in Europe?

ADs issued by EASA apply in a harmonised way across all member states for aircraft within its scope. For an aircraft of non-European design, it is necessary to check both the ADs of the authority of origin and their possible transposition into an EASA AD.

Key takeaway — Airworthiness directives are never a mere administrative formality: reading them carefully before any purchase is one of the best indicators of how seriously an aircraft has actually been maintained.
