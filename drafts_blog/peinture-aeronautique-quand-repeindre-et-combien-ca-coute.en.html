Aircraft Paint: When to Repaint and What It Costs

Signs it's time for a new paint job, what a proper preparation involves, and a realistic budget for a microlight, a single-engine or a light twin.

Why paint is more than cosmetic

On an aircraft, paint is not just about looks: it protects the airframe against corrosion, particularly on aluminium structures exposed to humidity and temperature swings. Cracked or peeling paint lets moisture work its way under the finish, which can trigger corrosion that is invisible at first glance but expensive to treat once it has taken hold.

Beyond protection, paint condition is also one of the first visual cues a prospective buyer uses to judge how well an aircraft has been looked after, well before they ever open the maintenance logbooks. Neglected paint can raise doubts about the overall standard of maintenance, even if the airframe and engine are in excellent condition.

Signs it's time to repaint

A quality aircraft paint job lasts on average 10 to 15 years, depending on storage conditions (enclosed hangar versus outside tie-down) and local climate. Several visual signs indicate that a full repaint, rather than a simple touch-up, is becoming necessary.

  • Chalking — the surface turns dull and leaves a whitish powder when touched, a sign that the protective clear coat has degraded under UV exposure.
  • Cracking and flaking — spider-web cracks or pieces of paint lifting off, especially around rivets and panel edges.
  • Visible corrosion under the paint — blistering or white staining (aluminium corrosion) that locally deforms the painted surface.
  • Significant fading — colours that have clearly shifted from the original shades, particularly reds and bright tones that are most UV-sensitive.
  • A patchwork of visible local touch-ups — repainted areas from different periods that eventually become more costly to even out than a full repaint.
Tip — A close visual inspection every two to three years allows you to schedule a repaint before corrosion sets in under the finish, avoiding far more expensive airframe work later.

The steps of an aircraft paint job

A proper aircraft paint job follows a multi-step process, each step critical to the durability of the final result.

Stripping and preparation

The old paint is removed by chemical stripping or controlled mechanical sanding down to bare metal. This step also reveals any hidden corrosion under the previous coats, which must be treated before any new application.

Corrosion treatment and primer

Once the airframe is clean, a chemical conversion treatment followed by an anti-corrosion primer coat (usually epoxy) is applied to protect the metal long-term and ensure proper adhesion of the following layers.

Masking and topcoat

Areas that must not be painted (windows, antennas, temporary regulatory markings) are carefully masked. The topcoat (polyurethane in the vast majority of cases) is then applied in several passes, followed where needed by stripes, livery and registration marks.

Finishing and quality control

A final check verifies paint film thickness (added paint weight remains a genuine concern on small aircraft), colour uniformity and the absence of defects before the aircraft is returned to flying configuration.

What a full repaint costs

The budget for a full repaint depends above all on the size of the aircraft, the number of colours and the complexity of the livery (simple stripes versus elaborate gradients and patterns). The figures below are indicative for a full repaint including stripping, labour and materials.

Aircraft typeIndicative budget (stripping + full repaint)
Microlight / light two-seater€3,000 to €7,000
Light single-engine (Cessna 172, Piper PA-28)€8,000 to €16,000
High-performance single-engine (Cirrus SR22, Diamond DA40)€12,000 to €22,000
Light twin-engine€18,000 to €35,000
Single-engine turboprop€25,000 to €45,000
Tip — A simple localised touch-up (without full stripping) costs considerably less but never fixes underlying corrosion: always ask for a proper diagnosis before choosing between a touch-up and a full repaint.

How long the aircraft is grounded

A full repaint typically grounds the aircraft for one to three weeks, depending on its size, the complexity of the livery, and the drying time needed between coats, which cannot be shortened without compromising the quality of the result. It is advisable to schedule this work alongside a major overhaul or engine work already planned, to make the most of the downtime on the ground.

Choosing a paint shop

Not all aircraft paint shops are equal: the quality of preparation and adherence to procedures (sanding, corrosion treatment, film thickness) directly determine how long the result will last. Favour a shop with Part-145 or Part-CAO approval that has a dedicated paint booth, and always ask for references on aircraft similar to yours.

  • Valid maintenance approval for airframe work.
  • A temperature- and dust-controlled paint booth, essential for a durable finish.
  • A detailed quote specifying the products used (brand and reference of primer and topcoat).
  • Verifiable references on aircraft of comparable size and complexity.

Impact on resale value

Paint in good condition generally does not increase resale value beyond its own cost, but it noticeably shortens time to sale and makes negotiation easier by reassuring the buyer about overall maintenance. Conversely, badly degraded paint can cost proportionally more than the price of a repaint, since it fuels doubt about the true condition of the airframe underneath.

Tip — If you plan to sell within the next two to three years, paint in decent condition (not necessarily brand new) is usually sufficient: there's no need to invest in a full repaint right before listing, unless the finish is badly degraded.
