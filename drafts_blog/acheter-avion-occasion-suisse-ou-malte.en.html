Buying a used aircraft in Switzerland or Malta: which country to choose

Market, prices, OFAC or AAM registration, taxation and operating costs: a full comparison to help you choose between Switzerland and Malta for your used aircraft purchase.

Two markets, two very different philosophies

Switzerland has a mature and active general aviation market, backed by a dense network of regional airfields and high purchasing power. The used aircraft inventory is varied, ranging from two-seat leisure aircraft to twin-engine touring aircraft, and the overall maintenance standard is widely regarded as particularly meticulous, which tends to reassure foreign buyers. Switzerland is not an EU member, but it participates in the European aviation safety framework through bilateral agreements with EASA, which simplifies the mutual recognition of licences and certificates for most private purposes.

Malta, by contrast, is a true niche market: the country's small size and limited number of civil airfields sharply restrict the local supply of touring aircraft. Malta has instead built a reputation over recent years as a sought-after registration jurisdiction for high-value aircraft — particularly business jets — operated under professional or semi-commercial arrangements, thanks to a registry known for its responsiveness and a regulatory framework aligned with EU rules, since Malta is a member of both the European Union and EASA.

Prices compared by aircraft type

In Switzerland, used aircraft prices generally run close to, or slightly above, the average seen across continental Europe for a comparable aircraft in equivalent condition and avionics fit, partly reflecting a higher cost of living and a reputation for immaculate maintenance. In Malta, the thin volume of touring aircraft listings makes direct comparison harder: the few available aircraft are priced more according to local scarcity than any genuine market dynamic.

Registration: OFAC vs AAM

In Switzerland, aircraft registration is handled by the Federal Office of Civil Aviation (FOCA, known locally as OFAC/BAZL), under the HB- prefix. Since Switzerland is not an EU member, transferring registration from another country involves deregistering from the original register before completing the Swiss registration process — generally well documented, but worth building into the purchase timeline.

In Malta, the Authority for Transport in Malta (AAM register) handles civil aircraft registration, under the 9H- prefix. This registry has built a reputation for speed and flexibility, particularly for commercially operated aircraft or those financed through leasing structures, which explains its appeal for certain business jets. For a simple touring aircraft intended to fly mostly on the continent, however, it offers little practical advantage over a Swiss registration or one in the buyer's home country.

  • Valid certificate of airworthiness (CofA) — verify before signing anything, regardless of the aircraft's registry of origin.
  • Bill of sale — dated, signed by both parties, mentioning the aircraft's serial number.
  • Liability insurance certificate — required before any first flight under the new registration.
Good to know — Bilateral agreements between Switzerland and the EU on aviation safety ease the mutual recognition of licences and certificates, but they don't remove the need to check customs formalities case by case, since Switzerland sits outside the EU's customs territory.

VAT and taxation compared

Switzerland runs its own VAT regime, separate from the EU's, since it is not a member state: a sale between Swiss residents follows national tax rules, while a cross-border purchase can trigger specific customs formalities when the aircraft enters or leaves Swiss territory. Malta, as an EU member, applies the harmonised European VAT framework, following the same general principles as elsewhere in the Union for sales between private individuals or by a professional seller.

Malta has also built a distinct reputation around aircraft ownership structuring, with arrangements that have historically attracted companies operating high-value aircraft. These structures mainly concern professional operators and business jets: they are not relevant to a straightforward private purchase of a light leisure aircraft, whether in Switzerland or Malta.

Climate, bases and operating costs

Switzerland has a dense network of airfields, constrained by alpine terrain: some strips require a specific mountain rating, and weather conditions can vary sharply from one valley to the next. Hangar and maintenance costs tend to be high, in line with the country's cost of living and skilled-labour rates.

Malta enjoys a mild Mediterranean climate well suited to year-round flying, but the very limited number of civil airfields (Malta's airport is the main point of entry) sharply restricts basing options for a light touring aircraft. Parking and routine maintenance costs are also high, reflecting the island's insularity and a thinner network of approved maintenance shops for light general aviation than on the mainland.

Pre-purchase inspection and paperwork

Regardless of the aircraft's country of origin, a pre-purchase inspection carried out by a licensed mechanic independent of the seller remains the best protection against unpleasant surprises. It should cover airframe condition, the consistency of the engine and airframe logbooks, engine hours and the remaining time before the next major overhaul (SMOH), as well as avionics compliance with current European requirements.

  • Up-to-date airframe, engine and propeller logbooks, with no chronological gaps, regardless of the register of origin.
  • Three years of maintenance invoices, translated if drafted in an uncommon language.
  • For a Malta-based aircraft: confirm the real availability of an approved local workshop for future servicing — a point some island-based buyers underestimate.
  • For a Switzerland-based aircraft: check whether mountain ratings were required for its usual operations and whether this affected the wear of certain equipment.

Which country to choose based on your profile

For a private buyer looking for a touring aircraft for leisure use across continental Europe, Switzerland offers a richer market, aircraft that are often immaculately maintained, and a regulatory framework well understood thanks to its agreements with EASA — at the cost, however, of generally higher purchase prices and operating costs than the European average.

Malta mainly suits specific profiles: owners or companies operating a high-value aircraft, particularly a business jet, looking for a responsive registry within a recognised EU regulatory framework, or buyers specifically seeking a Mediterranean base. In both cases, a specialist aviation and tax advisor is recommended before any registration project, whether in Switzerland or Malta.
