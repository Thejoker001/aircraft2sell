Buying a Used Aircraft in Malta or Cyprus: Which Country to Choose

Registration, taxation, operating costs and market depth: a comparison between Malta and Cyprus to help you choose the right country to buy and register your used aircraft.

Malta and Cyprus: two Mediterranean aviation hubs

Malta and Cyprus share several advantages that have, each in its own way, made them recognised locations for aircraft registration and operation in the Mediterranean: European Union membership, active civil aviation regulators experienced with international files, and a network of aircraft management companies, specialist law firms and brokers accustomed to cross-border transactions. For a used-aircraft buyer considering registering their aircraft in one of these two countries, the choice depends less on any fundamental difference in the shared European regulatory framework than on practical nuances: speed of the process, the locally available ecosystem of service providers, and the intended tax structuring.

Both countries are European Union member states and therefore apply the common EASA framework for airworthiness, which greatly simplifies the comparison relative to a non-EU third country. The differences worth studying lie more on the administrative, tax and support-service-availability side.

The used-aircraft market in both countries

Neither Malta nor Cyprus has a domestic used light-aircraft market comparable to that of larger continental countries such as France or Germany: the small size of the territory and the limited number of airfields explain a more restricted local supply of listings. However, both countries are sought-after registration locations for aircraft purchased elsewhere in Europe or imported, particularly for business jets and turboprops operated by local management companies.

In practice, a buyer rarely targets an aircraft physically based in Malta or Cyprus: they buy an aircraft wherever it may be located in Europe, then choose their country of registration based on the administrative and tax criteria detailed below. This is especially common for business jets and turboprops, where the ownership structure often matters as much as the aircraft's physical location.

Registration: comparing the procedures

In Malta, aircraft registration falls under Transport Malta – Civil Aviation Directorate, under the 9H- prefix. The Maltese register has developed particularly strongly for business jets and aircraft held through corporate structures, with well-established procedures for international files and routine support from specialist firms based on the island.

In Cyprus, registration falls under the Cypriot Department of Civil Aviation, under the 5B- prefix. The Cypriot register is likewise used for private and business aircraft held through international structures, with an administrative framework broadly comparable to Malta's.

  • Certificate of airworthiness and technical file — have these checked beforehand; documentation requirements are similar in both countries due to the shared EASA framework.
  • Ownership structure — local company, trust or direct ownership: the structure chosen influences the choice of register and should be studied with specialist advice before deciding.
  • Deregistration from the register of origin — essential before any new registration for an aircraft already registered elsewhere, regardless of the destination country.
Good to know — Both registers routinely serve an international clientele and apply the common EASA rules for airworthiness. The choice between the two mainly depends on the intended legal ownership structure and the availability of specialist advisers for the aircraft type in question: it is advisable to request a precise timeline quote from both administrations rather than relying on a generic figure.

VAT and taxation at purchase

Malta and Cyprus both apply the European Union's common VAT regime to aviation transactions, with rules that depend on the buyer's status (private individual or company), the aircraft's intended use and the place of delivery. Both countries have developed recognised expertise in structuring the taxation of business aviation transactions, which partly explains their appeal to this type of client.

SituationTax treatment
Sale between EU resident private individualsGenerally VAT-exempt
Sale by a professional or through a corporate structureVAT potentially applicable, depending on the structure used
Import from outside the EUImport VAT, to be confirmed case by case with a tax adviser

The exact calculation rules and any special regimes change regularly and depend heavily on the structure chosen: it is essential to have your tax situation confirmed by an adviser specialised in aviation taxation in the relevant country before any acquisition, rather than relying on generalities.

Operating costs and operating structures

In both countries, operating a private or business aircraft frequently goes through a local aircraft management company, which handles regulatory compliance, maintenance, crewing and, where applicable, charter marketing of the aircraft. This ecosystem of service providers is a selection criterion as important as taxation or registration itself: the availability of managers experienced on the intended aircraft type can tip the decision one way or the other.

Parking, insurance and crew costs remain broadly aligned with Mediterranean standards and depend mainly on the chosen base airfield rather than the country of registration itself, since the aircraft is not necessarily based in its country of registration.

Pre-purchase inspection and documents to check

Whichever country of registration is ultimately chosen, a pre-purchase inspection carried out by a mechanic or shop independent of the seller and qualified on the type remains the best protection before finalising a transaction. It covers the condition of the airframe, the consistency of the engine logbook, complete maintenance history and compliance of the avionics with current European requirements.

  • Up-to-date airframe, engine and propeller logbooks (or engine logbook for a jet), with no chronological gaps.
  • History of maintenance invoices for recent years, particularly regarding remaining engine status.
  • Consistency between the intended ownership structure and the chosen country of registration, confirmed by legal advice before signing.
Point of caution — Never finalise a purchase without having seen the original logbooks and had their consistency checked by an independent professional, and without having confirmed in writing the actual registration timeline applicable to your file with the chosen administration.

How to choose between Malta and Cyprus

In practice, the choice between Malta and Cyprus for registering a used aircraft rarely comes down to a single criterion: it results from a trade-off between the intended ownership structure, the availability of an ecosystem of advisers (tax, legal, aircraft management) experienced with the type of aircraft in question, and sometimes existing business relationships already established by the buyer or their adviser in one country or the other.

For a buyer with no prior preference, the safest approach is to request advice in parallel from a specialist in each of the two countries, compare timeline and cost quotes for your specific file, and verify the availability of managers experienced on the intended aircraft type before making a final decision.
