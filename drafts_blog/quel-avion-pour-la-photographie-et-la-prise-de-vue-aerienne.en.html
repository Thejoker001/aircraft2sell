Which aircraft for photography and aerial footage?

Visibility, slow cruise speed, high wing or low wing: how to choose the right aircraft for photography and aerial footage, and when to prefer a microlight or a helicopter instead.

Why the aircraft matters as much as the camera gear

In aerial photography and filming, the camera is only part of the equation. The aircraft itself determines the available viewing angle, the stability of the shooting platform, the speed at which the subject moves through the frame, and how easily the photographer or camera operator can open a window, remove a door, or fit a camera mount. A poorly suited aircraft can make certain shots impossible, no matter how good the optical equipment is.

The right choice also depends heavily on the subject being photographed: a slow, stable real-estate or agricultural survey has very different requirements from a fast sightseeing flight or a mapping mission that needs a precise, repeatable straight-line flight path.

The technical criteria to prioritise

A few criteria consistently matter when choosing an aircraft for aerial photography: low stall and cruise speeds, so you can slow down safely and give yourself more time to frame the shot; good peripheral visibility from the cabin; the ability to open a window, fit an optical glass port, or, on some aircraft, safely remove a door; and a platform stable enough to limit vibration transmitted to the camera equipment.

  • Low stall and cruise speed to allow multiple slow passes without excessive risk.
  • Clear downward and lateral visibility, with no bulky structure obstructing the field of view.
  • Openable window, optical glass port or removable door depending on the shot required.
  • Stability in slow flight and in turns, to limit vibration and keep a steady, continuous frame.
  • Enough range to cover a wide area without multiple refuelling stops.

High wing or low wing: the impact on framing

Wing position radically changes what the photographer can see and shoot. A high-wing aircraft leaves a clear downward view from the cabin, which has historically made it the preferred choice for vertical aerial photography and ground surveys: the subject stays visible without the wing cutting into the frame. This is part of the appeal of aircraft like the Piper PA-28 Cherokee for certain uses, although that particular model is actually low-wing — in practice, the choice depends more on the angle you are after, oblique or lateral, than on a strictly vertical view.

Conversely, a low-wing aircraft offers a better upward and side view during a turn, which can suit oblique shots or flights where the subject is viewed from a banked flight path rather than straight down. Before choosing a model, it is worth precisely defining the kind of shot you need: a vertical top-down view, an oblique shot during a tight turn, or a simple panoramic flyover.

Classic single-engines: Cessna, Piper and equivalents

Light high-wing single-engines, led by the Cessna 172, remain a reference for general-purpose aerial photography: wide availability on the used market, parts and mechanics easy to find, a moderate cruise speed, and clear downward visibility thanks to the high wing position. This type of aircraft suits both occasional rental use and purchase for a regular professional aerial photography activity.

Low-wing single-engines, such as the Piper PA-28 Cherokee mentioned above, have their place for missions where lateral visibility or cruise comfort matters more than a straight-down view, or when the photographer works mainly from a side window rather than looking down through the aircraft.

Microlights and trikes: lightness and low-level flying

For very low-altitude shots over short distances and on a reduced operating budget, microlights — and trikes in particular, such as the weight-shift microlight — offer unmatched freedom and visibility: no enclosed cabin on many models, very low speed, and great manoeuvrability for tracking a moving subject on the ground. This makes them particularly well suited to photographing rural landscapes, local events or sites that are hard to reach for a faster aircraft.

The trade-off is more limited range, greater sensitivity to wind, and conditions less suited to carrying heavy or fragile camera equipment over long sessions. Microlights therefore suit short, targeted missions better than extended coverage over several hours.

Aircraft, helicopter or drone: when to prefer each

An aircraft remains the most economical option per flight hour for covering large distances at a constant speed — mapping, regional surveys, monitoring large construction sites. A helicopter, by contrast, can hover and fly flight paths no aircraft can match, which makes it essential for certain precise, close-up shots or ones requiring a sustained hold in position, at a significantly higher operating cost.

Drones, finally, have become the go-to choice for low- and medium-altitude shots over a limited area, with a much lower cost and great operational flexibility, but they remain subject to height, distance and endurance limits that make them unsuitable for large-scale missions or flights over restricted areas requiring a specific authorisation. The choice between these three options therefore depends above all on the area to cover, the precision of the shot required, and the available budget.

Cabin layout and photo equipment

Beyond the aircraft model itself, the cabin layout strongly affects the quality of the footage. A stabilised camera mount fixed to the structure, a dedicated opening in the floor or door on some aircraft specifically fitted out for aerial photography, and a secure harness for a photographer working with the door open, are all elements worth checking before choosing an aircraft for regular use.

Good to know — Any modification to the cabin (removable door, floor opening, camera mount fixed to the structure) must be documented and approved in the aircraft's airworthiness file before being used in flight, whether for personal or professional use.

For occasional use, a simple openable window or a good-quality optical glass port is usually enough; for a regular professional activity, investing in an already fitted-out aircraft or financing a dedicated conversion remains the most cost-effective solution over time.

Budget, licence and regulations to know

From a regulatory standpoint, aerial photography from an aircraft generally remains compatible with a private pilot licence for unpaid use, but moves into the field of aerial work as soon as it is billed to a client, bringing the corresponding obligations in terms of professional licence, insurance, and sometimes an operating certificate depending on the regulation applicable in the relevant country. It is essential to check the precise, up-to-date rules with the competent civil aviation authority before starting a commercial activity, rather than relying on an unverified figure or rule.

On the budget side, operating cost per flight hour remains one of the deciding factors: a light single-engine rented or amortised over several missions stays considerably more affordable than a helicopter, which is why the aircraft remains the go-to solution for most aerial photography and filming projects on a controlled budget.

Word of caution — Before buying an aircraft dedicated to aerial photography, have a full pre-purchase inspection carried out, especially if a door has already been modified or removed by a previous owner: this modification must be checked and documented just like the airframe and engine.
