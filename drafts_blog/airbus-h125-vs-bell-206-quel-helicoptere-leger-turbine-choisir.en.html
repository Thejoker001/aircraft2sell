Airbus H125 vs Bell 206: Which Light Turbine Helicopter to Choose

Two philosophies of the single-turbine light helicopter

The Airbus H125 (formerly the Eurocopter AS350 Ecureuil / AStar) and the Bell 206 JetRanger are among the most widespread single-turbine light helicopters in the world, each having built its reputation over decades of intensive use across a wide range of missions: tourism, aerial work, training, VIP transport, rescue or surveillance operations. Yet these two aircraft reflect two different generations and two different design philosophies.

The Bell 206, designed in the 1960s and produced for decades, became a near-universal benchmark among light helicopters, known for its mechanical simplicity and controlled operating cost. The more recent H125 was designed to deliver greater power and payload capacity, particularly in hot-and-high conditions, which made it the go-to choice for mountain missions and demanding operations. Choosing between the two therefore starts with clearly defining the intended mission and the operating budget you're prepared to commit to over time.

The Airbus H125: power and mountain versatility

The H125 has established itself as the benchmark light helicopter for high-altitude and hot-weather operations, thanks to its Turbomeca Arriel engine offering a comfortable power margin even at maximum weight. This capability has made it a particularly valued aircraft for mountain rescue missions, high-altitude aerial work (longline operations, firefighting) and operations requiring a solid power reserve for takeoff and landing on unprepared sites.

On the used market, the H125 and its predecessor the AS350 Ecureuil also stand out for a cabin more spacious than the Bell 206's, offering better passenger and cargo payload capacity, along with often more modern avionics on later-built examples. This versatility and available power, however, translate into a purchase budget and an hourly operating cost generally higher than the Bell 206's.

The Bell 206: the historic, economical benchmark

The Bell 206 JetRanger (and its stretched LongRanger variant) remains one of the most-produced and most widespread light helicopters in the world, which translates into a very broad used market, extensive parts availability, and a particularly well-developed network of mechanics familiar with the type, including across Europe. Its simple, proven mechanics, paired with a Rolls-Royce (Allison) engine renowned for its reliability, have historically made it the preferred choice for training, tourism, aerial surveillance and many light aerial-work missions.

Compared with the H125, the Bell 206 offers more limited available power, particularly in hot or high-altitude conditions, which restricts its payload capacity in those settings. In exchange, its acquisition cost on the used market is generally more accessible, and its hourly operating cost remains among the most competitive in its category, which explains its lasting popularity among operators working with a controlled budget.

Budget comparison: acquisition and operation

The choice between the two aircraft largely depends on the balance sought between operational capability and running cost. The H125 represents a larger investment at purchase, but its available power and superior payload capacity can justify that extra cost for certain demanding missions. The Bell 206, older and produced in far greater numbers, generally trades at a more accessible acquisition price, with parts and labour costs often more competitive thanks to the sheer size of the global operating fleet.

Beyond the purchase price, you should always factor in the real cost per flight hour, which depends on the airframe's age, the remaining time before the next engine and transmission overhaul, and the intended intensity of use.

Which aircraft for which mission

For an operator whose missions take place mainly over flat terrain or at low altitude, with moderate payload requirements, the Bell 206 remains a relevant, economical and proven choice. For mountain operations, hot-weather conditions, or missions requiring greater payload capacity, the H125 brings an operational safety margin and performance that justify its higher cost.

  • Bell 206: ideal for training, light aerial tourism, surveillance and missions with a tightly controlled operating budget.
  • Airbus H125: relevant for mountain missions, high-altitude or hot-weather operations, and carrying heavier loads.
  • Local availability of mechanics and parts: a criterion to check based on your intended operating region.
  • Frequency and type of planned missions: decisive factors in assessing the real long-term operating cost.

In both cases, the choice should also factor in the local availability and cost of type-specific training and currency maintenance, which can vary considerably by region and operator network.

Buying either one used: points to watch

Whether it's an H125/AS350 or a Bell 206, buying used calls for particular vigilance regarding the history of the airframe, engine and main transmission, as well as compliance with the manufacturer's scheduled overhauls. On both the Bell 206 and the H125, always check the hours remaining before the next engine and transmission overhaul, which represent the most significant expense items over the ownership period.

Good to know — Have the pre-purchase inspection carried out by an independent mechanic familiar with the type, and ask for the complete flight and maintenance logbooks, including the engine and transmission overhaul history.

As with fixed-wing aircraft, comparing several listings of similar models and model years remains the best way to assess a fair price and spot a well-maintained aircraft, whether it's a Bell 206 or an Airbus H125.
