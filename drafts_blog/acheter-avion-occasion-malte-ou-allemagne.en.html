Buying a used aircraft in Malta or Germany: which country to choose

Market, prices, registration, VAT and tax: compare the two countries to find the one that fits your buying project best.

Two very different markets

Germany is one of the most active general aviation markets in Europe, supported by a dense network of airfields and a strong general aviation tradition: numerous flying clubs, widespread microlights, and a large fleet of touring single-engine aircraft such as the Cessna 172, Piper PA-28 and Diamond DA40. Used supply is abundant, which makes it easy to compare several similar aircraft before deciding.

Malta, by contrast, is a small market: the island has few airfields and a light aviation fleet that is modest relative to its population. Malta's appeal doesn't lie in the volume of listings available locally, but in its tax regime and its attractiveness as a registry for certain owner profiles — particularly those who already operate an aircraft based elsewhere in Europe but wish to register it under the Maltese flag.

Average prices by aircraft type

In Germany, price ranges remain broadly aligned with those seen across the rest of the European Union for comparable aircraft, sometimes with a premium for well-equipped aircraft with modern avionics. In Malta, the low volume of local listings makes comparisons harder: buyers interested in Malta often purchase an aircraft located in another European country and then register it under the Maltese flag, which partly decouples the purchase price from the final registration country.

Registration: TM vs LBA

In Germany, any aircraft based and operated in the country must be registered with the Luftfahrt-Bundesamt (LBA), under the D- prefix. For a sale between individuals already registered in Germany, the transfer is a simple update of the owner on record. For an imported aircraft, deregistration from the original registry is required beforehand.

In Malta, the registry is kept by the Malta Civil Aviation Authority, under the 9H- prefix. The Maltese registry built its reputation in business aviation for its flexibility and regulatory attractiveness, a positioning that extends to light aviation as well: the process is generally fast for a complete file, and the registry accepts operators and owners based in other EU countries.

  • Valid CofA and ARC — essential in both countries before signing anything.
  • Prior deregistration from the original registry — mandatory for any imported aircraft, whether bound for Germany or Malta.
  • Ownership file and bill of sale — comparable documentary requirements in both cases, governed by common EASA rules.
Good to know — EASA rules govern transfers between member states in both cases. Expect roughly 2 to 8 weeks in Germany for a complete file; Maltese timelines vary depending on file complexity and operator type.

VAT and tax: Malta's main appeal

In Germany, a sale between individuals is generally not subject to German VAT (Umsatzsteuer), while a sale by a professional or a company may be, and an import from outside the EU remains subject to import VAT.

Malta has built a particular reputation for aviation taxation, with a framework perceived as favourable by some European operators and owners, notably through specific arrangements applicable under certain conditions to the import and operation of aircraft. This framework remains technical, however, and depends heavily on the ownership structure (individual, company, commercial or private use): it is strongly recommended to have your exact situation validated by a tax advisor specialised in aviation before deciding, rather than relying on generalities found online.

Pre-purchase inspection and documents

Whatever the final registration country, a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic remains the best protection before signing. It covers the condition of the airframe, the consistency of the engine logbook, maintenance history and avionics compliance with current European requirements.

  • Up-to-date airframe, engine and propeller logbooks, with no chronological gaps, regardless of where the aircraft is physically located.
  • Maintenance invoices for the last three years.
  • Cross-checking the country where the aircraft actually sits against the intended registration country — two points that can differ in the Maltese case.
Caution — Never finalise a purchase without having seen the original logbooks: they remain the best indicator of how seriously the aircraft has been maintained, regardless of the registration country chosen.

Practicality: basing, airfields and weather

Operationally, Germany offers a dense network of airfields, typical temperate continental European weather, and close proximity to the rest of the European flying club network — a real advantage for regular leisure or training use.

Malta, for its part, offers a pleasant Mediterranean climate for much of the year but a far more limited airport infrastructure, with a small number of fields available for light aviation. An owner registering an aircraft in Malta while basing it elsewhere in Europe needs to plan for this split between the actual operating location and the registration country, which carries practical implications (local maintenance, shop availability) that shouldn't be overlooked.

Pitfalls to avoid in both countries

In Germany, the most common mistakes mirror those seen across Europe: overlooking deregistration from the previous registry for an imported aircraft, underestimating transfer fees, or signing without getting the exact condition of advertised equipment confirmed in writing.

For Malta, the main pitfall is choosing this registry purely on the basis of assumed tax advantages, without having concretely validated your situation with a specialist advisor or planned for the practical constraints tied to the distance between the actual operating location and the registration country.

Tip — Compare several similar listings on Aircraft2Sell before committing, whatever registration country you ultimately choose: available supply remains the best benchmark for assessing price and general market condition.

Which country to choose based on your profile

Germany naturally suits a buyer looking primarily for an aircraft available quickly, in a deep market that allows comparing many listings, and who wants to base and operate the aircraft in a country with dense aviation infrastructure.

Malta suits a more specific profile: an owner or entity already familiar with aviation tax matters, ready to have their situation validated by a specialist advisor, for whom the registration country doesn't need to match the aircraft's actual operating country. For most straightforward purchases of a light aircraft for regular personal use, Germany remains the simpler, more immediately operational choice.
