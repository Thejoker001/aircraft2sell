The Beagle Pup, a vintage two-seater still loved today

The Beagle Pup is a small British single-engine tricycle-gear aircraft, designed in the late 1960s by Beagle Aircraft as a light training and touring airplane. Despite the manufacturer ceasing production in the early 1970s, a meaningful fleet of Pups has survived across Europe, mainly in the UK but also on the continent, kept airworthy by a dedicated community of owners and type clubs.

Its classic look, typical of British trainers of that era, its reputedly pleasant handling and compact size make it a sought-after used aircraft for pilots looking for something with real character, cheaper to run than most modern single-engine aircraft, but one that requires a different approach to parts sourcing and maintenance than a newer type.

The different Pup variants

The Pup was built in several variants, mainly differing in engine power and seating configuration. The higher-powered versions offer noticeably better takeoff and climb performance, a real benefit for operations from short strips or at higher altitude airfields. Before buying, it is essential to identify exactly which variant is for sale, since this directly affects performance, fuel burn and resale value.

Some aircraft have had approved modifications fitted over the decades (upgrade kits, replacement of certain equipment), which can noticeably change the stated specifications from one aircraft to another, even within the same original variant.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used Beagle Pup depends above all on the overall condition of the airframe, engine hours remaining before the next major overhaul, the quality of the paint and interior, and the specific variant. Being an older aircraft built in limited numbers, pricing is more volatile than for a mass-produced type like the Cessna 172 or Piper PA-28, and two aircraft of the same year can show a significant price gap depending on actual condition.

Beyond the purchase price, budget for a thorough pre-purchase inspection, registration transfer if the aircraft is changing country, insurance, and possibly a paint or interior refresh if the aircraft needs one.

What to check before buying

As with any vintage aircraft, a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic familiar with older British light aircraft is essential before finalising a transaction. For a Pup, a few points deserve particular attention.

  • Corrosion of the metal structure, particularly on the spars and hard-to-reach areas, common on an aircraft this age if poorly stored.
  • Complete engine history: hours elapsed since the last major overhaul, compliance with the maintenance schedule, traceability of replaced parts.
  • Condition of flight controls, cables and linkages, which often show wear on an older airframe.
  • Compliance of modifications fitted over the decades with the corresponding official approvals.
  • Complete logbook and technical file, with no grey areas in the aircraft's history and successive owners.
Good to know — On an aircraft this old, a well-documented history often matters more than general appearance: a plainer aircraft with a clean technical file is usually a better buy than a freshly repainted one with a vague paper trail.

Parts availability and technical support

Since the original manufacturer ceased operations long ago, parts availability is one of the most important points to check before buying a Pup. A community of owners and a handful of workshops specialising in British vintage aircraft can generally source new or used parts, but lead times tend to be longer than for a mass-produced type still supported by its manufacturer.

Before buying, it is worth checking with type clubs and owner associations on the real availability of critical parts for the specific variant you're considering, and on any known constraints (scarce parts, limited approved workshops in certain regions).

Maintenance and operating costs

The operating cost of a Pup remains fairly modest compared with newer aircraft, thanks to a relatively simple and economical engine. The main expense items remain the mandatory annual inspection, insurance, parking and, in the longer term, engine overhaul once the hourly potential is reached, which can be a significant expense on an older aircraft if the needed parts have to be sourced.

Having the aircraft maintained by a shop familiar with British vintage aircraft, or in coordination with type clubs, helps limit unpleasant surprises and preserve its resale value.

Tip — Joining a type club before you even buy often gives valuable insight on a specific aircraft and helps anticipate real maintenance costs.

Logbook and certificate of airworthiness

As with any used aircraft, check that the logbook is complete and consistent with the hours shown, and that the aircraft's certificate of airworthiness (CofA) is current and valid in the country where you plan to operate it. On an aircraft this old, pay particular attention to the continuity of the technical file across successive owners, as some older documents may be incomplete or have been lost over the decades.

If there is any doubt about the validity or continuity of the paperwork, check with the relevant civil aviation authority or a continuing airworthiness management organisation before committing.

Resale and the used market

The used market for the Beagle Pup remains small compared with mass-produced types, but a loyal community of enthusiasts across Europe, particularly in the UK, keeps steady demand for well-maintained examples. An aircraft with a transparent history, complete technical file and consistent maintenance generally resells more easily than one with a poorly documented past.

Comparing several Pup listings, or similar British vintage aircraft, remains the best way to assess a fair price, keeping in mind that the market for this type of aircraft is less liquid and less standardised than that of today's most common light aircraft.
