Escrow and Secure Payment When Selling an Aircraft

The sums involved in selling a used aircraft call for a rigorous payment process. An overview of escrow solutions, their limits, and the precautions to take before funds change hands.

Why secure payment is essential

Selling a used aircraft involves sums often far higher than a typical used-car transaction, and frequently brings together two parties based in different countries, speaking different languages, meeting for the first time through the deal itself. That combination of large amounts, geographic distance and limited trust between strangers makes it a prime target for fraud: a fake seller who disappears after receiving the funds, a forged payment instruction intercepted by email, or a buyer who disputes a payment after taking possession of the aircraft.

A poorly secured payment can cost a buyer the entire purchase price with no realistic recourse, particularly when funds have passed through accounts located in a different jurisdiction from the actual seller. Conversely, a seller can end up handing over the aircraft, or its registration file, before an actual, final payment has cleared. Using a secure payment mechanism, whether a formal escrow arrangement or reinforced banking precautions, is specifically designed to neutralise these risks for both parties.

Escrow: how it works

The principle behind escrow is to route the buyer's funds through an account held by an independent third party, neither the buyer nor the seller, who releases the money to the seller only once agreed conditions have been met: delivery of title documents and the maintenance logbook, clearance after a pre-purchase inspection, effective transfer of registration, or simple confirmation of physical delivery of the aircraft under the negotiated terms.

This mechanism protects both parties: the buyer knows their money will only be paid to the seller if the agreed conditions are satisfied, and the seller knows the funds are already reserved and available before handing over the aircraft. Several companies specialising in aviation escrow, notably in the United States and the United Kingdom, offer this service for aircraft transactions, charging fees generally calculated as a percentage of the escrowed amount or as a flat fee depending on the provider.

Good to know — A genuine aviation escrow agent should never be chosen unilaterally by only one party without cross-checking: always obtain its full contact details independently, for example via its official website or a professional directory, rather than through a link supplied in an email.

Choosing a reliable escrow agent or trusted third party

Not every entity presenting itself as an "escrow" service is equally trustworthy. A serious aviation escrow agent generally has an established track record, a verifiable legal form, stable professional contact details, and a reputation that can be cross-checked independently with other buyers, brokers or industry associations. It should be able to confirm, through a channel separate from ordinary email (such as a phone call to a verified number), the bank details to which funds must be sent.

  • Verify the escrow company's legal existence independently, without relying on links provided in correspondence from the seller or broker.
  • Confirm bank details by phoning a number found independently, never relying on email alone.
  • Request a written escrow agreement spelling out the exact conditions for releasing funds and the applicable fees.
  • Be wary of excessive urgency or last-minute pressure to change bank details, a classic sign of attempted fraud.

Direct wire transfer: what you need to know

Many aircraft transactions still proceed by direct wire transfer between buyer and seller, without going through an escrow third party, particularly for smaller amounts or between parties who already know each other. This method remains legitimate, but it places the entire counterparty risk on one side or the other depending on the order of operations: if payment happens before delivery, the buyer carries the risk; if delivery happens before payment, the seller does.

Wire transfer also carries a well-documented specific risk: email interception and substitution of bank details by a malicious third party (so-called "business email compromise" fraud). It is advisable never to rely solely on bank details received by email, even if apparently from a usual address, and to always confirm details by phone before any significant transfer.

Good to know — An international wire transfer can be difficult, slow or costly to cancel or recall once executed. Before any substantial transfer, consider sending a token amount first to confirm the bank details are correct and belong to the intended recipient.

Letters of credit and other banking instruments

For higher-value transactions, particularly between a buyer and seller in different countries with no prior relationship of trust, some parties use a documentary letter of credit issued by the buyer's bank in favour of the seller, honoured only upon presentation of specific documents (invoice, proof of title transfer, delivery confirmation). This mechanism, common in international trade of high-value goods, offers a high level of security but involves higher banking fees and longer processing times than a simple wire transfer or a standard escrow arrangement.

Other options exist depending on the amount involved and the parties' habits: a bank guarantee, a blocked account held by a notary or lawyer acting as a trusted third party in some jurisdictions, or staged payment with a significant portion held back until final confirmation of the registration transfer. The choice of instrument depends above all on the amount at stake, the pre-existing level of trust between the parties, and the complexity of the transaction.

The escrow clause in the sales contract

Whichever payment mechanism is chosen, it should be formalised through a clear clause in the sales contract, specifying the exact amount to be paid, the payment schedule if applicable (deposit then balance), the precise conditions for releasing the funds, and how the escrow third party's fees are split between the parties. A vague or purely verbal arrangement exposes both parties to conflicting interpretations if a disagreement arises later.

The clause should also cover what happens if the deal falls through after funds have been placed in escrow, for example if the pre-purchase inspection reveals a major undisclosed defect: how and when funds are returned in full to the buyer, and how any costs already incurred by the escrow third party are allocated. Having this clause reviewed by a lawyer specialising in aviation law before signing remains a reasonable precaution for any transaction of significant value.

Specific precautions for cross-border deals

When the buyer and seller are located in different countries, the choice of payment currency, the country where the escrow account is held, and the law governing the contract should all be explicitly negotiated and put in writing before any funds move. An escrow account held in a neutral third country with recognised banking regulation is often preferred when both parties want to avoid the perception of favouring the other party's jurisdiction.

Banking fees linked to international transfers, currency conversion and, where applicable, escrow third-party commissions should be clearly allocated between the parties in the contract, to avoid any dispute over who bears these ancillary costs at final settlement.

Checklist before you transfer the funds

  • Verify the actual identity of the seller or buyer, beyond the information shown on an online listing alone.
  • Confirm any bank details received by email by phone, using a number found independently.
  • Verify the actual legal existence of any escrow third party or payment intermediary proposed.
  • Put the exact conditions for releasing funds and the allocation of fees in writing, in the sales contract.
  • Stay alert to any unusual urgency or last-minute change of bank details.
  • Keep a complete written record of all exchanges relating to the payment, in case of a later dispute.

A secure payment process is never a wasteful use of time or money: the fees of a professional escrow service, or the extra delay of a bank verification call, are trivial compared with the risk of losing the entire amount invested in a fraudulent transaction. For a used aircraft sale, care over the payment method should carry the same weight as the technical inspection of the aircraft.
