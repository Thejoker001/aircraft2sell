What is a gyrocopter

A gyrocopter, also known as an autogiro or gyroplane, is a rotary-wing aircraft whose rotor is not powered: it spins freely under the effect of the relative airflow generated by the propulsion unit (tractor or pusher propeller), a principle known as autorotation. Unlike a helicopter, a gyrocopter cannot perform true hover flight, but in return it offers simpler mechanics, generally more affordable maintenance and an appreciated inherent safety feature: in the event of an engine failure, the rotor keeps turning in autorotation and allows a controlled descent.

The most common models on the European used market are two-seaters, used for leisure flying, introductory training on rotary-wing aircraft, or, for some models, light professional uses such as aerial surveillance or photography.

Structurally, a gyrocopter consists of a free rotor in autorotation, a generally lightweight fuselage made of tube-and-fabric or composite materials, and a powerplant with a tractor or pusher propeller depending on the model. This relative mechanical simplicity, compared with a helicopter whose rotor is continuously driven by the engine through a complex transmission, largely explains the more accessible acquisition and maintenance costs for a rotary-wing flying experience.

Regulations and the licence category

In Europe, gyrocopters most often fall under a specific national regulation rather than a full EASA certification, much like many rotary-wing microlights. Flying a gyrocopter requires a licence or rating adapted to this category of aircraft, distinct from the fixed-wing licence (PPL) and the helicopter licence: it is generally a specific gyrocopter rating, whose exact content (training hours, theoretical and practical tests) varies by country and competent national authority.

  • Gyrocopter rating or licence — distinct from fixed-wing and helicopter licences; check with the national authority of the country of operation.
  • Regulatory category of the aircraft — microlight gyrocopter or certified gyrocopter depending on the model, with different obligations.
  • Training in autorotation and the specifics of handling — a central element of any serious training on this type of aircraft.
Good to know — The precise requirements (maximum weight, number of seats, training required) vary noticeably from one European country to another for microlight-category gyrocopters: always check with the national authority before any purchase or training.

The used gyrocopter market

The European used gyrocopter market remains considerably smaller than that of light aircraft or helicopters, but it is active, driven by a community of enthusiasts organised around clubs and specialist gatherings. Listings are found mainly on marketplaces specialising in light aviation and microlights, as well as through networks of manufacturers and approved dealers. The market's small size often means longer selling times than for a conventional aircraft, and a supply that is more geographically scattered.

Kits and amateur-built aircraft occupy a significant place in this segment, which makes the build and follow-up history just as decisive as the flight history when assessing a used aircraft.

Geographically, the supply of used gyrocopters remains concentrated in a few countries where the discipline benefits from a larger base of practitioners and more established national regulations, particularly the United Kingdom, France, Italy and Spain. A buyer willing to widen the search to several European countries mechanically increases the chances of finding an aircraft that precisely matches their budget and expectations, provided transport or ferry-flight logistics are planned for from the outset of negotiations.

Average prices by model and condition

Prices for a used gyrocopter vary significantly depending on age, engine, installed avionics and, above all, the care taken over maintenance and construction (for kit-built aircraft). A well-maintained aircraft with a complete maintenance history generally sells for noticeably more than a similar model with poorly documented upkeep.

Pre-purchase inspection and technical points to watch

Beyond the standard checks applicable to any used aircraft (engine logbook, engine hours, absence of corrosion), the gyrocopter presents specific inspection points linked to its rotary-wing mechanics. A pre-purchase inspection by a mechanic experienced with this type of aircraft remains essential, ideally a professional specifically familiar with gyrocopters rather than a general fixed-wing specialist.

  • Condition and balance of the rotor blades — any impact or deformation must be documented and assessed by a professional.
  • Rotor head and mechanical linkages — excessive play, bearing wear, absence of corrosion.
  • Engine logbook and engine hours (SMOH) — as with any aircraft, consistency between the flight logbook and the engine logbook.
  • Build quality (for kits) — welds, fasteners, compliance with the manufacturer's build instructions.
Point of caution — The rotor and its head are at the heart of a gyrocopter's safety: never trade a discount for a thorough inspection of this specific point.

Registration and insurance

Depending on its regulatory category (microlight gyrocopter or certified gyrocopter), the aircraft is registered with the competent national authority of the country of operation, with procedures similar to those for a microlight or a conventional light aircraft: proof of ownership, technical file or certificate of airworthiness as applicable, and a liability insurance certificate before the first flight under the new owner. As with any aircraft, one imported from another country must be deregistered from its register of origin before any new registration.

Good to know — Insurance for a gyrocopter must be adapted to this specific aircraft category; check with your insurer that it properly covers this type of aircraft before signing, as generic light-aviation policies do not always cover it.

Pitfalls to avoid

The most common mistakes when buying a used gyrocopter involve underestimating the importance of the build file for kit-built aircraft, failing to check the exact rating required to fly the intended model in the planned country of operation, and neglecting the specific rotor head inspection in favour of a simple general "light aircraft" inspection.

Tip — Before buying, talk to a club or association specialising in gyrocopters in your country: this network remains the best source for identifying a competent mechanic and assessing the reputation of a model or seller.

Finally, never skip a trial flight with an instructor or a pilot qualified on the intended model before finalising the purchase: a gyrocopter's handling, particularly during take-off and landing phases, differs noticeably from one model to another depending on rotor design and weight distribution. A hands-on trial, beyond simply reading technical sheets and logbooks, remains the best way to confirm that the aircraft truly matches your expectations before committing to the transaction.
