Delivery-day checklist for a purchased aircraft

Why a dedicated delivery-day checklist matters

The pre-purchase inspection and the price negotiation aren't the end of the buying process. Delivery day remains a critical moment: it is the last checkpoint before responsibility for the aircraft, in both contractual and practical terms, changes hands. A structured checklist helps ensure nothing is overlooked at a moment that's often rushed and emotionally charged, with the buyer eager to finally take possession of the aircraft.

This checklist complements the earlier pre-purchase inspection: it is not a substitute for a thorough technical check, but a way to confirm that nothing has changed since that inspection and that every contractual and administrative item is in order before departure.

Before you travel: documents to bring with you

Prepare your own file before heading to the aircraft, so you can immediately cross-check the documents presented by the seller against what was already exchanged during negotiation.

  • Copy of the signed purchase agreement, with all conditions precedent cleared (satisfactory inspection, financing secured).
  • Proof of the payment method arranged for the outstanding balance, as agreed with the seller.
  • Temporary or final insurance certificate covering the aircraft in your name from the delivery time onward.
  • Contact details for the mechanic who carried out the pre-purchase inspection, in case a last-minute question arises on an item in their report.
  • List of items the seller agreed to fix before delivery, so you can confirm they were actually addressed.

Document check on site

On site, before even approaching the aircraft, go through the entire technical and administrative file with the seller. This is the moment to confirm nothing is missing and that everything matches what was shown to you during negotiation.

  • Certificate of airworthiness and airworthiness review certificate (ARC) currently valid.
  • Flight logbook up to date, with no unexplained gaps or missing pages, and the latest flight matching the hour meter reading.
  • Engine logbook: total time and SMOH consistent with the flight logbook and the pre-purchase inspection report.
  • Maintenance logbook complete, with invoices for recent work and the list of airworthiness directives complied with.
  • Flight manuals and manufacturer technical documentation supplied with the aircraft, in their latest revision.
  • Keys, covers, chocks and any accessory included in the sale, matching what was stated in the listing or the contract.
Word of caution — If a document is missing or differs from what was presented during negotiation, hold off on completing delivery until the point is cleared up. Signing despite a documentary doubt gives away an important piece of negotiating leverage.

Final walk-around of the aircraft

Even after a satisfactory pre-purchase inspection, do a complete walk-around of the aircraft on delivery day. The goal isn't to redo the technical inspection, but to confirm no damage has occurred since the inspection report and that any agreed repairs were actually carried out.

  • No new impacts, signs of impact damage or visible leaks on the airframe, wings and landing gear.
  • Condition of tires, brakes and shock struts, especially if the aircraft has been moved since the inspection.
  • Oil and fuel levels matching what was agreed in the purchase agreement.
  • Repairs or refurbishment negotiated after the pre-purchase inspection actually completed, with the corresponding invoice.
  • Avionics and equipment advertised in the sale tested, either on battery power or with the engine running.

Engine run and test flight, if planned

If the terms of the deal allow it, a ground engine run — or even a short test flight before final signing — remains the best way to confirm the aircraft is behaving normally. Check the engine parameters (temperatures, pressures, RPM), the absence of unusual vibration and that the instruments work correctly in flight.

If a test flight isn't possible on delivery day for practical reasons (weather, availability), agree in writing on the terms and the deadline by which it must take place, as well as the consequences if a defect is found during that flight.

Ownership transfer and insurance formalities

Transferring ownership of an aircraft generally follows a formal procedure, with a signed bill of sale and supporting documents exchanged between seller and buyer. Make sure your insurance coverage takes effect exactly at the time of delivery, with no gap between the seller's coverage and yours.

  • Bill of sale signed by both parties, dated and stating the transfer time if the aircraft is due to depart the same day.
  • Cancellation of the seller's insurance effective from the delivery time, to avoid any overlap or gap in coverage.
  • Written confirmation from your insurer that the policy is active before any ferry flight.

Registration and notifying the authorities

Depending on the aircraft's country of registration and the one you may be transferring it to, specific procedures for notifying a change of ownership must be completed with the relevant civil aviation authority, within timeframes that vary by country. If the aircraft is moving to a new national register, a complete file must be assembled before any flight under the new registration.

Check with the competent authority of the country concerned for the exact deadlines and documents required, since these regulatory requirements are not harmonised across all European countries.

After delivery: filing and keeping your documents

Once delivery is complete, immediately file every document you received: bill of sale, pre-purchase inspection report, aircraft logbooks, insurance certificates and proof of completed administrative formalities. A well-organised file from day one of ownership makes any future resale, or dealings with the authorities, much easier.

Tip — Scan the entire technical and administrative file as soon as you get back from delivery, and keep a digital copy in addition to the paper originals.
