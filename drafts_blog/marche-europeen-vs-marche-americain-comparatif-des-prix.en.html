European market vs US market: a price comparison

Why does a used aircraft so often cost less in the United States than in Europe? Market structure, the exchange rate, and the real cost of a transatlantic import.

Two markets, two price dynamics

The US used aircraft market is historically deeper and more liquid than the European one: more aircraft in circulation, a denser network of approved mechanics, and a longer-standing private aviation culture. This depth often translates into lower listed prices for comparable models, particularly for the classic single-engine types that are so widespread in the United States.

In Europe, supply is smaller and more fragmented across countries, with local regulations, tax regimes and maintenance costs that vary significantly from one national market to another. This fragmentation limits liquidity and can keep prices higher on certain sought-after models, while also creating sizeable price gaps between European countries themselves.

Why prices differ structurally between Europe and the United States

Several structural factors explain the average price gap between the two markets, beyond the simple exchange rate. The sheer volume of production and resale activity in the US creates stronger competition among sellers, pushing prices down on the most common models. In Europe, higher maintenance costs, the relative scarcity of certain models, and parking constraints (hangar, tie-down) weigh differently on an aircraft's perceived value.

  • Fleet volume — a far larger North American fleet for most classic single-engine types.
  • Maintenance cost — labour and parts are often cheaper in the US, which supports lower resale prices.
  • Tax and regulation — a more uniform framework in the US, against a patchwork of national rules in Europe.

The role of the dollar/euro exchange rate in perceived price gaps

Part of the price gap observed between the two markets is not structural but purely monetary: fluctuations between the US dollar and the euro can, on their own, make the American market look more or less attractive from one year to the next, even though the real value of the aircraft hasn't changed. A European buyer should therefore think in terms of total cost converted at the day's exchange rate, not on the basis of a fixed or outdated rate.

This monetary factor explains why interest in importing from the United States runs in cycles: the weaker the dollar against the euro, the more affordable American aircraft appear to a European buyer, and vice versa.

Import and EASA/FAA compliance costs

The listed price gap between a US ad and a European one never reflects the real final cost of a transatlantic purchase. An FAA-certified aircraft generally needs to go through a recognition or conversion process toward EASA certification to be durably registered and operated in Europe, with inspection fees, potential compliance work and administrative paperwork to budget for.

  • Transatlantic transport (ferry flight or container shipping) to factor into the total budget.
  • FAA-to-EASA certification conversion costs, which vary by model and its technical file.
  • VAT and customs duties on import into the destination European country.
Good to know — The total cost of an aircraft imported from the United States almost always exceeds its listed purchase price. Always get a full cost breakdown before comparing it to an equivalent European offer.

Aircraft categories where the price gap is widest

The gap between the two markets is not uniform across aircraft categories. It tends to be most visible on the piston singles that are extremely common in the United States, where abundant supply pushes prices well below equivalent, rarer models on the European market. It is often less pronounced on more specific aircraft, or those already well established in Europe, where local supply remains sufficient to keep prices broadly comparable across both continents.

For turboprops and business jets, the gap depends more on the rarity of the specific model sought and its global operator base than on the geographic origin of the listing alone, as these categories are by nature more international.

The pitfalls of a "cheap" US purchase

A listed price noticeably below its European equivalent can hide differences in configuration, avionics or maintenance standard that partly explain the gap, with no direct link to geographic origin alone. It is essential to compare aircraft that are genuinely equivalent in terms of engine hours, time since the major overhaul (SMOH) and overall logbook condition, rather than focusing on the listed price alone.

  • Compare aircraft with genuinely comparable engine hours and SMOH, not just the same model designation.
  • Check the accident/incident history, often more easily accessible for a US-registered aircraft.
  • Cost out the full import before concluding that a US offer is genuinely more advantageous.

How to compare listings across both markets

To meaningfully compare a European listing with a US one, think in terms of total acquisition cost usable in Europe: purchase price converted at the day's exchange rate, plus transport, plus regulatory compliance, plus import taxes, minus whatever an equivalent refurbishment would cost on the competing European aircraft. This calculation provides a far more reliable comparison basis than the simple listed-price difference.

It is also worth arranging an independent pre-purchase inspection for both options, so as not to compare a theoretical US price against an already negotiated and verified European one. A simple line-by-line comparison table helps keep the decision objective rather than being swayed by the mere impression of a transatlantic "bargain".

  • List purchase price, transport, certification conversion, taxes and estimated refurbishment separately for each option.
  • Always convert amounts to euros at the current day's rate, not the rate noted during an earlier search.
  • Ask for a realistic timeline for each option: a transatlantic purchase almost always takes longer than a local one.

Aircraft availability also factors into the equation, even if it is not expressed in euros: a US aircraft that looks appealing on paper but is unavailable for several months of paperwork can turn out to be less attractive than a European aircraft that is immediately usable, especially for a buyer with a short-term flying plan.

Conclusion: compare beyond the sticker price

The US market often remains deeper and cheaper to buy into for many common models, but the real gap with the European market shrinks considerably once transport, regulatory compliance and import taxation are factored in. Before committing to a transatlantic option, always build a complete, comparable cost breakdown rather than relying on the sale price shown in the listing alone.

Caution — Never compare two listings on listed price alone: always factor in transport, certification conversion and import taxation before concluding that one market is more advantageous than the other.
