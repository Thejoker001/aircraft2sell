Aircraft Leasing and Financing in Europe: Comparing the Hubs

Why Ireland, Luxembourg and Malta account for a large share of aircraft leasing and financing activity in Europe, and how to pick the structure that fits your purchase.

Why Europe has dedicated aircraft financing hubs

Financing an aircraft, whether a light aircraft for private use or a fleet of business jets, rarely relies on a straightforward bank loan once the amounts involved go beyond a few hundred thousand euros. Professional players in the sector — leasing companies, specialist banks, investment funds — have gradually concentrated their activity around a handful of European jurisdictions that offer a stable legal framework, recognised local expertise and corporate structures well suited to owning aircraft.

Three centres stand out in particular across Europe: Ireland, Luxembourg and Malta. Each has built its own ecosystem, with its own strengths, advantages and limitations. Understanding the differences between them is useful even for a private buyer or a small professional operation, since the choice of registration country or holding company can have a direct impact on financing, insurance and the eventual resale of the aircraft.

Ireland: the aircraft leasing powerhouse

Ireland, and Dublin in particular, has established itself over several decades as one of the world's leading centres for aircraft leasing. This position stems from the long-standing presence of major international lessors, a deep pool of legal and financial expertise specialised in aircraft lease contracts, and a regulatory framework that international lenders consider predictable.

While most of this activity concerns the commercial fleets of airlines, the Irish ecosystem also, more marginally, benefits the business aviation and high-end light aviation segment: some operating lease structures for private jets or turboprops rely on Irish legal vehicles, particularly when the aircraft is intended to be leased or operated across several European countries.

  • Deep legal expertise — law firms and tax advisers well versed in international aircraft leasing contracts.
  • Mature financial ecosystem — established relationships with major banks and funds specialised in aircraft financing.
  • Framework suited to multi-country fleets — useful when an aircraft is operated or leased across several European jurisdictions.
Good to know — Ireland remains primarily the reference point for commercial fleet leasing. For a light aircraft or a private jet used mainly for personal purposes, going through an Irish structure usually only makes sense when several operators or countries are involved.

Luxembourg: structuring and institutional financing

Luxembourg occupies a particular place in the European landscape thanks to its recognised expertise in structuring investment vehicles and cross-border financing. The country has its own aircraft registry (LX prefix) and a favourable framework for setting up dedicated holding companies for an aircraft, a common practice among owners of business jets or aircraft of significant value.

Using a Luxembourg holding company often helps clarify governance (several co-owners, investors or users of the same aircraft), makes it easier to put a structured bank financing package in place, and simplifies certain procedures when the aircraft is intended to operate widely across Europe. Luxembourg also benefits from a strong presence of private banks and financial institutions used to handling this type of file.

  • Dedicated aircraft registry (LX) — recognised and aligned with European standards.
  • Expertise in structuring dedicated companies — useful for co-ownership or holding by several investors.
  • Access to a private banking network — experienced in financing high-value assets, including aircraft.

Malta: a dedicated registry and flexibility

Malta has, over several years, built up an aircraft registry (9H prefix) that attracts a growing number of private and professional owners, particularly in business aviation. The island has put in place a regulatory framework specifically designed for aircraft ownership and financing, with relatively fast procedures for registering security interests and mortgages over the aircraft, an important point for lenders.

This dedicated framework, combined with a tax regime that can prove attractive depending on the structure chosen and the owner's profile, explains the growth of the Maltese registry in the private jet and modern turboprop segment. As with any offshore or semi-offshore jurisdiction, it remains essential to have your situation validated by a specialist adviser, since the applicable rules depend heavily on the final owner's country of residence and the intended use of the aircraft.

  • Dedicated aircraft registry (9H) — security registration procedures designed to reassure lenders.
  • Framework designed for business aviation — particularly active in jets and turboprops.
  • Taxation to be confirmed case by case — depends on the owner's profile and country of tax residence.
Word of caution — The tax advantages often mentioned for Malta or Luxembourg vary significantly depending on individual circumstances and change as European regulation evolves. Never rely on a figure you've seen quoted without having it confirmed by a tax adviser specialised in aviation.

The main types of financing available

Beyond the choice of jurisdiction, several broad families of financing coexist across Europe for buying an aircraft, from a light aircraft to a business jet.

  • Conventional bank loan — suited to light aircraft and private buyers with a significant deposit.
  • Finance lease — the lender remains the legal owner during the contract term, with the user buying the aircraft at the end of the period.
  • Operating lease — common for professional fleets and some business jets, without a systematic purchase option at the end of the contract.
  • Financing through a dedicated company — a structure (often Luxembourg- or Malta-based) owns the aircraft on behalf of one or several owners, who use it.

Choosing the right structure for your project

For a light aircraft intended for personal use in Europe, going through an international financing hub is generally neither necessary nor cost-effective: a conventional bank loan or a finance lease taken out in the buyer's country of residence remains the simplest and administratively cheapest solution. These hubs become relevant once the aircraft represents a significant investment, involves several co-owners or investors, or is to be operated commercially across several countries.

Before committing to an international structure, it is worth asking a few simple questions: will the aircraft be used by a single person or several rights-holders? Does the financing come from a generalist bank or an institution specialised in aviation? Will the aircraft be resold in the short or medium term, and in which country? The answers naturally point towards the most suitable structure, without needing to automatically aim for the most sophisticated solution.

Pitfalls and points to watch

The main pitfall is choosing a complex structure in anticipation of tax advantages that are not fully understood, without the guidance of an adviser specialised in aviation law and international taxation. The rules evolve, vary according to the owner's country of residence, and can later undermine a poorly prepared arrangement. Another common mistake is overlooking the real cost of the structure (company formation fees, accounting, audits) relative to the value of the aircraft being financed: for a light aircraft, these costs can quickly outweigh any benefit sought.

Tip — Always have the full cost of an international financing structure (set-up and annual running costs) quantified before comparing it with a conventional financing arrangement in your home country: the net gain is sometimes smaller than expected once all the costs are factored in.
