Buying a Used Aircraft in Iceland or Norway: Which Country to Choose

Market, prices, tax, registration and maintenance: a comparison between Iceland and Norway to help you decide where to look for your used aircraft.

Two Nordic markets, two very different realities

Iceland and Norway share a Nordic climate and a strong general aviation tradition suited to remote territories, but their used-aircraft markets have little in common in terms of size. Norway, a far more populous country with a much denser airfield network, offers a noticeably wider supply of used aircraft, with a significant share of floatplanes and aircraft adapted to short strips along its fjords. Iceland, for its part, has a much smaller market, in line with its limited population and volcanic terrain, but remains active for rugged aircraft capable of operating from demanding strips and in changeable weather conditions.

For a buyer prepared to accept the logistical constraints specific to these two Nordic countries, widening the search to both markets can uncover an aircraft already set up for particular operating conditions rarely found elsewhere in continental Europe.

Size and nature of the used market

Norway's light aircraft fleet is one of the most active in Northern Europe, with a notable supply of floatplanes, ski-equipped aircraft and touring single-engines adapted to the short, unpaved strips found in coastal and mountainous regions. The country's great length from north to south also explains a widespread use of light aviation for connecting isolated communities, which supports a relatively dynamic used market for this type of aircraft.

The Icelandic market, by comparison, is much smaller in volume, in line with the country's limited size and population. Supply there is concentrated on a limited number of aircraft, often rugged single-engines used for flights over remote areas, recreational flying and, in some cases, glacier or volcanic observation. Finding a specific model in Iceland may require more patience, or widening the search to neighbouring countries.

Comparing prices

Price levels between the two markets are not directly comparable on a like-for-like basis, since the small size of the Icelandic market limits the number of comparable listings available for any given model. In Norway, the depth of the market often allows several similar listings to be compared before deciding, which supports a degree of price regulation through local supply and demand.

Because of Iceland's geographic remoteness, the cost of ferrying or shipping the aircraft by sea needs to be factored into the overall budget from the very start of the search, a far less penalising factor for a purchase made in mainland Norway.

Tax and purchase-related costs

Iceland and Norway are both members of the European Economic Area (EEA), which allows them to apply the common EASA regulatory framework for flight safety, without being members of the European Union or of its harmonised VAT regime. A sale between private individuals is generally not subject to VAT in either country, but the rules applicable to an import from an EU country, or to a sale by a professional, can differ noticeably from those in force within the Union.

For any transaction involving Iceland or Norway, it is worth checking the exact applicable formalities with the local tax and customs authorities, as these can vary depending on the buyer's status and the aircraft's country of origin. Using a professional experienced with transactions involving EEA countries outside the European Union reduces the risk of procedural errors.

Registration: Samgongustofa versus Luftfartstilsynet

In Iceland, aircraft registration is handled by Samgongustofa (the Icelandic Transport Authority), under the TF- prefix. In Norway, it is the Luftfartstilsynet (the Norwegian Civil Aviation Authority) that manages the registration register, under the LN- prefix. Both authorities apply the common EASA rules within the EEA framework, which considerably eases the registration transfer of an aircraft already certified under that framework.

  • Valid certificate of airworthiness and ARC — a precondition for any flight under the new registration, whichever destination country is chosen.
  • Deregistration from the register of origin — required before the new registration if the aircraft changes country.
  • Complete technical file — airframe, engine and propeller logbooks to be submitted to the new authority.
Good to know — The exact timeline for a registration transfer to Iceland or Norway depends on how complete the technical file submitted is; it is advisable to check the precise timelines and requirements directly with Samgongustofa or the Luftfartstilsynet before planning a transaction.

Maintenance network and parts availability

Norway has a more extensive network of approved maintenance workshops than Iceland, with particular experience of floatplanes and ski-equipped aircraft, a specialty rarely found elsewhere in Europe. The country's elongated geography, however, can mean sizeable distances between two workshops approved for a given aircraft type, a point worth anticipating for routine maintenance.

In Iceland, the maintenance network is much smaller, in line with the size of the local market. Sourcing spare parts for a less common model may require ordering from continental Europe or North America, with a delivery delay to anticipate because of the island's geographic isolation. This factor deserves consideration before buying, particularly for an aircraft whose parts would not be available locally.

Which country to favour depending on your profile

For a buyer looking for a rugged aircraft adapted to short strips, skis or amphibious operations, Norway offers a markedly wider choice and a more developed specialist maintenance network, while remaining more logistically accessible from the rest of continental Europe. For a buyer specifically interested in a smaller Icelandic market that matches a very particular use (flights over remote areas, glacier or volcanic observation), Iceland can offer aircraft already equipped and proven for that type of operation, at the cost of a more limited supply and potentially longer search times.

The overall cost of ownership must factor in ferrying or sea transport of the aircraft, particularly for a purchase made in Iceland, where island remoteness markedly increases this step compared with a purchase made in mainland Norway, which is more easily connected to the rest of Europe. This often-underestimated factor is worth quantifying precisely before settling on one listing over another.

Good to know — In all cases, a pre-purchase inspection by a qualified independent mechanic remains essential, whether the aircraft is located in Iceland, Norway or elsewhere in Europe.

Ultimately, the choice between these two markets depends above all on the type of aircraft sought and the buyer's tolerance for the logistical constraints specific to each country. Taking the time to compare available listings calmly, factoring in transport and long-term maintenance costs from the outset, remains the best strategy for finding the aircraft best suited to your use in these Nordic regions.
