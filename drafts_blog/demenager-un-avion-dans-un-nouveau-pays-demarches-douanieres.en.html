Moving an Aircraft to a New Country: Customs Procedures

Moving an aircraft to a new country: customs procedures, documents to gather, deregistration, re-registration and VAT considerations by destination country.

Why moving an aircraft abroad is a regulated process

Moving an aircraft from one country to another — whether triggered by a purchase, a professional relocation, or a change of operating base — is never a simple administrative formality. The aircraft crosses a customs border, potentially changes tax regime, and often needs to be re-registered on the destination country's register. Each of these steps has its own timeline, its own paperwork, and, depending on the destination country, its own tax rules.

Underestimating this complexity is one of the most common causes of delay in an international purchase or relocation project: an aircraft can end up stuck for weeks at an airport, grounded while customs paperwork is sorted out, while parking and insurance costs keep accumulating.

Documents to prepare before departure

Before even thinking about the ferry flight, assemble a complete file. The exact list varies by departure country and destination country, but a common core comes up almost every time:

  • Certificate of airworthiness (CofA) and a valid airworthiness review certificate.
  • Proof of ownership — bill of sale, purchase invoice, or title, depending on the nature of the transaction.
  • Up-to-date logbook and engine logbook, required for any re-registration process.
  • Deregistration certificate (or a deregistration request) from the departure country's registry, often required before a new registration can be granted.
  • Appropriate customs declaration depending on whether the aircraft is moving within the EU/EEA or coming from a third country.
  • Proof of VAT status — evidence that VAT has already been paid in an EU member state, where applicable, to avoid double taxation.
  • Valid insurance certificate for the destination country and for the ferry flight itself.

Customs procedures, country by country

Customs rules applying to an aircraft vary significantly depending on the departure country, the destination country, and whether the move is an intra-EU movement or an import from a third country. There is no single universal procedure: each customs administration applies its own forms, its own timelines, and, for taxation, its own rates. For these reasons, never rely on a tax rate or threshold found online without having it confirmed by the relevant customs authority or a specialized professional.

  • Intra-EU movement — in principle simplified by free movement of goods, but VAT status and registration should still be verified based on the aircraft's tax status and the destination country.
  • Import from a third country — generally requires a formal customs declaration, with duties and taxes due according to the destination country's rules; the exact level depends on the local regulations in force at the time of the operation.
  • Special customs regimes — some countries offer temporary or suspensive regimes (such as temporary admission) for an aircraft that does not permanently change owner or base; check with the relevant customs authority before departure.
  • Arrival clearance — plan for a recognized customs entry point (often a designated customs airport) for the ferry flight, and allow time for the paperwork to be processed on arrival.
Watch out — VAT rates, customs duties and exemption thresholds vary by destination country and change over time. Always verify this information with the relevant customs authority or a customs broker specialized in aviation before committing, rather than relying on a figure found online.

Deregistration and re-registration

An aircraft can generally only be registered on one register at a time. Before it can be entered on the destination country's register, it must be deregistered from the departure country's register — a step whose timeline is often underestimated.

  • Request the deregistration certificate well in advance: some registers process these requests in a few days, others in several weeks.
  • Check whether a lien, mortgage, or lease is recorded against the aircraft — it generally must be cleared before deregistration.
  • Prepare the new registration file in parallel for the destination country: requested registration mark, proof of ownership, certificate of airworthiness, and sometimes a local compliance inspection.
  • Plan for a transitional window where the aircraft is registered on neither register: some countries accept a ferry flight under a temporary registration or a special flight permit during this period.

Ferry flight or ground transport

For an airworthy aircraft, a ferry flight to the destination country is the most common solution. It requires an international flight plan, compliance with the airspaces crossed, and often a fuel stop in a third country if the aircraft's range requires it.

For a non-airworthy aircraft, one under restoration, or when distance and local regulations make a ferry flight impractical, partially disassembled ground transport (by road, or by sea for long intercontinental distances) remains an option — significantly more expensive and slower, but sometimes unavoidable.

Good to know — An international ferry flight generally requires specific insurance covering the route and the countries overflown, separate from the aircraft's regular insurance. Confirm this coverage before departure, not after.

Timelines and costs to anticipate

An international aircraft relocation combines several timelines that rarely add up in a simple linear way: obtaining the deregistration certificate, customs clearance, re-registration, and sometimes a local compliance inspection in the destination country. Costs to budget for, depending on the situation, include:

  • Fees for a customs broker or agent specialized in aviation.
  • Any duties and taxes due according to the destination country and the aircraft's status.
  • Ferry flight costs (fuel, stopover fees, ferry pilot fee if you are not flying the aircraft yourself).
  • Parking and insurance costs during the administrative grounding period.
  • Re-registration fees and, where applicable, local compliance inspection fees.

Working with a customs broker

For an international relocation, hiring a customs broker or agent specialized in aviation is, in most cases, money well spent. These professionals know the exact forms required, the right customs entry points, and the real processing times applied by each administration — information that changes over time and is risky to piece together yourself from unofficial online sources.

They can also anticipate special customs regimes applicable to your situation (temporary admission, conditional relief, etc.) that an individual often does not know exist, and help avoid declaration errors that can lead to penalties or a prolonged grounding of the aircraft.

Common mistakes to avoid

Certain mistakes recur regularly in international aircraft relocation files, and are almost always avoidable with enough advance preparation:

  • Scheduling the ferry flight before confirming the real processing time for deregistration and customs in both countries.
  • Trusting a tax rate or exemption threshold found on a forum or an unofficial website, without confirmation from the relevant authority.
  • Forgetting to check that no lien or mortgage is recorded against the aircraft before starting the deregistration process.
  • Neglecting specific ferry flight insurance, assuming the aircraft's regular policy is enough.
  • Underestimating the time needed for re-registration in the destination country, especially if a local compliance inspection is required.
Tip — Start the deregistration and customs process several weeks, or even months, before your target flight date, and have every regulatory point confirmed by the relevant authority or professional rather than relying on a general estimate.
