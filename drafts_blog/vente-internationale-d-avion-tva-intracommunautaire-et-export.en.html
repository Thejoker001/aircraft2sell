International Aircraft Sale: Intra-EU VAT and Export

Understanding how VAT applies when selling a used aircraft between EU countries or outside the EU: mechanisms, documentation and mistakes to avoid.

VAT principles on an aircraft sale

The sale of a used aircraft rarely crosses just one tax border: the seller, the buyer, the aircraft's current country of registration and its destination country can all be different. VAT treatment depends above all on three combined factors: the status of the parties (private individual or VAT-registered professional), whether the parties are located inside or outside the European Union, and the actual place of delivery of the aircraft at the time of the transaction. The same aircraft can therefore be treated very differently depending on whether it is sold between two professionals established in two member states, between two private individuals, or shipped to a third country.

Before negotiating the price, it is therefore essential to clarify the tax situation of the intended transaction, since VAT treatment can represent a very significant amount relative to the price of the aircraft itself. A misclassification can lead to a subsequent tax adjustment, long after the sale has closed and the aircraft has changed hands several times.

Sale between professionals within the EU

When a used aircraft is sold by a VAT-registered professional in one EU country to another VAT-registered professional in a different member state, the transaction generally falls under the intra-EU supply regime. The seller typically invoices VAT-free, and it is the buyer who self-assesses VAT in their own country under the reverse-charge mechanism, provided the substantive and formal conditions specific to this regime are met.

  • Both parties must hold a valid intra-EU VAT number, verifiable with national tax authorities.
  • The aircraft must actually leave the seller's country for the buyer's country, with evidence of transport or ferrying.
  • The invoice must explicitly state the exemption regime applicable to the intra-EU supply.
Good to know — The precise validation rules and supporting documents required vary depending on each member state's tax authority: an accountant or tax adviser specialising in intra-EU VAT must confirm the regime's applicability before invoicing.

Sale between private individuals within the EU

When the sale takes place between two private individuals, neither of whom is VAT-registered for the purposes of the transaction, the intra-EU supply regime between professionals does not apply. The situation becomes more complex if the aircraft was previously acquired by the private seller under conditions that gave rise to VAT recovery (for example, past professional use): in that case, a subsequent resale may still raise a residual VAT question, which should be checked with a tax law professional before the transaction.

In the vast majority of ordinary sales between EU-resident private individuals, for a used aircraft on which VAT has already been permanently settled by a previous owner (referred to as an aircraft in free tax circulation within the EU), no additional VAT is generally due on resale. It remains advisable, however, to have this tax status confirmed in writing, for instance through a seller's declaration or a documented history of previous transactions.

Export to a country outside the EU

When the aircraft is sold and physically shipped to a country located outside the European Union, the sale can in principle benefit from a VAT exemption on export, provided actual departure from the EU customs territory can be proven. This proof generally relies on a customs export declaration together with transport evidence (international flight plan, ferrying invoice, customs exit documents).

The seller must keep all these supporting documents for the statutory retention period applicable in their country, since they bear the burden of proof in the event of a tax audit concerning the exemption applied. A poorly documented export can lead the tax authority to reclassify the sale as a taxable transaction, with a VAT reassessment and penalties.

Point of caution — A simple change of registration to a non-EU register is not, on its own, proof of export for tax purposes: only evidence of actual physical and customs departure from EU territory is admissible before the tax authority.

Import from a country outside the EU

Conversely, importing a used aircraft from a country outside the European Union into a member state generally triggers import VAT, calculated on the customs value of the aircraft (purchase price, transport and insurance included up to the point of entry into the EU). This import VAT is added, where applicable, to any customs duties that apply based on the aircraft's tariff classification.

The applicable VAT rate and the customs declaration procedures vary by country of import: it is essential to use a customs broker or freight forwarder specialised in aviation to secure this step, particularly for a high-value aircraft where an error in the declared value can have significant financial consequences.

Documents and records to keep

Regardless of the nature of the transaction, a complete and consistent documentary file remains the best protection in the event of a later tax audit, sometimes several years after the sale. This file should bring together both the tax records and the technical records of the aircraft, as the tax authority may cross-check the two to verify the consistency of the case.

  • Detailed sales invoice — stating the VAT regime applied, intra-EU VAT numbers where relevant, and the aircraft's serial number.
  • Proof of transport or ferrying — flight plan, transport invoice, customs export or import declaration as applicable.
  • Logbook and certificate of airworthiness (CofA) — their consistency with the aircraft's declared history can be examined by the authorities if the reality of the transaction is questioned.
  • Engine hours history, including time since major overhaul (SMOH) and the date of the last major overhaul, useful to justify the declared value of the aircraft.
  • Evidence of previous tax status — proof that VAT was already permanently settled on the aircraft in a prior transaction, where relevant.

Common mistakes and points of caution

The costliest VAT mistakes on an international aircraft sale most often come from misjudging the applicable regime: treating a sale by default as an exempt intra-EU supply without having verified that the buyer actually holds a valid, active intra-EU VAT number, or treating a shipment to a third country as an export without holding the complete customs documentation.

Another common mistake is overlooking the aircraft's prior tax status: an aircraft on which VAT has never been permanently settled within the EU (for example, one kept under a suspensive customs regime) can carry a latent VAT liability that transfers to the buyer on resale, without the buyer always being informed of it at the time of negotiation.

Tip — For any significant international transaction, have the tax structure validated by an accountant or a tax lawyer specialising in aviation before signing, not after: the cost of this advice remains marginal compared to the risk of a VAT reassessment on the aircraft's full value.

Finally, keeping a written record of every exchange with the tax or customs authorities (VAT number confirmations, acknowledgements of export declarations) provides solid evidence should the transaction be audited several years after it took place, a timeframe that remains common in international tax audits.
