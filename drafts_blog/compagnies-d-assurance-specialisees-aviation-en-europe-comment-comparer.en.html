Specialist aviation insurers in Europe: how to compare

The aviation insurance landscape in Europe

Aviation insurance is a niche market, very different from motor or home insurance: the number of insurers capable of properly assessing aviation risk is limited, and a large part of the market runs through specialist brokers rather than direct contact with the underwriter carrying the risk. Some policies are underwritten by specialist European insurers, others by international syndicates operating through approved brokers in each country.

This structure has an important practical consequence for anyone buying or owning a used aircraft: comparing "two insurers" often actually means comparing two brokers placing the risk with different underwriters, sometimes the same underwriters but on different placement terms. It is therefore essential to compare the final policy (coverage, excess, exclusions) rather than just the brand reputation printed on the quote.

Comparing coverage line by line

A complete aviation policy combines several types of cover that are not always included by default. Comparing two quotes without breaking down each line often leads to comparing coverage levels that are not actually equivalent.

  • Liability cover — covers damage caused to third parties (passengers, people and property on the ground). Check the limit per claim and per passenger, since regulatory minimums vary by use and aircraft weight.
  • Hull cover (all-risk or ground-only) — compensates for loss of or damage to the aircraft itself. Check whether cover applies in flight as well as on the ground, or only one of the two.
  • War and hijacking cover — often excluded by default and must be bought separately if the aircraft is likely to fly to areas considered sensitive.
  • Named pilot cover — some policies restrict cover to one or more named pilots, with specific hours or qualification requirements.
  • Assistance and aircraft repatriation costs — useful if the aircraft is grounded far from base; check this line by line rather than assuming it is included.
Key point — Always ask for the full list of exclusions, not just the list of included coverage: that's often where the real difference between two similarly-priced policies is hiding.

Excess and agreed value: two levers that change everything

The excess (the amount the policyholder bears in the event of a claim) and the agreed value (the amount contractually fixed as the aircraft's value in case of total loss) are two parameters that directly influence the premium, yet are often underestimated when comparing quotes based only on the headline price.

A seemingly lower premium can hide a much higher excess, or an agreed value that would not cover the actual replacement cost of the aircraft in the event of total loss. Conversely, an agreed value set too high increases the premium with no real benefit if it exceeds the aircraft's observed market value. It is advisable to have the agreed value reassessed at every policy renewal, especially after significant work (major overhaul, avionics upgrade) that changes the aircraft's actual value.

Word of caution — Comparing two quotes solely on the annual premium amount, without aligning excess and agreed value to comparable figures, almost always leads to a distorted comparison.

Using a broker versus dealing directly

The majority of light aircraft owners in Europe go through a specialist aviation broker rather than contacting an insurer directly. A good broker knows which underwriters are available for a given aircraft and use profile, and can put several quotes in competition for you in a single step.

The trade-off is that the broker is paid (usually through a commission built into the premium), which makes comparing several brokers on the same aircraft profile just as useful as comparing several insurers. The questions to ask when choosing a broker (actual duties, remuneration, placement exclusivity) are covered in detail in our dedicated guide to choosing an aviation broker.

The criteria that actually matter when comparing two quotes

  • The liability limit per passenger and per claim, not just the headline overall limit.
  • The exact excess amount, line by line (ground damage, in-flight damage, total loss).
  • The method used to calculate agreed value and how often it is contractually reassessed.
  • Precise requirements regarding covered pilots (total hours, hours on type, qualifications).
  • The responsiveness and recognised financial strength of the final underwriter, not just the broker presenting the quote.
  • Cancellation terms and policy portability in case of resale or aircraft change.

Documents needed for a reliable quote

The quality of a quote depends directly on the quality of the information provided. Submitting a complete file from the first request produces quotes that are genuinely comparable across several insurers or brokers, rather than provisional estimates to be refined later.

  • Flight logbook and engine logbook (total hours and time since overhaul, SMOH).
  • Certificate of airworthiness and recent maintenance history.
  • Licence and logbook of each pilot to be covered, with total hours and hours on type.
  • Intended use of the aircraft (private, flight school, rental, commercial use).
  • Usual parking location (closed hangar or outdoor tie-down), which significantly affects the premium.

Common mistakes when comparing quotes

Certain mistakes come up repeatedly among owners who compare aviation insurance quotes without a method. Identifying them upfront avoids wasting time or signing an unsuitable policy.

  • Comparing only the premium price, without checking that coverage and excess are equivalent across quotes.
  • Forgetting to check coverage when changing pilots or adding a pilot with little experience on type.
  • Not reading the exclusions list carefully, particularly for international use or rental to third parties.
  • Waiting until the last moment before renewal to compare, which limits the time available to negotiate or switch underwriter.

A simple method for comparing with confidence

To compare specialist aviation insurers or brokers effectively, systematically request a line-by-line breakdown of coverage, excess and agreed value for every quote, plus the full list of exclusions. Then align these parameters on a common basis before comparing premiums — without this step, any price comparison stays misleading.

Plan to start this comparison several weeks before your current policy's renewal date, so you have time to negotiate, ask for clarifications, or switch underwriter without time pressure. This is particularly useful after a major engine overhaul or a significant avionics upgrade, both of which change the aircraft's actual value and therefore the relevant coverage terms.

Tip — Keep a written record of every quote broken down line by line: it is your best negotiating tool, both with your current broker and with any new candidate.
