Cessna 172 vs Piper Cherokee PA-28: which training aircraft to choose

High wing or low wing, the Cessna 172 and the Piper Cherokee PA-28 remain the world's two leading trainer aircraft. A head-to-head comparison to help you choose based on your budget and intended use.

Two legends of pilot training

The Cessna 172 Skyhawk and the Piper Cherokee PA-28 have competed for more than six decades for the title of the world's most widely used trainer aircraft. Produced in very large numbers, rugged, simple to maintain and found in abundance at flying clubs as well as among private owners, these two fixed-gear single-engines share the same mission: making flight training accessible and reassuring. Yet their architecture differs fundamentally — high wing versus low wing — which shapes handling, visibility, maintenance and, ultimately, the buying decision.

For a future owner or a club fleet manager, the question is therefore not "which one is better" in absolute terms, but which one best fits the intended use, the available budget and the habits already established in the training environment.

The Cessna 172: the universal high wing

The Cessna 172 is the most produced light aircraft in aviation history. Its high-wing layout provides excellent downward and sideways visibility, a valuable asset in training for observing the ground, other traffic and working the pattern. Its cabin is generally seen as a touch roomier than the Cherokee's, and its sturdy tricycle fixed gear readily forgives the occasionally heavy landings of beginner students.

The used market offers a very wide range of model years and engines, with avionics spanning from simple analogue panels to fully glass-cockpit versions (G1000 and equivalents) on the newest examples. This diversity makes it possible to find an aircraft suited to almost any budget, from a first club aircraft to a well-equipped personal example.

The Piper Cherokee PA-28: the sturdy low wing

The Piper Cherokee PA-28 was designed around the same era as the 172 to answer the same training and light touring needs, with a fundamentally different architecture: a low wing bolted beneath the fuselage, which provides good roll stability and a structure that's simple to visually inspect from the ground by looking over the top of the wing. Its cabin, slightly narrower depending on the version, remains comfortable for two occupants and classic training flights.

The PA-28 has gone through many variants over the decades (different engines, varying wingspans, retractable-gear versions for some derivatives), which also creates a very well-stocked used market. Its reputation for ruggedness and mechanical simplicity has made it a backbone of flight school fleets across Europe.

Flying and training: the real differences

For a student pilot, both aircraft remain broadly comparable in terms of ease of learning. The 172's high-wing layout makes it easier to spot the ground and visual references, a plus during pattern work and landing. The Cherokee's low-wing layout, meanwhile, is often described as slightly more stable in turbulent air and requires a bit more precision in the flare, though this is far from a major hurdle.

  • Cessna 172: better downward visibility, an approach many instructors describe as slightly gentler.
  • Piper Cherokee PA-28: good roll stability, wing structure that's easy to inspect visually.
  • Both models are certified and widely accepted by training organisations across Europe.
  • Transitioning between the two models for an already-trained pilot remains quick, as both are simple and conventional.

In practice, a club's or owner's choice often depends more on the existing fleet history, instructor habits and the local availability of a good example than on any intrinsic superiority of one model over the other.

Purchase budget and operating costs

For comparable model years, avionics and engine hours, used prices for the Cessna 172 and the Piper Cherokee PA-28 move within similar ranges. The differences observed mostly come down to each individual aircraft's condition: remaining engine potential before the major overhaul, the quality of documented maintenance in the logbook, any corrosion or previously repaired structural damage, and the level of avionics equipment.

Routine maintenance costs (periodic inspections, tyres, brakes, annual checks) also remain close between the two models, with a particularly dense network of mechanics trained on both types across Europe, which eases maintenance whichever one you choose.

Buying used: points to watch

Since both aircraft have been produced since the 1950s-1960s, many examples in circulation are several decades old, which calls for particular vigilance on points common to both models: the condition of the aluminium structure (corrosion, especially around fuel tanks and attachment points), the complete engine history and remaining hours before major overhaul (SMOH), the quality and continuity of the maintenance logbook, and compliance with applicable airworthiness directives.

On the Cherokee, specifically check the condition of the low wing attachment points and spars, areas particularly exposed to wear on aircraft that have flown heavily in training. On the 172, attention should focus on the main landing gear and shock struts, often stressed by beginner student landings.

Good to know — Whether it's a Cessna 172 or a Piper Cherokee that has served in a training fleet, always request the complete flight hours history and have a pre-purchase inspection carried out by an independent mechanic, ideally one specialised in the relevant type.

Resale and parts availability

Both models benefit from a particularly liquid resale market in Europe, thanks to their steady popularity with clubs, schools and private owners. The Cessna 172 enjoys a slight edge in the sheer volume of examples in circulation, which generally translates into a wider choice both when buying and reselling. The Piper Cherokee PA-28, although produced in slightly lower numbers, retains a loyal base of owners and clubs, with a spare parts supply that remains largely satisfactory.

In both cases, a well-documented aircraft with a complete maintenance logbook and up-to-date avionics generally sells faster and at a better price than a similar but poorly tracked example.

Which model to choose for your profile

For a club or school that wants to keep its fleet consistent with a type already in operation, staying with the same model simplifies instructor training and maintenance management. For an individual buyer with no fleet constraint, the choice can instead focus on the locally available example offering the best balance of price, engine potential and maintenance quality, regardless of the model.

In short, the Cessna 172 will particularly suit pilots who favour visibility and ease of handling, while the Piper Cherokee PA-28 will appeal to those who appreciate low-wing stability and simple visual inspection. In both cases, it's the individual aircraft's quality — far more than the choice of model — that will determine long-term satisfaction.
