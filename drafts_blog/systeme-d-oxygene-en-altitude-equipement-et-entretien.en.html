Oxygen Systems at Altitude: Equipment and Maintenance

Why supplemental oxygen becomes necessary at altitude, which equipment to choose (cylinder, concentrator, masks and cannulas) and how to maintain it properly.

Why oxygen becomes necessary at altitude

The higher the altitude, the lower the partial pressure of oxygen in the ambient air, progressively reducing the amount of oxygen available to the body with each breath. For a pilot flying an unpressurised cabin above a certain altitude, this decline translates into a measurable drop in cognitive and physical performance, well before any perceptible shortness of breath. It is precisely this mismatch between actual performance degradation and the absence of a felt warning signal that makes supplemental oxygen essential beyond a certain threshold, whether in a light aircraft, a glider, or a microlight flying over mountainous terrain.

Mountain flights, long high-altitude cruise legs, or night flights at moderate altitude are the situations most concerned. Even without a strict regulatory requirement applicable to your type of flight, having a functional, properly maintained oxygen system remains a sensible safety measure as soon as your planned cruise altitude drifts well above sea level.

Understanding hypoxia and its symptoms

Altitude hypoxia develops progressively: mild euphoria, difficulty concentrating, reduced peripheral vision, slower reaction time, then confusion and impaired judgement if exposure continues without correction. The main trap is that these symptoms are often imperceptible to the person experiencing them — it is usually a passenger or crew member who notices the change in behaviour before the pilot does.

  • Vision — reduced visual acuity and colour perception, often the first measurable sign.
  • Judgement — a sense of overconfidence, decisions less cautious than usual.
  • Coordination — less precise movements, fine piloting errors that go unnoticed at the time.
Word of caution — Sensitivity to hypoxia varies widely between individuals and depends on fatigue level, hydration, or recent alcohol or tobacco consumption. Never rely on a tolerance you think you observed on a previous flight.

Equipment: cylinders, concentrators, masks and cannulas

Two main families of equipment coexist in the light aviation market: compressed oxygen cylinder systems, the most widespread, and portable oxygen concentrators (POCs), more recent, which produce enriched oxygen from the ambient air without requiring a refill.

Cylinder system

Made up of a cylinder (aluminium or lighter composite), a pressure regulator, a flow meter, and a distribution device. It is the simplest and least expensive solution to implement, but it requires refills from an approved supplier and strict monitoring of the available pressure before each relevant flight.

Portable oxygen concentrator (POC)

Powered electrically (battery or aircraft socket), it removes the refill constraint but introduces electrical endurance management and a different maintenance routine (filters, battery). Its purchase cost is generally higher than a conventional cylinder system.

Masks and nasal cannulas

The mask covers nose and mouth, provides better sealing, and remains the reference above a certain altitude. The nasal cannula, lighter and compatible with wearing a radio headset, is suitable at moderate altitudes but becomes less effective as altitude increases, due to mouth breathing reducing the effective intake.

Tip — A portable pulse oximeter (a few tens of euros) lets you check blood oxygen saturation in flight and detect early hypoxia before subjective symptoms appear.

Choosing your system based on your flying profile

The choice of system depends above all on the type of flying you do: occasional mountain flights for a leisure pilot, regular high-altitude cruises for the owner of a high-performance single-engine aircraft, or intensive use on a turboprop or business jet that systematically operates at altitude.

Flying profileGenerally suitable solution
Occasional mountain flights, glidingCompact cylinder + nasal cannula
Regular high-altitude cruises (high-performance single-engine)Larger-capacity cylinder or portable concentrator + mask
Turboprop, business jet, systematic altitude operationsIntegrated aircraft system, dedicated masks per seat

For an aircraft with an integrated system (fitted to the airframe rather than portable), always check its compatibility with the number of occupied seats and the actual capacity available for the intended flight duration, not just its presence on the spec sheet.

Maintenance and periodic checks

A poorly maintained oxygen system can prove ineffective, or even dangerous, at the precise moment it becomes useful. Maintenance covers three distinct elements: the cylinder itself, the pressure regulator, and the distribution components (hoses, masks, cannulas).

  • Pressure check before each relevant flight — verify that the remaining pressure covers the planned flight duration with a safety margin.
  • Periodic cylinder requalification — every compressed oxygen cylinder is subject to a periodic hydraulic test at an approved facility, under the regulations applicable to the cylinder type.
  • Inspection of hoses and fittings — check for cracking, hardening, or contamination by grease or oil (incompatible with pure oxygen, risk of ignition).
  • Cleaning and replacement of masks/cannulas — according to usage frequency and manufacturer recommendations, particularly the parts in contact with skin.
Word of caution — Never use grease, oil or any fat-based lubricant on an oxygen fitting or regulator: contact between pure oxygen and a fatty substance can cause spontaneous ignition. Only lubricants specifically approved for oxygen use are permitted.

Storage and safety of the oxygen cylinder

The cylinder must be securely strapped down in the aircraft, protected from impact and heat sources, with its valve closed when not in use. On the ground, it should be stored in a ventilated area, away from any ignition source and flammable materials, in accordance with the supplier's instructions and the rules governing the transport and storage of compressed gases.

When transporting the cylinder to or from the aircraft, always handle it with the valve protection cap in place, and never let it roll or fall: a sharp impact on a pressurised cylinder can damage the regulator or, in an extreme case, cause a sudden failure of the whole assembly.

What to check when buying an equipped aircraft

If the aircraft you are considering is fitted with an integrated oxygen system, include this point in your pre-purchase inspection on the same footing as the airframe or engine. Ask for the cylinders' requalification documentation, the system's maintenance history, and test its actual operation rather than relying on its mere visual presence.

  • Date of each cylinder's last requalification and the next due date.
  • Visual condition of hoses, fittings and masks (cracking, wear, cleanliness).
  • Actual operation of the regulator and flow meter during a ground test.
Tip — An oxygen system in apparently good condition but never checked for several years represents a compliance cost to anticipate in your negotiation, even if it looks visually fine.
