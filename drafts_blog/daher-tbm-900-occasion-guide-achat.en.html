Used Daher TBM 900: buying guide

Why the TBM 900 appeals to owner-pilots

The Daher TBM 900, launched by the French manufacturer Daher in 2014, marks a notable evolution in the TBM lineage started by Socata and continued under the Daher brand. Compared with the preceding TBM 850, the TBM 900 introduced winglets, a new four-blade composite propeller, a redesigned engine cowling and split flaps that reduce drag and improve both cruise and landing performance.

This pressurised single-engine turboprop gives an owner-pilot performance close to that of some light jets, while remaining simpler and less costly to operate than a twin-turbine or a business jet. A pressurised cabin, high cruise speed and long range make it particularly well suited to business or family travel over long distances across Europe.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used TBM 900 depends above all on the production year, total airframe and engine hours, the engine's status relative to its overhaul programme, and the avionics configuration and options fitted (enhanced anti-ice system, cabin configuration, navigation equipment). The earliest examples in the series, being older, trade at a noticeably more accessible level than the last model years built before the arrival of the TBM 910.

Beyond the purchase price, your overall budget should include ancillary costs: a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic qualified on the type, registration and transfer fees, insurance suited to a turboprop, and possibly an avionics upgrade if the aircraft does not already have one.

The Pratt & Whitney PT6A engine: what to know

The TBM 900 is powered by a Pratt & Whitney Canada PT6A-family turboprop engine, a widely used and well-proven powerplant fitted to many aircraft types worldwide, which makes parts and approved maintenance shops easy to access. The engine maintenance programme is governed by an overhaul schedule set by the engine manufacturer, with intervals that vary depending on use and the maintenance programme chosen by the owner.

Before buying, it is essential to ask for the engine's exact status relative to its current overhaul programme, as well as complete documentation of the work carried out. Some owners enrol in an hourly-cost maintenance programme with the engine manufacturer, which smooths out overhaul costs over time and can be a strong argument at a future resale.

Good to know — Never rely solely on the displayed total hours: always ask a PT6A-approved shop for the detailed engine status before making an offer.

Garmin avionics and differences with the 910 and 930

The TBM 900 comes standard with a Garmin G1000 avionics suite, an integrated cockpit that combines flight instruments, navigation and engine monitoring on digital displays. This generation of avionics later evolved on subsequent versions of the range: the TBM 910 shares the same improved airframe with a newer G1000 NXi suite, while the TBM 930 introduces touchscreen Garmin G3000 avionics along with additional safety equipment.

For a used-aircraft buyer, it is important to check precisely which version and software update level is fitted to the aircraft in question, as this directly affects available features (mapping, onboard weather, decision-support tools) and the cost of any future upgrade.

What to check before buying

As with any used aircraft, but even more so for a pressurised turboprop, a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic qualified on the type is essential before finalising a transaction.

  • Complete PT6A engine status relative to its overhaul programme, and traceability of replaced parts.
  • Condition of the structure and winglets, and the history of any previous damage or repair.
  • Operation and sealing of the cabin pressurisation system, a significant expense item if faulty.
  • Update level of the Garmin G1000 avionics and compliance with current traffic-monitoring equipment requirements.
  • Complete logbook, engine logbook and technical file, with no grey areas in the aircraft's history.
Good to know — A written independent inspection report, including a specific opinion on the PT6A engine, remains the best protection against a bad surprise after the purchase.

Licence and training required to fly a turboprop

Flying a TBM 900 requires a rating suited to high-performance pressurised aircraft, in addition to the pilot licence required for this type of single-engine aircraft. Type-specific transition training, generally provided by an approved training centre or the Daher training network, is strongly recommended, and may even be required by some insurers before they will cover a pilot with little experience on this type of aircraft.

Before buying, it is worth checking with your insurer on the exact cover conditions and any additional training required, which may represent an extra cost and delay to factor into your buying project.

Maintenance and operating costs

Maintaining a TBM 900 requires approved shops familiar with the Daher airframe and the PT6A engine, a network that remains well developed in Europe given the popularity of both components across many aircraft types. The main expense items remain the mandatory annual inspection, insurance specific to a turboprop, parking and, in the longer term, engine overhaul once the programme's deadline is reached.

Rigorous, well-documented maintenance, ideally under an hourly-cost maintenance programme, is a strong argument at a future resale and limits unpleasant surprises during ownership.

Tip — Always ask for the detail of the latest engine and airframe maintenance invoices before making an offer: they give an accurate picture of how seriously the aircraft has been maintained.

Resale and the used market

The TBM 900 benefits from an active used market in Europe and worldwide, supported by the reputation of the TBM range and by parts availability linked to the popularity of the PT6A engine. A well-maintained aircraft, with a transparent history, a favourable engine status and up-to-date avionics, generally resells more easily and on better terms than one whose technical file has grey areas.

Comparing several listings of similar model years remains the best way to assess a fair price, both for buying and for preparing a future resale. Price differences between two aircraft of the same year are almost always explained by remaining engine status and the quality of the avionics fitted.
