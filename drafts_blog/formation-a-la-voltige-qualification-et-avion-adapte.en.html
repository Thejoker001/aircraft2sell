Aerobatics, a discipline of its own

Aerobatic flying involves performing, at the controls of an aircraft specifically designed or adapted for the purpose, manoeuvres that subject both airframe and pilot to load factors far beyond those of a normal touring flight. Loops, rolls, controlled spins and more advanced figures such as snap rolls are part of the basic repertoire taught from the very first hours of dedicated training.

Contrary to a common assumption, aerobatics is not reserved for competition pilots: many private pilots take it up to sharpen their precise handling of the aircraft, better manage unusual attitudes and gain confidence across their whole flying, well beyond the aerobatic box itself.

Prerequisites: pilot licence and experience

To enrol in aerobatic training you generally need to already hold a valid private pilot licence (PPL), or at least to have completed solo flight and the basics of handling with an instructor. Some schools, however, offer introductory aerobatic flights accessible from the early hours of training, carried out dual with an instructor, without constituting a formal rating.

Beyond the regulatory framework, good general physical fitness and the absence of any medical contraindication to load factors (the familiar "G") are common-sense prerequisites, checked in practice by the instructor from the first progressive flights onward.

The aerobatic rating in practice

In Europe, under the EASA regulatory framework, aerobatic flight beyond certain bank and pitch limits requires obtaining an aerobatic rating. This rating is obtained after a number of theoretical and practical training hours set by the approved training organisation (ATO) with which the pilot enrols, and confirms mastery of a set of basic figures as well as recovery procedures in case of an unusual attitude.

  • Theoretical training — aerodynamics under high load factors, the aircraft's structural limits, the physiology of aerobatic flight.
  • Dual practical training — basic figures, recovery procedures, controlled spin exercises.
  • Final assessment flight — validation of figures and safety procedures with a qualified instructor from the training organisation.
Good to know — The exact number of hours and the detail of the programme vary by approved training organisation; check directly with the ATO you are considering for the programme and the fees charged.

Progression steps, from beginner to competition

Progression in aerobatics is generally organised in levels, which also match the categories used in competition: the beginner level (often called the "elementary programme") introduces basic figures, flown solo under supervision, while intermediate and advanced levels progressively add figures that demand higher load factors and precision, up to the high-level programmes flown in international competition.

Each level requires a specific number of dual and then solo flight hours, supervised by a qualified instructor, before moving up to the next one. Progression stays individual: some pilots are content to stop at the introductory level purely for enjoyment and personal mastery, never aiming for competition.

Which aerobatic aircraft to choose for your level

The choice of aircraft depends directly on the aerobatic level you are aiming for and your available budget. For introductory flying and the first levels, aircraft such as the CAP 10 or the Decathlon offer good tolerance to moderate load factors in an accessible training setting, often available for rental through flying clubs.

For progression towards intermediate and advanced levels, specialised single- or two-seaters such as the Pitts Special offer a higher roll rate and sharper handling, at the cost of demanding more precise skills. At international competition level, aircraft such as the Extra 300 or the Zivko Edge 540 are designed to withstand very high load factors with a specifically reinforced structure and a powerful engine.

Budget: training hours and the cost of an aerobatic aircraft

The cost of aerobatic training mainly depends on the number of hours needed to obtain the targeted rating and the hourly rate of the aircraft used, usually higher than that of a standard touring aircraft because of more demanding airframe maintenance. A detailed quote from the chosen training organisation remains the only reliable way to estimate the total budget, since both the hourly rate and the required number of hours vary considerably from one ATO to another.

For a pilot considering buying a used aerobatic aircraft rather than renting through a club, the budget must also factor in the purchase cost, the specific maintenance linked to repeated load factors, and the rigorous tracking of the airframe logbook, often subject to fatigue life limits specific to this type of use.

Safety: human factors and required equipment

Aerobatics calls for particular attention to human factors: individual tolerance to load factors varies widely between pilots, and progressing too fast can lead to disorientation or discomfort that should be anticipated through a gradual build-up of exposure, always under the supervision of a qualified instructor.

  • Parachute — mandatory equipment for the pilot (and occupants) during aerobatic flights under many national regulations.
  • Suitable multi-point harness — essential reinforced restraint for high-load-factor figures.
  • Minimum safety height — a recovery altitude threshold strictly respected before each figure, as set by the training programme followed.
Good to know — The precise equipment and safety-height requirements vary according to national regulation and the training programme followed; always refer to the training organisation's operations manual.

Training at a club or investing in your own aircraft

For most pilots starting out in aerobatics, training within a flying club or an approved training organisation remains the simplest and most affordable option: the aircraft, its maintenance and the instructional support are shared, which lets you try the discipline without committing to a large budget.

A pilot who progresses steadily and considers competition may, at a more advanced stage, find it worthwhile to invest in a used aerobatic aircraft matching their level and flying style precisely, rather than depending on the availability of a shared club aircraft. This decision is best made after several seasons of regular practice, once your level and personal goals are better defined.

In all cases, whether flying a club aircraft or a personal one, strict compliance with structural limits and the maintenance programme specific to aerobatics remains the basic condition for a sustainable and safe practice of this demanding discipline.
