Why the King Air C90 remains a benchmark

The Beechcraft King Air C90 belongs to the large King Air family, pressurised twin turboprops produced continuously since the late 1960s. The C90 is the most compact and most accessible version, designed for short to medium missions with a reduced crew, often a single pilot. Its reputation for ruggedness, its relative ease of maintenance for a twin turboprop and the size of its fleet in circulation in Europe and the United States make it an aircraft regularly sought by buyers moving up from a high-performance single-engine piston to a first twin turboprop.

On the used market, the C90 comes in several successive sub-versions (C90, C90A, C90B, C90GT then C90GTx), with notable developments in avionics, available power and cabin layout. This diversity of model years, spanning several decades of production, explains a wide price range, which makes it essential to target your budget and sub-version carefully before starting a serious search.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used King Air C90 depends above all on the age of the aircraft, the sub-version, total engine and airframe hours, engine status (remaining potential before major overhaul) and the level of avionics fitted. The oldest models, with reduced engine potential and original analogue avionics, trade at a noticeably more accessible entry level than well-equipped recent versions with digital avionics, which represent a far larger investment.

Beyond the purchase price, your budget should include ancillary costs: a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic qualified on the type, registration and transfer fees, insurance (noticeably higher than on a single-engine piston), and possibly an avionics upgrade if the aircraft needs one to fly in current European airspace.

Engine hours, SMOH and major overhaul

The King Air C90 is powered by two Pratt & Whitney Canada PT6 turboprop engines. For this engine family, the key indicator to monitor is the number of hours elapsed since the last major overhaul, commonly noted SMOH (time since last overhaul). An engine close to its maximum potential before major overhaul must systematically be valued lower than a recently overhauled one, the cost gap between the two situations potentially representing a very significant share of the aircraft's total price.

It is essential to request the full detail of each engine: total hours, precise SMOH, history of critical parts replaced and compliance with the engine manufacturer's recommended maintenance program. Two aircraft of the same year can carry very different prices purely based on remaining engine potential.

Point of caution — Never rely solely on the total airframe hours shown in a listing: always request the detailed SMOH of each engine and have it confirmed by a shop approved on the type before making any offer.

What to check before buying

As with any used aircraft, and even more so for a twin turboprop, a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic qualified on the type is essential before finalising a transaction. For a King Air C90, a few points deserve particular attention.

  • Complete history of both engines: SMOH, compliance with the engine manufacturer's maintenance program, traceability of replaced parts.
  • Condition of the cabin pressurisation system and the pneumatic de-icing of the lifting surfaces, two systems specific to this type of aircraft.
  • Compliance of the avionics with current regulatory requirements (transponder, traffic-monitoring systems) for operation in Europe.
  • Condition of the retractable landing gear and its associated hydraulic systems.
  • Complete logbook and technical file, with no grey areas in the aircraft's history.
Good to know — Never rely solely on the seller's statements about overall condition: a written independent inspection report, including a borescope engine check, remains the best protection against a bad surprise after the purchase.

Maintenance and operating costs

The operating cost of a King Air C90 is noticeably higher than that of a single-engine piston, due to turbine fuel consumption, the hourly cost of engine maintenance and the aircraft's general complexity (pressurisation, hydraulic systems, retractable gear). The main expense items remain the mandatory annual inspection, insurance, parking and, in the longer term, the engine major overhaul once the hourly potential is reached.

Having the aircraft maintained at an approved shop familiar with the type helps limit unpleasant surprises and preserve its resale value. Rigorous, well-documented maintenance records are a strong argument at a future resale, all the more so for an aircraft in this category where buyers pay particular attention to the logbook.

Tip — Always ask for the last three maintenance invoices before making an offer: they give an accurate picture of how seriously the aircraft has been maintained, particularly on systems specific to turboprops.

Training and qualification required

Moving from a single-engine piston to a twin turboprop such as the King Air C90 requires type-specific training, in addition to the qualifications already held by the pilot. Beyond the regulatory aspect, this transition requires real adaptation to procedures specific to turbine operation (power management, engine-failure procedures, cabin pressurisation) which differ noticeably from those of a piston engine.

It is advisable to budget for this training from the start of the buying project, whether it is taken at a specialised training centre or directly financed as part of the acquisition with some professional sellers who offer type familiarisation support.

Resale and the used market

The King Air C90 benefits from an active used market in Europe and the United States, supported by a very large installed fleet and good parts availability, the model having been produced for decades. A well-maintained aircraft, with a transparent history, a favourable SMOH and up-to-date avionics, generally resells more easily and on better terms than one whose technical file has grey areas.

Comparing several listings of similar models remains the best way to assess a fair price, both for buying and for preparing a future resale. Price differences between two aircraft of the same sub-version are almost always explained by remaining engine potential and the quality of the installed avionics.
