Why the Cheetah appeals to owner-pilots

The Grumman AA-5A Cheetah is a fixed-gear four-seat single produced by American Aviation and then Grumman American from the mid-1970s to the early 1980s. Recognisable by its bubble-style sliding canopy and a structure built largely with bonded rather than riveted assembly, it stands out clearly from the Cessna 172 and Piper Cherokee of the same era thanks to its low profile and the wide, all-round visibility that pilots in training and leisure flying alike have long appreciated.

On the European used market, the Cheetah remains an interesting alternative to more common types: fewer examples are available, often at an attractive entry price, but the type demands particular attention to the canopy and the bonded structure, two traits specific to the Grumman American line.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used AA-5A Cheetah depends above all on airframe and engine hours, the remaining potential before the next overhaul, the condition of the canopy and airframe, and the level of avionics fitted. An aircraft with an engine close to its overhaul limit trades noticeably cheaper than a recently overhauled, well-equipped example, but the price gap should always be weighed against the cost of the overhaul that is coming.

Engine hours remaining before overhaul (SMOH)A low SMOH weighs heavily on price, both up and down

Beyond the purchase price, budget for ancillary costs: an independent mechanic's pre-purchase inspection familiar with the Grumman American line, registration fees, insurance, and possibly an avionics refresh if the aircraft needs one.

What to check before buying

As with any used aircraft, a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic qualified on the type remains the best protection before finalising a transaction. For a Cheetah, a few points deserve particular attention.

  • Condition and mechanism of the sliding canopy: rails, weatherstrip seals and the absence of cracks in the plexiglass, a classic weak point of the line.
  • Integrity of the bonded structural joints, which deserve careful inspection since repairing a structural debond can be lengthy and costly.
  • Complete Lycoming engine history: engine hours, compliance with the manufacturer's maintenance program, traceability of replaced parts.
  • Complete logbook and technical file, with a current airworthiness certificate (CofA) and no grey areas in the aircraft's history.
  • Condition of the fixed landing gear and tyres, as well as the exterior paint, which often reveals the overall standard of upkeep.
Good to know — Never rely solely on the seller's statements about overall condition: a written independent inspection report remains the best protection against a bad surprise after the purchase.

Sliding canopy and bonded structure: known weak points

The bubble-style sliding canopy that gives the Cheetah its distinctive look is also a point of technical attention: its sliding mechanism and weatherstrip seals wear with time and opening cycles, and a cracked or yellowed canopy needs replacing for both safety and in-flight comfort.

The airframe also makes extensive use of bonded assembly rather than riveting, a technique that requires specific expertise during inspection: a mechanic unfamiliar with the Grumman American line may miss a flaw that a specialist on the type would spot immediately.

Lycoming O-235 engine: upkeep and remaining potential

The Cheetah is most commonly powered by a Lycoming O-235, a four-cylinder engine known for its reliability and its running costs, kept lower than more powerful engines. Tracking the remaining engine potential (hours since the last major overhaul) remains the number one factor to check before any purchase, since a major overhaul represents a significant expense to plan for in your financing.

Regular maintenance carried out at an approved shop familiar with the type, with oil changes and periodic oil analysis, helps limit unpleasant surprises and preserve the aircraft's resale value.

Tip — Always ask for the last three maintenance invoices before making an offer: they give an accurate picture of how seriously the aircraft has been maintained.

Real-world use: training, leisure and short trips

The Cheetah is mainly used for leisure flying and training, thanks to gentle, predictable handling and running costs that stay reasonable for a four-seater. It also handles short to medium family trips well, although its payload and cruise performance remain more modest than more powerful types of the same era.

This versatility makes it a sensible choice for a first purchase or for shared ownership within a club or a group of pilots, provided the owner plans ahead for the maintenance costs specific to the bonded structure and canopy.

Resale and the used market

The Cheetah's used market remains smaller than that of the Cessna 172 or Piper Cherokee, which can lengthen the time to resale but also offers buying opportunities at an attractive price for a patient buyer. A well-maintained aircraft, with a transparent history and a canopy in good condition, generally resells more easily than one whose technical file has grey areas.

Comparing several listings of similar models remains the best way to assess a fair price, both for buying and for preparing a future resale. Price differences between two aircraft of the same model year are almost always explained by remaining engine potential and canopy condition.
