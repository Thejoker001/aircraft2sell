The Piper Apache: Piper's pioneering light twin

The Piper PA-23 Apache holds a special place in light aviation history: it was the first twin-engine aircraft produced in series by Piper Aircraft, launched in the mid-1950s. Unlike the tube-and-fabric single-engine aircraft the manufacturer had built until then, the Apache was designed with an all-metal airframe, retractable gear and two wing-mounted piston engines. It paved the way for an entire family of light twins at Piper, with the Twin Comanche, the Aztec and later the Seneca as direct descendants or subsequent developments.

On the used market, the Apache mainly appeals to owner-pilots who want to move up to a twin-engine rating without committing to the budget of a newer, higher-performance model such as the Aztec or the Seneca. Its recognisable silhouette, with its straight vertical tail (later replaced by a swept tail on the last production aircraft and on the Aztec that succeeded it), and its cabin, roomy for its era, make it an aircraft still sought after by a community of owners who are passionate about classic light twins.

Versions and developments of the model

Over its commercial career, the PA-23 went through several engine upgrades, generally identified by the power installed on each engine. The earliest versions had a more modest powerplant, before later versions received more powerful engines, noticeably improving climb performance and the safety margin in the event of an in-flight engine failure. The most capable version in the range, often referred to as the Apache 235, benefited from markedly higher installed power than the very first production aircraft.

Before targeting a specific aircraft, it is essential to ask the seller for the exact version of the model, the serial number, and the list of manufacturer bulletins and airworthiness directives that apply. Some aircraft have also undergone later modifications, including engine replacements or avionics retrofits, which should be precisely documented in the technical file before any transaction.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used Piper Apache depends mainly on the age of the aircraft, the overhaul status of each of the two engines, total airframe hours, the condition of the metal structure and the level of avionics fitted. An Apache with minimal maintenance history and close to the overhaul limit on one or both engines trades at a noticeably more accessible entry level than a well-maintained example with a comfortable remaining potential on both engines and updated avionics.

Beyond the purchase price, your budget should include ancillary costs: an independent mechanic's pre-purchase inspection on the type, registration and transfer fees, insurance (generally higher than for an equivalent single-engine aircraft), and possibly an avionics upgrade if the aircraft needs one.

What to check before buying

As with any used aircraft, a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic qualified on the type is essential before finalising a transaction. For an Apache, a few points deserve particular attention, related to its age and twin-engine configuration.

  • Corrosion of the metal structure: pay particular attention to sensitive areas such as the spars, landing gear attachment points and cabin floor, especially on an aircraft kept outdoors or in a humid environment.
  • Complete history of both engines: hours elapsed since the last overhaul for each, compliance with the manufacturer's maintenance program, traceability of replaced parts on each powerplant.
  • Condition of the retractable landing gear, control cables and the associated hydraulic or electrical system, as well as correct propeller synchronisation.
  • Condition and updates of the onboard avionics, as well as compliance with current regulatory requirements (transponder, traffic-monitoring systems).
  • Complete logbook and technical file for the airframe and for each engine, with no grey areas in the aircraft's history.
Good to know — Never rely solely on the seller's statements about overall condition: a written independent inspection report, ideally carried out by a workshop familiar with older Piper twins, remains the best protection against a bad surprise after the purchase.

Twin-engine flying profile and training

The Apache is historically regarded as a relatively docile twin to fly, which made it a popular training aircraft for multi-engine qualification for a long time. Its single-engine performance margin, however, remains modest compared with newer, more powerful twins, a point every candidate pilot must factor into their preparation and into regular practice of engine-failure procedures.

Thorough training on this type of aircraft, including repeated practice of engine-failure procedures on takeoff and in cruise, is essential before considering private ownership and operation. Regular contact with an instructor qualified on the type, even after obtaining the rating, remains advisable to maintain safe reflexes on this type of aircraft.

Many current Apache owners value the aircraft precisely for its budget accessibility as an entry point into the world of twins, before possibly considering a step up to an Aztec or a Seneca once multi-engine experience has been gained.

Maintenance and operating costs

The operating cost of an Apache is structurally higher than that of a comparable single-engine aircraft, since it requires budgeting for the routine maintenance and overhaul of two engines rather than one. The main expense items remain the mandatory annual inspection, insurance, parking and, in the longer term, the overhaul of each of the two engines once their hourly potential is reached.

Having the aircraft maintained at an approved shop familiar with older Piper twins helps limit unpleasant surprises and preserve its resale value. Rigorous, well-documented maintenance records for each of the two engines are a strong argument at a future resale.

Tip — Always ask for the last three maintenance invoices for each of the two engines before making an offer: they give an accurate picture of how seriously the aircraft has been maintained.

Resale and the used market

The used market for the Apache remains smaller than that of newer twins such as the Aztec or the Seneca, but a loyal community of owners and a handful of workshops specialising in older Pipers ensure reasonable availability of common parts. A well-maintained aircraft, with a transparent history on both engines and functional avionics, generally resells more easily and on better terms than one whose technical file has grey areas.

Comparing several listings of similar aircraft remains the best way to assess a fair price, both for buying and for preparing a future resale. Price differences between two aircraft of the same model year are almost always explained by the remaining potential on each of the two engines and by the documented condition of the metal structure.
