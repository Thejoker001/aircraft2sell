Cessna 180 vs Maule M-7: which classic bush plane to choose

Two legends of bush flying

The Cessna 180 and the Maule M-7 hold a special place among classic bush aircraft. Both are taildraggers built to operate from rough strips — grass, gravel, sand or snow. Yet their design philosophies differ in meaningful ways, and choosing between them really comes down to intended use and the budget you're willing to commit.

The Cessna 180 embodies the toughness of a 1950s design, mass-produced and proven on every continent. The Maule M-7, with a more modern development history, focuses on STOL performance (short take-off and landing) and an engine lineup that varies across versions. Comparing the two aircraft helps clarify what a buyer of a used bush plane is really looking for.

Before digging into performance and budget, it's worth noting the secondary market each type lives in across Europe: both remain a minority here compared with North America, which often means widening the search to several countries and planning for import procedures if the aircraft comes from a non-European registry.

The Cessna 180: the unbreakable taildragger

Produced from 1953 to 1981, the Cessna 180 became a reference for light transport in remote regions, aerial work and recreational flying off difficult terrain. Its all-metal airframe, well known for toughness, combined with a conventional tailwheel gear, makes it an aircraft able to absorb strip conditions that few single-engine types tolerate as well.

On the used market, the Cessna 180 benefits from a large installed fleet and good parts availability, especially in North America. Many examples have received approved modifications — STOL kits, oversized tires, constant-speed propellers — that noticeably improve short take-off and landing capability compared with the original factory configuration.

That reputation for toughness comes with a flip side: older aircraft often carry high accumulated engine hours, and maintenance history can vary widely depending on the aircraft's region of origin. A careful review of the logbook remains essential before any purchase.

The Maule M-7: STOL performance and modern engines

The Maule M-7 belongs to a family developed from the 1980s onward, designed from the outset to excel at short take-off and landing. Its purpose-built wing, leading-edge slats and large-travel flaps allow it to operate from noticeably shorter strips than most classic single-engine aircraft, including the Cessna 180.

The manufacturer offered the M-7 with several engines over the years, from four-cylinder to six-cylinder, allowing a fairly fine balance between available power, useful load and fuel burn depending on intended use. That variety of versions is both a flexibility advantage and a complicating factor when buying, since performance figures vary noticeably from one engine option to another.

Unlike the Cessna 180, whose production ended in the early 1980s, the Maule M-7 kept evolving, which translates on the used market into generally younger aircraft and easier access to factory-original parts.

Comparing performance and bush capability

On paper, the Maule M-7 generally posts shorter take-off and landing distances than the Cessna 180 at comparable weight, thanks to a wing optimized for slow flight. The Cessna 180, in turn, compensates with an airframe known for absorbing rough strip conditions and a useful load that stays very competitive once fitted with appropriate tires.

In practice, the choice between the two often comes down to practical factors: local availability of a shop experienced with the type, parts access in your operating region, and a personal preference for the feel in flight, which differs noticeably between an all-metal airframe and a lighter fabric-covered structure.

Purchase budget and operating costs

Acquisition budget depends heavily on model year, remaining engine hours before the next major overhaul, and equipment level (STOL kits, avionics, oversized tires). An older Cessna 180 with reduced remaining engine life generally trades at a more accessible entry point than a well-equipped recent Maule M-7, but the gap narrows considerably depending on the modifications fitted to each individual aircraft.

On the operating side, both aircraft sit within a comparable cost range for a piston single in this category, with a few nuances: maintaining the fabric sections found on parts of the Maule requires regular fabric inspection, while the all-metal Cessna 180 airframe demands increased vigilance regarding corrosion, particularly for aircraft that have operated on floats or in humid environments.

Buying either used: points to watch

Whether a Cessna 180 or a Maule M-7, the pre-purchase inspection should be entrusted to an independent mechanic familiar with bush aircraft and their real-world use. A few points deserve particular attention.

  • Complete engine history: accumulated engine hours, SMOH status (hours since major overhaul), and traceability of major interventions.
  • Structural condition: checking for corrosion on the Cessna 180, and inspecting fabric and tube structure condition on the fabric-covered sections of the Maule.
  • Compliance and documentation of approved modifications (STOL kits, tires, propeller), which must appear in the aircraft's technical file.
  • Validity of the CofA and consistency of the logbook with the aircraft's declared real-world use (aerial work, recreational, floats).
  • Operational history: a bush aircraft used intensively on rough terrain deserves a more thorough inspection of the landing gear and attachment points.
Good to know — Always request the full logbook and maintenance invoices, and have the pre-purchase inspection carried out by a shop experienced with bush aircraft, whose structural stresses differ from those of an aircraft used only on paved runways.

It's also worth remembering that both types often had an intensive working life before reaching the used market: aerial work, towing, or operation on floats or skis depending on the region. That operational past directly affects the airframe's real condition and should be documented as precisely as possible by the seller, beyond the hours shown on the meter.

Ultimately, the Cessna 180 will appeal to buyers who favor a proven all-metal airframe and a wide choice of modified aircraft on the market, while the Maule M-7 will suit those seeking maximum STOL performance and simplified access to factory parts on a generally younger aircraft.
