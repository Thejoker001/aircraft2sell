Buying a Used Aircraft in Croatia or Greece: Which Country to Choose

Market, prices, taxation, registration and maintenance: a comparison between Croatia and Greece to help you decide where to look for your used aircraft.

Two Mediterranean markets, two contrasting profiles

Croatia and Greece both front the eastern Mediterranean, favourable terrain for leisure flying, but their used-aircraft markets have developed quite differently. Greece has an older light aviation fleet, concentrated mainly around Athens and a handful of historic flying clubs on the mainland and larger islands, though activity there remains quieter than in other parts of Western Europe. Croatia, whose civil aviation sector has grown considerably since joining the European Union in 2013, has a younger, growing fleet, driven by coastal tourism along the Dalmatian coast and a rising number of active flying clubs.

A buyer based in Greece may well find an interesting aircraft in Croatia, and vice versa, even though the distance between the two countries is greater than between two direct neighbours, which means ferrying needs to be planned carefully. Understanding the administrative specifics of each market before embarking on a cross-border transaction remains essential, not least because the two countries have different lengths of regulatory membership within the European Union.

Size and activity of the used-aircraft market

The Greek used-aircraft market remains modest in volume, with listings focused on light touring single-engine aircraft and a handful of microlights, often offered by flying clubs or private owners rather than structured dealers. The density of airfields usable for light aviation remains limited outside the Athens region, which can lengthen the search for a buyer who is particular about the aircraft's location.

The Croatian market, while also small by European standards, shows a more visible momentum, driven by growing coastal air tourism and a more regular flow of listings, concentrated around Zagreb and the airfields of Dalmatia. Foreign buyers are also more numerous there, drawn by the flying environment along the Adriatic.

Price comparison

For a comparable model and equivalent maintenance condition, price levels remain broadly close between the two markets, with observed differences owing more to the local scarcity of certain models than to any structural gap between Croatia and Greece. An aircraft with a complete, consistent maintenance logbook sells at a similar level on either shore of the eastern Mediterranean.

In both cases, comparing several listings of similar models remains the best way to assess a fair price before committing, regardless of which country the aircraft is located in.

Tourist seasonality influences both markets, though slightly differently: in Croatia, part of the supply appears at the end of the summer season as owners reconsider their plans, while in Greece sales activity stays more evenly spread across the year, in the absence of as pronounced an aviation tourism peak on the light-aircraft segment.

Taxation and purchase-related costs

The taxation applicable to the purchase of a used aircraft depends above all on the buyer's status (private individual or professional), their country of tax residence, and the country in which the aircraft will be registered after the transaction, rather than simply the country where the aircraft happens to be at the time of purchase. In a transaction between Croatia and Greece, two European Union member states, it is advisable to find out precisely about any intra-EU VAT formalities applicable between professionals, as well as the ownership transfer fees specific to each country, which can vary significantly.

Using a professional experienced in cross-border transactions (broker, lawyer specialising in aviation law) helps avoid procedural mistakes that can prove costly, particularly regarding VAT or documentation when the aircraft changes country of registration.

Registration and administrative procedures

In Croatia, civil aircraft registration falls under the Croatian Civil Aviation Agency (Hrvatska agencija za civilno zračno prometstvo, CCAA), under the 9A- prefix. In Greece, it is the HCAA (Hellenic Civil Aviation Authority) that holds the national register, under the SX- prefix. An aircraft purchased in either country can be registered in the other, depending on the buyer's choice: there is no obligation to keep the registration of the country of purchase. This change involves specific procedures with the destination authority, including submitting the complete technical file, checking the validity of the certificate of airworthiness, and, where applicable, a compliance inspection.

  • Check the consistency between the maintenance logbook, the technical file and the actual condition of the aircraft before any registration change.
  • Plan for administrative timelines, which can vary significantly between the Croatian CCAA and the Greek HCAA depending on current workload.
  • Check for any specific requirements of the destination country regarding onboard equipment.
  • Budget for ferrying costs if an inspection or remedial work is required before the transfer.

Maintenance network and parts availability

Greece has a network of approved maintenance workshops concentrated mainly around Athens, with more limited coverage on the islands and in peripheral regions, which can lengthen turnaround times for some less common models. Croatia has a similarly small but differently distributed network, mainly present around Zagreb and the main coastal airfields of Dalmatia.

For rarer models or specific engines, it is worth checking in advance on parts availability and the proximity of a competent workshop before finalising the purchase, regardless of the aircraft's country of origin. The distance between Croatia and Greece is greater than between two bordering countries, which makes ferrying for maintenance between the two less practical on a day-to-day basis than with a direct neighbour.

The language barrier can also come into play: a Greek buyer not fluent in Croatian will often favour an English-speaking workshop, and a Croatian buyer will reason the same way in reverse. In both cases, technical documentation in English is generally accepted by approved workshops in both countries.

Which country to choose based on your profile

For a buyer who prioritises a growing market with a regular flow of listings and a flying environment oriented towards coastal navigation, Croatia can offer interesting opportunities, particularly along the Dalmatian coast. For a buyer more attached to the eastern Mediterranean basin and willing to work with a smaller but stable market, Greece remains an option worth considering, especially for leisure use centred on the mainland or the larger islands.

Good to know — In all cases, a pre-purchase inspection by a qualified independent mechanic remains essential, whether the aircraft is located in Croatia, Greece or elsewhere in Europe.

Climate and intended use can also guide the choice: a buyer mainly looking for a leisure aircraft to fly along the Dalmatian coast will naturally lean towards the Croatian market, while a buyer prioritising a foothold in the Aegean basin will often stay loyal to the Greek market. The two approaches are not mutually exclusive: nothing prevents searching for an aircraft in Greece while planning to have it maintained elsewhere in Europe, or vice versa, provided ferrying distances remain reasonable.

Ultimately, the country of purchase matters less than the actual quality of the aircraft and how well it has been maintained. Comparing listings from both markets widens your options and gives you a better negotiating position, whether on price or on the terms of the transaction.
