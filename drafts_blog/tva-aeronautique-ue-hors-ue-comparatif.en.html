Aviation VAT in the EU vs outside the EU: a comparison

Switzerland, the UK and Norway: how aviation VAT differs depending on where you buy, register or deliver an aircraft, and what to check before you sign.

Why aviation VAT varies so much across Europe

The same aircraft can be treated very differently depending on where the sale is legally completed, which country the aircraft will be registered in, and where the buyer is tax resident. The European Union harmonises the broad principles of VAT among its members, but each member state keeps some latitude on rates and specific exemptions. Outside the EU, countries such as Switzerland, the UK and Norway run their own VAT and customs rules — some close to the EU model, some meaningfully different.

For a light aircraft buyer, this matters in three common scenarios: buying an aircraft already based in another European country, importing an aircraft from a non-EU country, or reselling an aircraft registered in one country to be based in another. In all three cases, the VAT treatment depends far more on how the transaction is classified — private sale, professional sale, import — than on the aircraft's "nationality" alone.

The VAT regime inside the European Union

Within the EU single market, a sale between private individuals resident in the EU is generally outside the scope of VAT once the aircraft has already been placed in free circulation in the Union (VAT already settled, or EU-manufactured and never exported). A sale carried out by a professional — a dealer, management company or club — can trigger VAT, depending on the seller's status and the country the transaction is legally linked to.

  • Private sale between EU residents — generally outside the scope of VAT if the aircraft is already in free circulation in the Union.
  • Sale by a professional seller — VAT potentially due, with rates and rules specific to the country of the transaction.
  • Aircraft never customs-cleared in the EU — import VAT can still be owed even after several internal resales.

The most common pitfall sits precisely in that last case: an aircraft may have changed hands several times within Europe without its customs and VAT position ever being properly clarified. It's worth obtaining documentary proof of free-circulation status before assuming an aircraft is "clean".

Switzerland: outside the EU, but closely aligned

Switzerland is not an EU member and therefore runs its own VAT system, separate from the European framework, with its own national rate and its own exemption rules. An aircraft imported into Switzerland from the EU is in principle subject to Swiss import VAT, calculated on the customs value of the aircraft, barring specific exceptions. Conversely, an aircraft exported from Switzerland to the EU may be exempt from Swiss export VAT, but can then become liable for VAT in the destination country.

Geographic proximity and the bilateral agreements between Switzerland and the EU make aircraft movement and some customs steps easier, but they do not remove the need to clarify the applicable VAT for every cross-border move. The exact rate and any exemptions should always be confirmed with the Swiss tax authority or a specialist advisor — the precise figure varies with the nature of the operation and should never be assumed by analogy with another country.

The UK after Brexit: VAT and customs

Since leaving the European Union, the UK applies its own VAT and its own customs duties to movements with the EU, where these were previously purely intra-community. An aircraft moved from the EU to the UK, or the other way, is now treated as an import or export in customs terms, with the paperwork and taxation that follow from that.

  • Import into the UK — UK import VAT potentially due, calculated on the declared customs value.
  • Export from the UK to the EU — customs formalities are now mandatory and should be built into the transaction timeline.
  • Pre-Brexit customs status — an aircraft already based in the UK before withdrawal may have benefited from transitional status; this must be verified with documentation, never assumed.
Worth knowing — The exact treatment depends on the aircraft's historic customs status and the dates of earlier movements. Have the situation confirmed by a customs agent or tax advisor before any transaction, rather than relying on general rules.

Norway: outside the EU and outside the single market

Norway, a member of the European Economic Area but not of the European Union, follows its own VAT regime, separate from the EU's harmonised system. An aircraft imported into Norway from the EU is in principle subject to Norwegian import VAT, and a local sale between Norwegian residents follows purely national rules, with no automatic link to the EU regime.

EEA membership makes some regulatory and goods-movement aspects easier, but it does not create a tax union with the EU for VAT purposes: every cross-border aircraft movement to or from Norway must be examined as a transaction with a third country for VAT purposes, even though other areas of aviation regulation — EASA standards in particular — remain largely aligned.

Impact on where you buy or register

An aircraft's VAT treatment directly affects total acquisition cost, sometimes more significantly than the price gap between two comparable listings. Before comparing two offers located in different countries, factor in the potential tax cost and risk — not just the headline sale price.

  • Always request documentary proof of the aircraft's VAT status before negotiating.
  • Plan ahead for the final registration country: it can trigger tax obligations separate from the country of purchase.
  • Budget for a specialist tax advisor as soon as a transaction involves a non-EU country.

Cross-border purchase pitfalls

The most common mistake is to assume that an aircraft that is "in order" in its current country will automatically remain so once imported or re-registered elsewhere. A second classic pitfall is confusing aviation certification rules — often harmonised through EASA, including with some non-EU countries — with VAT rules, which are not. Finally, negotiating a price without first costing the potential VAT impact can turn an apparent bargain into a more expensive deal once the paperwork is settled.

Caution — Never treat a VAT rate or exemption as a given by analogy with another country or a previous deal: every transaction must be confirmed case by case with a tax advisor qualified in the relevant country.
