EFIS retrofit on an older aircraft: feasibility and budget

Replacing the analogue instruments of an older aircraft with a modern EFIS screen: what is actually possible, the options available and the budget to plan for.

Why retrofit an analogue cockpit

An older aircraft fitted with needle instruments can still fly perfectly safely, but the comfort and cockpit workload are nothing like those of an aircraft equipped with an EFIS (Electronic Flight Instrument System). A modern screen combines attitude indicator, altimeter, vertical speed indicator and airspeed indicator into a single display, with visual alerts that are far easier to read than a row of mechanical gauges.

Beyond comfort, a retrofit often solves a very practical problem: the failure or obsolescence of an old mechanical instrument, for which spare parts are becoming scarce and expensive. Replacing an attitude gyro at the end of its life with a solid-state EFIS can, over time, prove more cost-effective than repeated one-off repairs.

Technical feasibility: STCs, approvals and compatibility

The main sticking point is rarely technical — it's administrative. An instrument can only be installed on a certified aircraft if an approval exists that precisely covers that aircraft type, usually in the form of an STC (Supplemental Type Certificate) or an equivalent approval issued for the European market. EFIS manufacturers publish applicability lists by model and registration that must be checked before starting any project.

  • Check that an STC or equivalent approval exists that precisely covers your aircraft type and engine variant.
  • Confirm electrical compatibility: power supply, data bus and interfacing with existing equipment (transponder, autopilot, GPS).
  • Plan for an updated weight and balance sheet after installation.
  • Check whether an existing autopilot also needs replacing to remain compatible with the new EFIS.
Word of caution — An experimental aircraft (amateur-built) is not bound by these STC constraints and allows for much greater freedom of choice than a certified aircraft. This difference is an important factor when choosing between an older certified model and an experimental one.

Retrofit options available

Three retrofit levels coexist on the market, with very different budgets and installation constraints.

Partial replacement (standby instrument or simple EFIS)

This means adding a basic EFIS screen, sometimes certified as a standby instrument only, while keeping some or all of the original analogue instruments. This option limits wiring work and cost but only brings a partial improvement in comfort.

Replacing the PFD (Primary Flight Display)

The PFD replaces the attitude indicator, altimeter, vertical speed indicator and airspeed indicator with a single screen. This is the most common retrofit on older light aircraft, as it radically transforms cockpit ergonomics for a still-reasonable budget.

Full retrofit (PFD + MFD + digital engine display)

A full retrofit adds a multi-function display (MFD) for navigation and a digital engine instrument system (EIS), replacing the mechanical gauges for oil pressure, temperature and fuel pressure. This is the most expensive option, but the one that brings an older aircraft closest to a modern glass cockpit.

Between these three levels, the choice mainly depends on how the aircraft is actually used and on the available budget. An owner who flies occasionally and locally may be satisfied with a simple PFD, while a pilot flying long distances or regularly under IFR will see a clearer benefit from a full retrofit, thanks in particular to the GPS, traffic and weather integration usually offered on recent MFDs.

Real budget: hardware, installation and paperwork

The budget for an EFIS retrofit always breaks down into three items: hardware, installation labour and administrative costs linked to the approval. The figures below give a starting point for discussion, to be refined with an approved avionics shop based on your aircraft type.

Retrofit levelHardwareLabour and certification
Standby instrument / simple EFISA few hundred to around €3,000A few hundred euros
PFD onlyAround €5,000 to €12,000Several hundred to a few thousand euros
Full retrofit PFD + MFD + EISAround €15,000 to €30,000 or moreSeveral thousand euros, plus a compatible autopilot if needed

On top of these amounts, you almost always need to factor in the aircraft being grounded during the work, an updated flight manual and, where relevant, the replacement of ancillary sensors (temperature probe, antenna, pressure sensor) needed for the new equipment to work correctly.

Impact on resale value

A retrofitted cockpit generally improves the appeal of a listing and can make resale easier, particularly to buyers looking for a "ready to fly" aircraft with no further work needed. That said, you should stay realistic: the resale premium rarely covers the entire budget invested, especially on a model with limited demand or that is otherwise ageing (airframe, engine).

A retrofit is therefore primarily an investment in comfort and reliability for personal use, and an additional selling point rather than an operation you can expect to fully recoup financially.

Pitfalls to avoid before getting started

Several mistakes come up repeatedly among owners who start a retrofit without enough preparation.

  • Signing a quote before confirming the STC's exact applicability to your precise registration.
  • Underestimating the cost of an autopilot that becomes incompatible with the new EFIS.
  • Overlooking avionics shop availability and lead times, which can be long for certain equipment.
  • Forgetting to budget for updating airworthiness documents and the flight manual after installation.
Tip — Always ask your Part-145 / Part-CAO shop for a detailed line-by-line quote, and compare at least two providers before committing to a full retrofit.

Finally, remember to check with your insurer whether installing an EFIS affects your premium or the terms of your aircraft's coverage. Some insurers require training or an adaptation period on the new equipment before fully covering flights made with the retrofitted cockpit, particularly if the autopilot or navigation functions have also changed.

If you are considering buying an aircraft that already has an EFIS retrofit rather than carrying out the work yourself, always ask for the complete installation file: a copy of the applicable STC, the return-to-service form, the updated flight manual and a detailed invoice. A poorly documented retrofit can complicate a future resale, or even call the aircraft's compliance into question if the approval used did not exactly match your variant.
