What is the PZL-Bielsko SZD Jantar

The SZD Jantar is a family of single-seat gliders designed in Poland during the 1970s by the SZD design office (Szybowcowy Zakład Doświadczalny), later brought under the industrial group PZL-Bielsko. Built entirely from glass-fibre/epoxy composite materials, these gliders stood out for their flight performance at the time and helped build the international reputation of Polish glider manufacturing, including in competition.

The name "Jantar" means amber in Polish, a reference to the colour of the resin used in the early airframes. Several hundred examples were produced across the whole family, and a meaningful number remain airworthy today, particularly in Poland, Germany and the United Kingdom, where a used market for this type of glider stays active within the gliding community.

The main variants: Standard, Jantar 2, Jantar 3

Before buying, it is essential to identify precisely which variant is being offered for sale, since the differences in class and performance between them are significant.

  • Jantar Standard and Jantar Standard 2/3 — Standard Class gliders with a 15-metre span, designed to comply with the class limits for competition. The Standard 2 introduced increased water ballast capacity over the original model, and the Standard 3 simplified cockpit access with a one-piece canopy.
  • Jantar 2 — an Open Class glider with a span of over 20 metres, intended for top-level competition, featuring camber-changing flaps and a cruciform tail.
  • Jantar 3 — a further development of the Open line, with additional aerodynamic refinements compared to the Jantar 2.

The Standard Class variants, more common and simpler to maintain, are generally better suited to club use or as a first performance glider. The Open Class variants, with their greater span, are better suited to experienced pilots looking for higher flight performance, at the cost of more demanding ground handling and transport.

The used market in Europe

The used market for the SZD Jantar remains a niche within the broader market for older composite gliders. Supply is concentrated mainly in Central and Northern Europe, within gliding clubs and among private owners who enjoy performance gliders. Listings are generally fewer than for more recent Standard Class gliders, which can extend the search time needed to find an example that precisely matches your requirements.

The relative scarcity of some variants, particularly the Open Class versions, often makes it worth widening the search to several countries and staying in touch with gliding clubs that specialise in vintage gliders, which frequently have a good network of information on available examples.

What budget to plan for

The price of a used Jantar depends above all on the variant concerned, the overall condition of the airframe, its storage history (hangared or kept outdoors) and the quality of the equipment fitted (flight instruments, parachute, and a road trailer where applicable). Well-maintained Standard Class examples generally trade at an accessible entry level compared with other, more recent performance gliders, while rarer, well-maintained Open Class variants can represent a notably higher investment.

Beyond the purchase price, your budget should also cover related costs: a pre-purchase inspection, registration transfer, insurance, and possibly an upgrade of the flight instruments or the rescue parachute if their validity date has lapsed.

What to check before buying

As with any ageing composite glider, a pre-purchase inspection carried out by a qualified independent mechanic familiar with the type is essential before finalising a deal. For a Jantar, a few points deserve particular attention.

  • Condition of the composite structure: look for impacts, delamination, moisture traces or prior repairs, particularly around the spars and wing joints, which are difficult to detect without a specialised inspection.
  • Operation of the retractable monowheel landing gear and its disc brake, as well as the gear extension control.
  • Integrity and operation of the water ballast system housed in the wings, including the fill and drain valves.
  • Condition of the canopy and its opening mechanism, especially on older examples where the plexiglass can yellow or crack.
  • A complete, traceable logbook, with no gaps in the aircraft's history or in any past structural repairs.
  • Validity of the certificate of airworthiness (CofA) and compliance with any airworthiness directives applicable to the type, to be checked with the competent authority of the country of registration.
Good to know — Never rely solely on the seller's statements about the overall condition of an older composite glider: a written independent inspection report, including spot checks of the structure where possible, remains the best protection against unpleasant surprises after purchase.

Flight characteristics and pilot profile

The Jantar was originally designed for competition and retains demanding handling qualities, with a high glide ratio and a relatively contained stall speed for a performance glider. Variants fitted with water ballast offer a range of in-flight weight adjustment, allowing the aircraft's penetration to be matched to the day's weather conditions — a common practice among competition pilots, but one that requires some experience to use to full effect.

A pilot with little experience on this type will benefit from a transition course with an instructor familiar with the aircraft, particularly for managing the retractable gear and the in-flight water ballast, two features not found on the most common training gliders.

Maintenance, parts and logbook

Keeping a Jantar maintained relies mainly on the periodic inspections required by the maintenance programme applicable to the type and by the authority of the country of registration, as well as compliance with any airworthiness directives published since the aircraft entered service. The availability of certain specific parts can be more limited than for more recent production gliders, which is a good reason to turn to workshops that specialise in Polish composite gliders, or to club networks with good experience of the type.

A complete logbook documenting every intervention and structural repair since the aircraft entered service is a decisive factor for resale value as well as for buyer peace of mind. Conversely, an incomplete technical file or undocumented repairs should prompt extra caution before committing to a purchase.

Tip — Ask the seller for the complete history of any structural repairs and the name of the workshop that carried them out: this information often makes it possible to verify the quality of the work by contacting the workshop directly.

Who this glider suits

The SZD Jantar is aimed primarily at pilots who already have glider experience and are looking for a performance aircraft at a generally more accessible acquisition cost than an equivalent modern glider, and who are ready to accept the maintenance constraints and parts availability typical of an older composite aircraft. The Standard Class variants offer a good balance between performance and ease of operation for regular club use or private ownership.

The Open Class variants, rarer and more demanding to handle on the ground, are better suited to pilots focused on competition or cross-country flying, who are willing to invest the time needed to find a well-maintained example and to organise its transport. In every case, taking the time to compare several listings and arranging a thorough pre-purchase inspection remains the best guarantee of a successful purchase on this type of performance glider.
