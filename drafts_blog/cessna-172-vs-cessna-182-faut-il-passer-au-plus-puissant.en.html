Cessna 172 vs Cessna 182: is it worth going for more power

The Cessna 172 Skyhawk and the Cessna 182 Skylane share the same silhouette, but not the same budget or the same mission. Here is how to choose between them based on your real use.

Two Cessna best-sellers, two missions

The Cessna 172 Skyhawk and the Cessna 182 Skylane are, along with the Cessna 150/152, among the most-produced aircraft in the world. They share a similar overall architecture — fixed-gear single-engine, high wing, four-seat cabin — but serve different needs. The 172 is the archetypal trainer: economical and easy to operate. The 182, more powerful and better engined, appeals to pilots who need more payload, better high-altitude performance or more capacity for family travel.

The "172 or 182" question comes up often among owners considering stepping up from the first to the second: is it a justified, natural progression, or extra cost that doesn't match a real need?

The Cessna 172: the training benchmark

The Cessna 172 has established itself as the archetypal trainer and light touring aircraft, thanks to its operating simplicity, good spare parts availability and a very large used fleet across all generations. Its engine, more modest than the 182's, translates into lower fuel burn and generally lower maintenance costs.

On the used market, the 172 remains the most accessible choice for a first aircraft ownership purchase, club use, or regular personal use on short to medium trips with a limited crew and baggage load.

The Cessna 182: more power, more payload

The Cessna 182 Skylane shares the same general architecture as the 172, but with a noticeably more powerful engine and a reinforced structure to support a higher maximum take-off weight. This extra power translates into better payload, better take-off performance from short or high-altitude runways, and a higher cruise speed.

The 182 appeals to owners who regularly fly with four people and baggage, who operate from high-altitude or short-field airports, or who want more comfort and performance for longer trips, in exchange for a higher purchase and operating budget.

Budget comparison: purchase and operation

In practice, the choice between the two models often comes down to a budget trade-off. The 172 remains cheaper to buy for a comparable year and equipment, burns less fuel per hour, and generally carries a lower insurance premium. The 182, with a more powerful engine to maintain and higher fuel burn, means a higher annual budget, but in return offers greater payload and performance.

As with any used aircraft comparison, the real cost per flight hour depends heavily on the aircraft's age, the remaining engine potential before the next major overhaul, and the intended frequency of use.

Performance and payload in detail

The difference in engine power between the two models explains most of the performance gap. The 182 has noticeably more power than the 172, which directly translates into a higher maximum take-off weight and therefore a significantly higher available payload once the tanks are full.

  • Cessna 172: payload is often tight with full fuel and four occupants, so a weight and balance calculation is always required.
  • Cessna 182: payload is generally sufficient for four occupants and baggage with full fuel, a decisive advantage for family travel.
  • Cessna 182: better take-off performance from short runways or at altitude, an important factor for mountain flying.
  • Cessna 172: more modest cruise speed, but more than adequate for training and short to medium trips.

For a pilot who mostly flies solo or with one passenger on short trips, the 182's payload advantage often has little practical day-to-day value.

Which aircraft for which use

The choice between the 172 and 182 should first be based on the intended real use rather than a desire to "move up". For a pilot who flies mainly within a club, who is just starting out in aircraft ownership, or who flies short trips with little baggage, the 172 offers an excellent balance between controlled cost and flying enjoyment.

  • Cessna 172: ideal for training, a first aircraft ownership purchase, and regular club or personal use on a controlled budget.
  • Cessna 182: relevant for family travel, high-altitude or short-field airports, and pilots who value a performance margin.
  • Budget available for annual maintenance and fuel: often more decisive than the initial purchase price.
  • Frequency of flying with four occupants and baggage: a key factor in justifying the step up to the 182.

Conversely, an owner who regularly flies at full capacity, who operates from demanding airports, or who wants more comfort for longer trips will find in the 182 an investment justified by real use rather than a simple upgrade for its own sake.

Buying either one used: points to watch

Whether it's a 172 or a 182, the used market covers a very wide range of generations and engine variants, with significant price differences depending on age, engine hours and installed avionics. It's essential to compare listings of similar years and configurations to assess a fair price.

Good to know — Always check engine hours since the last major overhaul (SMOH), the consistency of the logbook, and have a pre-purchase inspection carried out by an independent mechanic familiar with the type, whichever model you choose.

For the 182 in particular, carefully review the engine history and any STC modifications (power upgrades, avionics), which can meaningfully affect the aircraft's real value and performance.
