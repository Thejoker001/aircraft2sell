Buying a used aircraft in Germany or Poland: which country to choose

Market, prices, tax, registration and maintenance: a comparison between Germany and Poland to help you decide where to search for your used aircraft.

Two neighbouring markets, two different aviation histories

Germany and Poland are two neighbouring Central European countries with an active light aviation fleet, but fairly different historical trajectories. Germany has a long-standing general aviation tradition and a dense network of airfields and maintenance workshops. Poland, for its part, has seen a more recent development of its leisure aviation market since joining the European Union, with a supply that has become much more structured over the past two decades.

A German buyer can perfectly well find an interesting aircraft in Poland, and vice versa, provided they understand the specifics of each market before entering into a cross-border transaction.

Size and activity of the used market

Germany has a very large fleet of light aircraft, supported by a dense network of flying clubs and widely practised leisure and touring flying, including trips to neighbouring countries. This translates into an abundant supply on the used market, particularly for the most common single-engine piston aircraft and microlights.

Poland also has a substantial and growing fleet of light aircraft, with a particularly dynamic supply of microlights and entry-level single-engine aircraft, often offered at slightly more accessible price levels than in Western Europe. Poland's network of flying clubs has developed strongly in recent years, especially around major cities.

Comparing the prices charged

For the same model and the same maintenance condition, prices observed in Poland are sometimes slightly lower than those seen in Germany, particularly for entry-level aircraft and microlights, although the gap tends to narrow for the most sought-after aircraft (recent avionics, high engine potential). In all cases, the actual condition of the aircraft and the quality of its maintenance weigh far more heavily on the final price than the single country where it happens to be located.

In both cases, comparing several listings of similar models remains the best way to assess a fair price before committing, regardless of which country the aircraft you want is located in.

The language barrier can play a meaningful role in negotiation: a German-speaking buyer will negotiate more easily in Germany, while a technical file written only in Polish may need translating before a thorough pre-purchase inspection.

Tax and purchase-related costs

The tax treatment applicable to the purchase of a used aircraft depends above all on the buyer's status (private individual or professional), the country of tax residence and the country in which the aircraft will be registered after the transaction, more than on the single country where the aircraft happens to be at the time of purchase. For a cross-border transaction between Germany and Poland, it is worth checking precisely any intra-EU VAT formalities applicable between professionals, as well as the ownership-transfer costs specific to each country.

Using a professional familiar with cross-border transactions (broker, specialised lawyer) helps avoid procedural mistakes that can prove costly, particularly regarding VAT or customs documentation when the aircraft changes country of registration.

Registration and administrative steps

An aircraft bought in Poland can be registered in Germany, and vice versa, depending on the buyer's choice: keeping the registration of the purchase country is not mandatory. This change, however, involves specific administrative steps with the civil aviation authority of the destination country, including submitting the complete technical file, checking compliance of the Certificate of Airworthiness (CofA) and, where applicable, a compliance inspection.

  • Check the consistency between the logbook, the technical file and the aircraft's actual condition before any change of registration.
  • Plan for administrative lead times, which can vary significantly between the two civil aviation authorities.
  • Check any requirements specific to the destination country regarding onboard equipment.
  • Budget for transport or ferry-flight costs if an inspection or a compliance fix is needed before the transfer.
Good to know — Common EASA rules govern transfers between EU member states and make them simpler than an import from outside the Union. Expect the process to generally take several weeks depending on how complete the submitted file is.

Maintenance network and parts availability

Germany has a particularly dense and well-established network of approved maintenance workshops for most common models, with generally good parts availability thanks to the size of the market. Poland has also developed a network of competent workshops, notably around its most active flying clubs, although availability of certain specific parts can occasionally require a longer lead time or an order from Western Europe.

For rarer models or specific engines, it can be useful to check in advance the availability of parts and the proximity of a specialised workshop before finalising the purchase, regardless of the aircraft's country of origin.

The distance between the two countries remains reasonable for a ferry flight or road transport of the aircraft if needed, which makes cross-border transactions straightforward without major logistical constraints.

Which country to favour depending on your profile

For a buyer working with a tight budget, particularly for a microlight or an entry-level single-engine aircraft, widening the search to Poland can give access to slightly more accessible prices, provided a thorough pre-purchase inspection is carried out and, if needed, the technical file is translated. For a buyer who above all wants a very well-documented maintenance record and an extremely dense network of nearby workshops, the German market often remains the most comfortable choice.

Good to know — In all cases, a pre-purchase inspection by a qualified independent mechanic remains essential, whether the aircraft is located in Germany, Poland or elsewhere in Europe.

Ultimately, the country of purchase matters less than the actual condition of the aircraft and the quality of its maintenance record. Comparing listings from both markets widens your options and gives you a stronger negotiating position, whether on price or on the terms of the transaction.
