Understanding the remaining time before overhaul (TBO) shown in a listing

TBO, remaining time before overhaul, SMOH: how to read these figures in a used aircraft listing and what they really mean for a buyer.

What is TBO (Time Between Overhaul)?

TBO, short for Time Between Overhaul, is the number of operating hours a manufacturer publishes between two major engine overhauls for a given engine model. It is expressed in flight hours and, depending on the manufacturer, sometimes also in a calendar limit (a number of years). It's one of the first figures a buyer looks for in a used aircraft listing, because it directly shapes the maintenance budget to expect in the months or years after purchase.

In a listing you'll often see a line such as "TBO 2000h, 650h remaining" or "70% life remaining before overhaul." That number is, in theory, how many hours are left to fly before the engine reaches the limit the manufacturer has set for a major overhaul.

How to read "remaining time before overhaul" in a listing

Remaining time is a simple subtraction: the engine's TBO minus the hours accumulated since the last major overhaul (or since new, if the engine has never been overhauled). An engine with a 2000-hour TBO showing 1350 hours since its last overhaul has 650 hours of remaining life as advertised.

The catch is that this calculation only holds if the hour meter and the "since overhaul" figure recorded in the engine logbook are accurate and current. A listing that quotes a flattering remaining-life figure without stating the date of the last overhaul, or without a matching engine logbook, should be treated with caution. The number is only as good as the paperwork behind it.

  • TBO is specific to the engine model, not the airframe type — two aircraft of the same model can carry engines with different TBOs depending on the installed powerplant.
  • A listed remaining-life figure must always be cross-checked against the date (or hours) of the last overhaul recorded in the engine logbook.
  • Some manufacturers also publish a calendar TBO (e.g. 12 years) in addition to the hourly one: whichever limit is reached first generally applies.

SMOH, TTAF, TSN: the abbreviations you need to know

Used aircraft listings are full of abbreviations tied to remaining engine life. Mixing them up can completely distort your reading of the real remaining time.

  • SMOH (Since Major Overhaul) — flight hours accumulated since the engine's last major overhaul. This is the figure to compare directly against TBO to get the remaining life.
  • TTAF / TSN (Total Time Airframe / Time Since New) — total hours on the airframe or engine since manufacture, regardless of any intermediate overhaul.
  • Engine TTSN — total hours since new on the engine itself; useful when an engine has never been overhauled, in which case TTSN and SMOH are identical.
  • Engine logbook — the authoritative record where all these hours must be entered and cross-checked; it, not the listing text, is the source of truth.

An engine shown as "450 SMOH" on a 2000-hour TBO has, in theory, 1550 hours of remaining life. The same engine listed as just "450h" without an SMOH / TTSN distinction may actually already have been overhauled once or more — or never overhauled at all. That nuance completely changes how the aircraft should be valued.

Is TBO a legal requirement or a recommendation?

This is one of the most misunderstood points for buyers. The TBO published by an engine manufacturer originates as a manufacturer recommendation based on its reliability statistics, not a hard physical limit at which the engine will simply stop running. A well-maintained engine, with regular oil analysis and no notable incident history, can keep flying beyond its published TBO.

That said, whether respecting TBO is mandatory depends heavily on the regulatory framework that applies to the specific aircraft and its use: private non-commercial operation, flight school, rental, commercial transport, country of registration. In some contexts, strict TBO compliance can be made mandatory through the aircraft's approved maintenance programme or by the operator. This point must always be confirmed with an approved maintenance organisation (Part-145 / Part-CAO) or the airworthiness manager — never assumed from the wording of a listing alone.

Key takeaway — Don't confuse "manufacturer recommendation" with "universal legal requirement": the applicable framework varies by use and by country. Have the exact regime for the specific aircraft you're interested in confirmed by a professional before basing a decision on an assumption.

Is an engine past its TBO automatically a deal-breaker?

No, but the paper trail needs to be exemplary. An engine "past TBO" is not automatically disqualifying: what matters is the quality of the monitoring it has received. Regular spectrometric oil analysis, cylinder compressions measured at consistent intervals, and frequent, regular use of the aircraft (an engine that flies rarely but in short bursts often ages worse than one used regularly) are more reliable indicators than the hour meter alone.

Conversely, an engine shown well under its TBO but that has flown very little over long stretches, or that shows signs of internal corrosion, can represent a higher risk than one close to its TBO but meticulously maintained. The remaining-life figure in a listing is a starting point for asking the right questions, not a conclusion.

How to verify the TBO shown is accurate

Before taking a listed remaining-life figure at face value, always ask for:

  • A copy of the complete engine logbook, not just the last page, to check that the hours are consistent from one entry to the next.
  • The date and report of the last major overhaul, including the name of the approved shop that performed it.
  • The history of oil analysis, if available, to catch abnormal wear that the hour meter alone wouldn't reveal.
  • Consistency between the airframe logbook hours and the engine logbook hours — an unexplained gap is a warning sign.
  • The opinion of an independent approved mechanic during a pre-purchase inspection, who can measure compressions and assess the engine's real condition beyond the stated figures.

How remaining engine life affects price and negotiation

Remaining engine life is one of the most concrete negotiation levers in a used aircraft purchase, because it translates directly into future cost. An aircraft close to its TBO means the buyer needs to factor in the likely cost of an upcoming overhaul, which can represent a significant share of the aircraft's value depending on the engine type.

Two common approaches: negotiate a price reduction reflecting the estimated cost of the upcoming overhaul, proportional to the hours already consumed against TBO; or require the seller to have the overhaul completed before the sale, with the price adjusted accordingly. Neither is universally better — it depends on the asking price, the aircraft's overall condition, and your own cash position to fund an overhaul after purchase.

Word of caution — Never trust a quoted "remaining life" percentage without knowing both the engine's exact TBO AND its precise SMOH. Two engines "at 70% remaining life" can represent completely different budgets depending on their underlying TBO.

Pitfalls to avoid around a listed TBO

Several phrasings deserve particular scrutiny when reading a listing:

  • "Engine in good condition" with no hours stated — always ask for the exact SMOH and the TBO applicable to that engine model.
  • Remaining life calculated against the wrong TBO — some sellers apply, by mistake or by optimism, an extended TBO that doesn't actually apply to their exact engine configuration; verify the manufacturer's official TBO for the exact installed engine reference.
  • A partial repair presented as a full overhaul — replacing cylinders or overhauling magnetos is not a complete major overhaul; only a documented overhaul resets the SMOH clock to zero.
  • Incomplete or discontinuous engine logbook — any undocumented period should be raised directly with the seller before moving forward with the transaction.

In short, the remaining time before overhaul shown in a listing is a useful indicator but never sufficient on its own. It should always be cross-checked against the engine logbook, confirmed through an independent pre-purchase inspection, and factored into your negotiation with a clear understanding of the real cost of a future overhaul.
