Finding a Part-66/Part-145 approved aircraft mechanic

Understand the Part-66 and Part-145 approvals, identify a competent and independent provider, and know what to negotiate before entrusting the maintenance or inspection of your aircraft.

Why an approved mechanic matters

Whether it's a pre-purchase inspection, routine maintenance or a major overhaul, entrusting your aircraft to a professional holding the right approvals is not a mere administrative formality: it guarantees that work is carried out under recognised procedures, that parts used are traceable, and that the maintenance logbook stays valid for insurance purposes and for a future resale.

Maintenance carried out outside the proper approvals, even by a competent individual, can complicate a future resale or create a coverage problem in the event of a claim. It is far better to check approvals before signing a quote, not after.

Part-66: the mechanic's licence

The Part-66 licence is the European framework that individually qualifies an aircraft mechanic (often called "certifying staff") to sign off an aircraft's return to service after maintenance or a repair. It specifies the categories of aircraft and systems the holder is authorised to certify, and must be kept current through continuing training.

Asking to see a mechanic's Part-66 licence before any work is not excessive distrust — it is a standard check that any serious professional will readily accept.

Part-145: the workshop approval

The Part-145 approval concerns the maintenance organisation (the workshop or company) rather than the individual. A Part-145-approved workshop has documented quality procedures, a traceability system for parts and work performed, and supervision of its mechanics by the relevant civil aviation authority. For lighter aircraft or operations outside a strictly commercial framework, a CAO (Combined Airworthiness Organisation) approval may also apply depending on the country and type of operation.

Before entrusting an aircraft to a workshop, check that its approval actually covers your aircraft's type and category: a Part-145 approval is not automatically valid for every aircraft type — it covers a defined scope (aircraft classes, specific types) listed in the approval certificate.

Where to look for an approved provider

Several sources can help you identify an approved mechanic or workshop in your area:

  • Local flying clubs and aviation associations, which regularly work with trusted workshops to maintain their fleet.
  • Lists of approved workshops published or available from the civil aviation authority of the country of registration.
  • Manufacturers and their networks of approved service centres for a given aircraft type, particularly useful for a newer model still under warranty or active manufacturer support.
  • Recommendations from other owners of the same aircraft type, through type-specific forums and owner associations.
  • Independent inspectors listed by platforms such as Aircraft2Sell, particularly useful for a one-off pre-purchase inspection.

What to check before handing over the aircraft

Beyond simply confirming an approval exists, a few practical checks help avoid unpleasant surprises:

  • Does the exact scope of the approval cover your aircraft type and the nature of the planned work (routine maintenance, major overhaul, structural repair)?
  • Does the workshop have recent, documented experience on this specific type, not just a general aircraft category?
  • Does the quote itemise labour, parts and any subcontracted work, rather than an opaque lump sum?
  • Are the quoted timelines realistic given parts availability, particularly for an older or less common aircraft?
Word of caution — An approval displayed on a website or a sign at the workshop's entrance should be verifiable with the authority that issued it. If in doubt, ask for a copy of the approval certificate and its exact scope.

Staying independent from the seller

For a pre-purchase inspection in particular, the choice of mechanic must remain entirely yours. A provider recommended by the seller may well be competent, but their independence of judgement is harder to guarantee — especially if they have an ongoing commercial relationship with that seller. Always choose your own mechanic, ideally a workshop that has never worked on this particular aircraft before.

The same logic applies to routine maintenance once you own the aircraft: occasionally switching provider for a second opinion on a costly or uncertain diagnosis is a sound practice, not a sign of distrust toward your regular mechanic.

Budgeting maintenance and major overhauls

Maintenance and overhaul costs vary considerably depending on aircraft type, age, use (private or commercial) and parts availability. There is no single figure that applies across the board: a recent light single-engine aircraft on routine maintenance represents a very different budget than an older twin requiring an engine or airframe major overhaul.

Rather than looking for one precise number, think in terms of the broad cost categories to plan for: the workshop's labour (usually billed hourly), parts cost (which varies with the rarity of the model), subcontracting fees for specialised work (engine overhaul, propeller, avionics), and a margin for unexpected findings once the aircraft is opened up. Always ask for an itemised quote before approving major work, and compare several approved workshops for a costly major overhaul.

Tip — Before buying an aircraft, ask for the full history of past major overhauls: invoices and reports give a realistic picture of the maintenance budget to expect on that specific model — far more reliable than a general average for the aircraft's category.

Building a lasting relationship with your mechanic

Beyond a one-off job, working consistently with the same approved mechanic or workshop has a real advantage: they know the aircraft's history, more easily spot an anomaly compared to its previous condition, and can anticipate upcoming maintenance deadlines rather than discovering them at the last minute.

This continuity also helps a future resale: a maintenance logbook tracked by a single approved workshop, consistent and well documented over time, reassures a potential buyer far more than a history fragmented across several unrelated providers.
