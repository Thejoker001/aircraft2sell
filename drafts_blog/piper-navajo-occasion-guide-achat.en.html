Why the Piper Navajo still appeals to twin-engine owners

The Piper Navajo (PA-31 family) is a piston twin originally designed for light passenger and cargo transport, before becoming a classic of business aviation and air taxi operations for several decades. Its cabin, wide for its category, its payload capacity and its structural toughness made it a favourite with professional operators, then with private owners once commercial fleets moved on to other types.

On the used market today, the Navajo attracts experienced pilots looking for a twin capable of carrying several passengers and baggage over long distances, at an acquisition cost that is often more accessible than an equivalent-capacity turboprop. This versatility has a flip side: the aircraft demands disciplined operation and maintenance skills specific to the airframe and twin-engine powerplant.

The main variants: Navajo, Navajo C/R, Chieftain, Mojave

The PA-31 family went through several successive upgrades between its launch in the late 1960s and the end of production in the 1980s. The original Navajo was followed by the Navajo C and C/R, then by the Chieftain (PA-31-350), a lengthened and more powerful version widely used in commercial operations. The Mojave, derived from a shorter fuselage, targets more performance-minded private owners.

Before targeting a specific variant, it is worth defining your actual mission: occasional family transport, regular business trips, or more intensive operation. The choice of variant directly shapes both the acquisition budget and the operating costs ahead.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used Navajo varies significantly with the variant, the age of the airframe, total hours and the engine status of each of the two engines. An older aircraft with reduced remaining potential on one or both engines trades at a noticeably more accessible entry level than a recently overhauled example with modernised avionics.

On top of the purchase price come costs that are often underestimated: an independent mechanic's pre-purchase inspection on the type, registration and transfer fees, insurance for a twin of this category, and a reserve for a possible engine overhaul in the short to medium term if remaining potential is limited.

What to check before buying

A twin of this generation calls for a particularly thorough pre-purchase inspection, carried out by a shop familiar with the type. Several points deserve closer attention than on a light single.

  • Complete history of both engines: hours since the last overhaul, consistency between the two powerplants, traceability of replaced parts.
  • Condition of the structure and engine mounts, particularly around the nacelles and areas exposed to vibration and corrosion.
  • Operation and condition of the landing gear and hydraulic systems, which are heavily stressed by the aircraft's weight and use.
  • Condition and updates of the avionics, as well as compliance with current regulatory equipment requirements.
  • Complete logbooks and technical file for both engines and the airframe, with no grey areas in the operating history.
Good to know — On a twin of this age, always request a separate inspection report for each engine: a consistent history across both powerplants is a sign of good fleet management by previous owners.

The twin-engine operating angle: rating and safety

Flying a Navajo requires a multi-engine rating as well as specific familiarisation on the type, particularly for handling an engine failure in flight and flying at maximum weight. This additional skill represents a training cost to factor into the purchase project if you are not already rated on piston twins.

On the safety side, the redundancy offered by the second engine is an argument owners of light twins often make, but it comes with increased complexity of management in the event of a failure, which justifies regular training and serious currency maintenance, beyond the minimum regulatory requirements alone.

Maintenance and operating costs

Maintaining a Navajo reflects its complexity as a twin: two engines to track in parallel, hydraulic and electrical systems more extensive than a light single, and a larger airframe to inspect. The main expense items remain the mandatory annual inspection, insurance, parking suited to the aircraft's size and, in the longer term, overhauling both engines.

Having the aircraft maintained at an approved shop familiar with the PA-31 family helps limit unpleasant surprises and preserve resale value. Parts availability generally remains reasonable for a type that was widely produced in its time, but some specific components can involve longer lead times than on a high-volume single.

Tip — Ask for the complete maintenance logbooks of both engines over several years: significant gaps between the two powerplants can signal uneven upkeep or a past engine replacement that was poorly documented.

Resale and the used market

The used Navajo market remains a niche market in Europe, supported by a base of piston-twin enthusiasts and a few light transport operators. A well-maintained aircraft, with a transparent history on both engines and up-to-date avionics, generally resells more easily than an example with a poorly documented commercial operating past.

Comparing several listings of similar variants remains the best way to assess a fair price, keeping in mind that the value gap between two Navajos of the same model year is almost always explained by the remaining potential on each engine and by the quality of documented technical follow-up.
