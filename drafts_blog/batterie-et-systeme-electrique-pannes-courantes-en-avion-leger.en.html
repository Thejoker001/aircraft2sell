Battery and electrical system: common faults in light aircraft

Weak battery, failing alternator, a circuit breaker that pops in flight: an overview of the most common electrical faults in light aircraft, their symptoms and how to stay ahead of them.

Why the electrical system deserves close attention

On a modern light aircraft, the electrical system powers far more than cabin lighting: navigation and communication avionics, the transponder, electric flight instruments, flaps, and sometimes even the auxiliary fuel pump. An electrical failure generally doesn't prevent an aircraft from flying — a classic light-aircraft piston engine doesn't need electrical power to keep running once started — but it can strip the pilot of a large part of their instruments and communication means, which seriously complicates the rest of the flight, particularly in reduced visibility or controlled airspace.

It's precisely because an electrical failure rarely puts the aircraft in immediate danger, yet severely degrades the pilot's situation, that it ranks among the most feared failures in training and deserves to be well understood before it ever occurs.

The battery: the system's most fragile link

The battery serves two essential functions: supplying the energy needed to start the engine, and acting as a backup in case of an alternator failure in flight. On most piston-engine light aircraft it's lead-acid, or increasingly lithium-iron-phosphate on newer or retrofitted aircraft, the latter offering lower weight and better charge retention.

An ageing battery gives warning signs that shouldn't be ignored: increasingly sluggish engine starts, voltage that drops quickly under load, reduced endurance during a simulated alternator failure. Ageing is accelerated by deep charge/discharge cycles, long periods of inactivity without a trickle charger, and cold temperatures, which significantly reduce the usable capacity of a lead-acid battery.

  • Resting and on-load voltage measured at every periodic inspection and compared with expected values for the battery type.
  • Electrolyte level checked on unsealed, serviceable lead-acid batteries.
  • Manufacture date and years in service: a lead-acid battery rarely stays reliably in aviation service for many years.
  • No signs of corrosion or leakage around the terminals and battery box.

The alternator and voltage regulator: common failures

The alternator (or generator on older aircraft) recharges the battery and powers all electrical equipment while the engine is running. Its failure is one of the most frequent causes of an in-flight battery drain: the drive belt can loosen or break, the voltage regulator can drift and over- or under-charge the circuit, and internal brushes or bearings eventually wear out with flight hours.

A failing regulator is particularly deceptive: it can let voltage climb above normal, gradually damaging the battery and sensitive avionics equipment, without any warning light coming on immediately if the ammeter or voltmeter isn't checked regularly by the pilot in cruise.

Breakers, fuses and wiring: the circuit's weak points

Beyond the power source itself, it's often the electrical distribution that causes trouble on older aircraft. A circuit breaker that repeatedly trips on the same circuit almost always signals an intermittent short circuit or a genuine overload, and should never simply be reset in flight without the cause being investigated on the ground afterwards.

Ageing wiring, particularly on aircraft several decades old that have never had their wiring harness fully overhauled, can have insulation weakened by engine heat, vibration and repeated thermal cycling. Poorly crimped or corroded connectors, often located in hard-to-reach areas such as the engine bay or under the floor, are a frequent cause of intermittent faults that are difficult to reproduce on the ground, making diagnosis particularly time-consuming for a shop.

Caution — A circuit breaker that repeatedly trips must be investigated by a mechanic before the next flight, never simply reset "to see if it holds".

Recognising an electrical failure in flight

Signs of an ongoing electrical failure are usually progressive rather than sudden, except in the case of an outright short circuit. A continuous drop in indicated voltage, an ammeter that stays negative with the engine stabilised in cruise, or a noticeable dimming of cabin lighting and radio power are all signals worth watching for.

  • Check the voltmeter/ammeter at regular intervals in cruise, not just at takeoff.
  • Shed non-essential equipment at the first signs of a drain to preserve the battery's remaining endurance.
  • Remember the engine keeps running normally: an electrical failure is not a life-threatening emergency but it does require prioritising the equipment that remains available.
  • Plan for a transition to degraded navigation and communication if the failure is confirmed, informing air traffic control as early as possible while the radio still works.

Preventive maintenance and good practice

Most in-flight electrical failures originate from a defect that was detectable on the ground well before it showed up at altitude. Regularly checking alternator belt tension, periodically cleaning and re-tightening battery terminals, and measuring actual battery capacity at periodic inspections all help catch degradation before an outright failure.

For aircraft that sit idle for long periods, a suitable trickle charger avoids the deep discharges that drastically shorten a lead-acid battery's life. On aircraft fitted with newer lithium batteries, it's essential to strictly follow the charging and monitoring procedures specific to that technology, which differ from traditional lead-acid practice.

Tip — Ask your shop to include an actual capacity test, not just a visual check, at every periodic inspection: it's the only reliable way to catch a battery nearing end of life before it fails in flight.

What to check when buying a used aircraft

During a used aircraft's pre-purchase inspection, the electrical system deserves at least as much scrutiny as the engine or airframe, since defects there are often cumulative and hard to spot without a targeted diagnosis. Always ask for the history of battery, alternator and regulator replacements recorded in the logbook, along with the date of the most recent capacity tests.

An aircraft that has gone through several alternators or regulators in a short number of flight hours may point to an underlying wiring or charging problem that was never properly diagnosed, rather than simple normal part wear. Conversely, a clear history with well-spaced, documented replacements is a good indicator of the overall health of the electrical system on the aircraft you're considering buying.
