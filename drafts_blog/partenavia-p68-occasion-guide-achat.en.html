The Partenavia P68: a versatile light twin

The Partenavia P68 is a light Italian twin-engine aircraft designed in the 1970s, kept in production under several successive names over the decades and still actively flown across Europe today. Its high-wing, wing-mounted engine layout, robust metal structure and roomy cabin for its category have made it a popular choice both for training and for light transport or utility missions.

On the used market, the P68 stands out for an attractive balance between running cost and payload among light twins, with a structure that's relatively simple to maintain and good parts availability thanks to the type's decades-long production run.

P68 versions and engine options

The P68 has gone through several evolutions over its production life: base versions with fixed landing gear, retractable-gear variants, versions optimised for aerial observation or surveillance with a distinctive glazed nose, and stretched-cabin versions offering extra passenger capacity. The piston engines fitted have also evolved across generations, with different power ratings and propellers depending on the version.

The glazed-nose versions, in particular, were built for very specific aerial surveillance, photography or survey missions, and are typically equipped accordingly: dedicated observer stations, specific fuselage openings, or sensor mounts. An aircraft modified this way doesn't serve the same purpose — and usually doesn't carry the same resale value — as a version built strictly for passenger transport. When reviewing a listing, it's therefore essential to pin down exactly which version and generation the aircraft belongs to, since commercial naming has sometimes shifted over the years without the underlying technical configuration changing fundamentally.

This variety of versions means you need to pin down, before you even start searching, the exact configuration you're after: seating capacity, landing gear type, avionics fit and any specific modifications (observation, mapping) — all of which can significantly change how the aircraft is used and how it's valued on the used market.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used P68 depends above all on the aircraft's age, total airframe hours and the hours on each of the two engines, the status of each powerplant (remaining potential before overhaul), and the level of avionics fitted. As with any twin, the cost of maintaining two engines and two propellers weighs more heavily on the overall budget than an equivalent single-engine aircraft — a factor to build into your search from the start.

Beyond the purchase price, your budget should include ancillary costs: an independent mechanic's pre-purchase inspection, registration and transfer fees, insurance (generally higher than for a comparable single-engine aircraft) and, if needed, an avionics or cabin interior refresh.

What to check before buying

As with any used aircraft, a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic qualified on the type is essential before finalising a transaction. For a twin like the P68, a few points deserve particular attention.

  • Complete history of each of the two engines separately: hours elapsed since the last overhaul, and consistency between the two powerplants.
  • Condition of the metal structure: looking for corrosion, particularly around the wing spars and engine mounts.
  • Operation and history of both propellers, and their compliance with the airworthiness directives applicable to the type.
  • Landing gear, especially on retractable-gear versions: actuator wear and correct operation of the retraction system.
  • Complete logbook and technical file for both engines, with no grey areas in the aircraft's history.
Good to know — Never rely solely on the seller's statements about overall condition: a written independent inspection report, covering each engine separately, remains the best protection against a bad surprise after the purchase.

Maintenance and running costs of a light twin

Operating a twin like the P68 inherently means a higher maintenance cost than an equivalent single-engine aircraft, since every overhaul, every propeller replacement and every engine intervention must generally be planned for both powerplants. In exchange, the engine redundancy that a twin-engine layout provides is a valuable safety argument for certain missions, particularly flights over mountains or areas difficult to reach in the event of a failure.

As with any light twin, you should also budget for periodic maintenance items specific to the shared structure and systems between the two engines: wiring, engine controls and duplicated fuel lines all need rigorous follow-up to keep the whole system reliable. An approved workshop familiar with the type will know how to anticipate these items and avoid unexpected aircraft downtime.

Having the aircraft maintained at an approved shop familiar with the type helps limit unpleasant surprises and preserve its resale value. Rigorous, well-documented maintenance records for both engines are a strong argument at a future resale.

Tip — Always ask for the latest maintenance invoices for each of the two engines before making an offer: they give an accurate picture of how seriously the aircraft has been maintained.

Typical uses: training, light transport, utility work

The P68 is flown in a wide range of missions across Europe: multi-engine pilot training for schools that want to offer an accessible twin rating, light regional transport for small groups of passengers, and specific utility missions thanks to versions equipped for aerial observation or mapping.

  • Multi-engine training: a frequent choice for flight schools thanks to its relatively simple operation for a twin.
  • Light regional transport: a cabin capacity that's attractive for its category.
  • Utility missions (observation, mapping): relevant on versions equipped accordingly.
  • Personal use for a pilot seeking the engine redundancy of a twin on a controlled budget.

Before buying, it's therefore worth clearly defining the intended main mission, since the various P68 versions are not all equally suited to every use. An aircraft configured for aerial observation, for example, won't necessarily be the most comfortable or economical choice for regular passenger transport, and conversely a standard cabin version won't suit a surveillance mission requiring specific equipment.

Resale and the used market

The P68 benefits from a sizeable installed fleet across Europe, particularly among flight schools and operators of specific missions, which supports steady demand on the used market. A well-maintained aircraft, with a transparent history on both engines and up-to-date avionics, generally resells more easily than one whose technical file has grey areas.

Comparing several listings of similar versions and model years remains the best way to assess a fair price, both for buying and for preparing a future resale. Price differences between comparable aircraft are almost always explained by the remaining potential on each of the two engines and the quality of the avionics fitted.
