Buying a used aircraft in Switzerland or the UK: which country to choose

Market, prices, FOCA or CAA registration, VAT and customs formalities: a comparison between Switzerland and the UK to help you decide where to search for your used aircraft.

Two non-EU countries, two different regimes

Switzerland and the United Kingdom have in common that neither is an EU member state, yet their regulatory situation in light aviation differs considerably. Switzerland has signed a bilateral agreement with the European Union that keeps it integrated into the European aviation safety system overseen by EASA, with a federal civil aviation office (FOCA) that applies the shared European rules. The UK, by contrast, left the EASA system after Brexit and now has its own authority, the Civil Aviation Authority (CAA), with certification rules that have started to diverge from EASA's.

For a buyer based in the EU, this difference matters: a purchase in Switzerland sits within a regulatory framework largely harmonised with EASA, while a purchase in the UK means dealing with a technical framework that has become distinct since 2021.

Size and activity of the used market

Switzerland has a modestly sized light aircraft fleet relative to its area, but generally a high standard of equipment and maintenance, supported by an active network of flying clubs and a mountain-flying tradition (mountain flying, gliding, utility helicopters). Supply on the used market there is smaller than in France or Germany, but the aircraft on offer are often well maintained.

The UK has a considerably larger fleet of light aircraft, with a highly developed general aviation culture, many private airfields and a dynamic used market, particularly for touring aircraft and some types less common on the continent. Overall supply is therefore broader in the UK than in Switzerland across all categories.

Comparing prices and currencies

Price levels vary mainly according to an aircraft's maintenance condition and equipment, more than according to the country itself. Switzerland, a country with a high cost of living, often shows higher maintenance and parking costs, which can be reflected in the sale price of very well-maintained aircraft. The UK, with a larger market, generally offers a wider range of choice across different price levels.

Registration: FOCA in Switzerland, the CAA in the UK

An aircraft registered in Switzerland falls under the EASA system, with an HB- prefix, and a Certificate of Airworthiness issued and overseen by the Federal Office of Civil Aviation (FOCA). Thanks to the bilateral agreement between Switzerland and the European Union, transferring registration between Switzerland and an EU EASA country remains relatively close to a standard intra-EASA transfer, even though Switzerland is not an EU member.

An aircraft registered in the UK now falls exclusively under the UK CAA, with a G- prefix, and has its own Certificate of Airworthiness (CofA) procedures and its own maintenance rules, which have progressively diverged from EASA's since Brexit. Moving an aircraft from the UK register to an EASA country therefore requires a fuller file than a transfer between two countries that remained in the EASA system, as is the case for Switzerland.

  • Check which regime (EASA or UK CAA) the aircraft is currently certified under before considering a change of country of registration.
  • For a Swiss aircraft, expect lead times generally close to a standard intra-EASA transfer.
  • For a UK aircraft, plan for a longer timeline and a technical compliance check against the destination EASA framework.
  • Seek advice from a Continuing Airworthiness Management Organisation (CAMO) familiar with Swiss or UK files, as applicable.

VAT and customs formalities for a European buyer

Both Switzerland and the UK sit outside the EU's customs and tax territory, which means a purchase from either country by an EU-based buyer is not treated as an intra-EU exchange, but as an import. The applicable VAT treatment must be checked case by case depending on the status of the buyer and seller, and customs formalities should be expected both when crossing the Swiss border and when crossing the Channel.

Point of caution — Always have your file reviewed by a professional familiar with aircraft imports (broker or specialised aviation lawyer) before committing to a purchase in Switzerland or the UK, to secure the VAT treatment and any applicable customs duties.

In practice, Switzerland, thanks to its bilateral agreements and its geographic and regulatory proximity to the EU, often involves slightly simpler formalities than a post-Brexit UK purchase, but neither situation should be underestimated when budgeting for a cross-border transaction.

Maintenance network and parts availability

Both countries have a network of approved maintenance workshops for the most common models. In Switzerland, workshops remain certified Part-145 / Part-CAO under EASA oversight via FOCA, which makes continuity of maintenance with an EU country easier. In the UK, workshops now operate under the UK's own equivalent of these approvals, defined and overseen by the CAA, distinct from the EASA framework.

For rarer models or specific engines, parts availability may vary slightly between the two markets, as some suppliers have adjusted their logistics between the EU, Switzerland and the UK since Brexit. It is advisable to check in advance how easily parts can be sourced for the aircraft type under consideration, regardless of the country of purchase.

Operating conditions: airfields, terrain and weather

Switzerland stands out for its mountainous terrain, which strongly shapes operating conditions: airfield altitude, local weather phenomena linked to the relief, and specific mountain-flying practices for certain aircraft types and activities (gliding, helicopters). The UK, by contrast, has generally more moderate terrain but an oceanic climate marked by changeable weather and dense air traffic around major urban areas.

A buyer whose flying plans are oriented towards mountains or terrain flying will find Switzerland a particularly suitable environment, while a classic touring or leisure flying project will benefit more from the density of airfields and the diversity of the UK fleet.

Which country to favour depending on your profile

For a buyer who wants to stay within a regulatory framework close to that of the European Union, with EASA continuity made easier by Switzerland's bilateral agreement, Switzerland remains a particularly consistent option, albeit with a more limited market supply. For a buyer willing to accept a few extra steps linked to the CAA framework in exchange for a wider choice, the UK market can be worthwhile, provided the cost and time of formalities specific to a non-EASA purchase are factored in.

Good to know — In all cases, a pre-purchase inspection by a qualified independent mechanic remains essential, whether the aircraft is located in Switzerland, the UK or elsewhere in Europe.

Ultimately, the country of purchase matters less than the actual condition of the aircraft, the consistency of its technical file and correctly anticipating the customs and tax formalities specific to a purchase outside the European Union. Comparing listings from both markets widens your options, provided you budget correctly for the time and cost of a potential import or registration transfer.
