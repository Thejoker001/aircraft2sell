Why the Discus remains a Standard Class benchmark

The Schempp-Hirth Discus is a single-seat Standard Class glider (15-metre wingspan, no flaps) designed by the German manufacturer Schempp-Hirth Flugzeugbau. Since its launch it has become one of the most widely flown competition and cross-country gliders in this category, remaining a reference point on the used market decades after it first appeared.

Its popularity rests on a well-known balance between aerodynamic efficiency, handling and forgiveness of piloting mistakes, which makes it a favourite both with club pilots progressing toward competition and with experienced pilots looking for a versatile single-seater for cross-country and racing flights. Its large production run across Europe has also built up a solid base of pilots and mechanics familiar with the type, which makes maintenance and knowledge-sharing between owners easier.

On the used market, the Discus comes in several production years and successive versions, with gradual changes to the wing profile, cockpit and finish. This long production run explains a fairly wide supply across Europe, but also a broad price range depending on version, age, airframe hours and gelcoat condition.

The Discus is also known for predictable handling, including in weak thermal conditions, which makes it a well-regarded aircraft both for learning performance flying and for more relaxed club flying. This versatility, combined with good spare parts availability and a network of competent workshops for the type, explains why so many European pilots keep looking for this model on the used market.

Discus a, Discus b and Discus CS: what's different

The Discus a, the first version in the line, established the basics of the model with a fully composite airframe and a wing profile optimised for Standard Class. It is the oldest version available on the used market, generally with a more accessible entry price, but buyers also need to factor in the more advanced age of these airframes.

The Discus b succeeded the original model with changes mainly to the cockpit, ergonomics and some aerodynamic details, while keeping the aircraft's overall philosophy. It is the most widely represented version on the European used market, offering a good balance between availability, price and relatively modern fittings.

The Discus CS (Discus Club Sport) introduces a wing with winglets and a few further refinements aimed at improving efficiency further, while staying in the spirit of an accessible glider for club flying and regional competition. Discus CS examples on the resale market are generally newer and are priced accordingly higher than earlier versions.

Before targeting one version over another, it is worth clearly defining the intended use — occasional club flying, regular cross-country flying, or competition ambitions — since the gap in budget, airframe age and aerodynamic performance between versions is significant.

What budget should you plan for

The price of a used Discus depends mainly on the version (a, b or CS), the age and total airframe hours, the condition of the composite gelcoat, and the quality of the onboard instruments (electronic variometer, flight computer, GPS logger approved for competition). The oldest first-generation examples, with heavy club use, trade at a noticeably more accessible entry level than newer, well-equipped, low-hours CS examples owned by a private pilot.

Beyond the purchase price, your budget should also cover pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic qualified on type, registration and transfer fees, insurance, and a road trailer plus a hangar or tie-down spot depending on how you plan to operate the aircraft.

What to check before buying

As with any used glider, a pre-purchase inspection carried out by an independent mechanic qualified on type is essential before closing a deal. For a Discus, a few points deserve particular attention.

  • Composite gelcoat condition: look for impacts, delamination or previous repairs, particularly on the leading edges and forward fuselage, the areas most exposed to ground handling and trailer transport.
  • Total airframe hours and periodic inspection history (CofA/ARC), with a complete, chronologically consistent logbook.
  • Operation of the airbrakes, camber flaps (on versions fitted with them) and play in the flight controls, all repeatedly stressed in performance flying.
  • Condition of the retractable landing gear and its locking mechanism, a sensitive point on a performance single-seater.
  • Past storage conditions (hangar or trailer): an example regularly kept under cover holds up noticeably better in composite gelcoat condition over time.
  • Presence and condition of the road trailer, often sold with the aircraft and whose value and condition deserve a separate assessment.
Good to know — Never rely solely on the seller's description of overall condition: a written independent inspection report, ideally from a workshop approved for the type, remains the best protection against unpleasant surprises after purchase, especially for a performance single-seater that may have flown in competition.

Maintenance and running costs

The running cost of a Discus stays noticeably lower than that of a comparable powered aircraft, which explains part of its appeal to private pilots and clubs alike. The main cost items remain the periodic airworthiness inspection, insurance, storage (hangar or trailer) and routine maintenance of the gelcoat and flight controls.

Having the aircraft maintained by a workshop approved for the type and familiar with composite materials limits unpleasant surprises and preserves resale value. Rigorous, well-documented maintenance is a strong selling point at any future resale, all the more so for an aircraft that may have flown in regional competition, where several pilots can have taken turns at the controls over the seasons.

Insurance for a performance single-seat glider is generally more affordable than for a comparable powered aircraft, but the level of cover chosen should reflect intended use: regular competition or racing use often justifies broader cover than strictly local, occasional flying.

Tip — Always ask for the complete logbook and the latest periodic inspection reports before making an offer: they give a faithful picture of how seriously the aircraft has been maintained, especially for an example that may have flown in competition.

Resale and the used market

The Discus benefits from an active used market in Europe, backed by a very large installed base among glider pilots and good parts availability from the manufacturer's network and its approved workshops. A well-maintained aircraft, with a transparent history and gelcoat in good condition, generally resells more easily and on better terms than one with gaps in its technical file.

Comparing several listings for similar examples remains the best way to judge a fair price, whether you're buying now or preparing a future resale. Price differences between two examples of the same vintage almost always come down to airframe hours, gelcoat condition and documented maintenance quality, as well as the version (a, b or CS) involved.
