Two different approaches to a six-seat single-engine aircraft

The Cessna 182 Skylane and the Piper PA-32 Cherokee Six both sit in the six-seat single-engine segment, but with different design philosophies inherited from two historic manufacturers. The 182 carries the classic high-wing, conventional-gear architecture of the Cessna family, while the PA-32 adopts the low wing and tricycle gear typical of the Piper range, with a noticeably wider cabin designed for carrying passengers and baggage.

Before comparing price and performance, it's worth understanding that these two aircraft serve slightly different priorities: the 182 bets on versatility and ease of operation from rough strips, the PA-32 on cabin volume and raw payload capacity.

The Cessna 182 Skylane: high-wing versatility

The Cessna 182 Skylane has established itself as a widely used family single-engine aircraft thanks to its ruggedness, ease of handling and good performance from unprepared strips, inherited from its high landing gear and propeller well clear of the ground. Its cabin seats up to six occupants depending on configuration, with a payload that often forces a trade-off between full fuel tanks and the number of passengers on the earliest generations.

On the used market, the 182 comes in many generations, with changes in engine, landing gear (fixed or retractable on the RG version) and avionics that create a wide range of prices and equipment levels.

The Piper PA-32 Cherokee Six: volume first

The Piper PA-32 Cherokee Six was designed from the outset as a family and utility transport aircraft, with a noticeably wider cabin than the 182's and a dedicated rear baggage door that makes loading bulky items easier. This superior payload capacity makes it an appealing choice for larger families or missions that require carrying bulky equipment in addition to passengers.

The PA-32 was also produced in retractable-gear versions and more powerful variants over the years, which widens the used market on offer. Its low-wing, tricycle-gear configuration generally makes it easier to handle on the ground and at landing for a pilot used to this type of architecture, even if it leaves it somewhat less comfortable on rough strips than the 182.

Budget comparison: purchase and operation

For comparable equipment and generation, the purchase price of the two aircraft stays broadly similar, with the differences seen on the market mostly down to the age of the aircraft, remaining engine potential and the level of avionics fitted, rather than any structural difference in value between the two models. The PA-32, with its wider cabin and generally higher payload, can justify a slightly higher operating budget on certain items tied to the aircraft's weight.

Which aircraft for which use

The choice between the Cessna 182 and the Piper PA-32 should start above all with the intended mission and usual operating environment. For a pilot who regularly operates from unprepared strips or short grass runways, the 182 offers a clear advantage thanks to its ground clearance and sturdy landing gear.

  • Cessna 182: preferred for operations from rough strips and versatile club or personal use.
  • Piper PA-32: relevant for family transport with bulky baggage and use mostly from paved runways.
  • Need for maximum payload with full fuel: often a decisive factor in favour of the PA-32 depending on the generation considered.
  • Ease of accessing and loading bulky baggage through the PA-32's rear door.

Conversely, a pilot who operates almost exclusively from paved aerodromes and who values cabin volume and loading convenience will find in the PA-32 a comfort and practicality argument that offsets its architecture being less suited to rough strips.

Buying either one used: points to watch

For both models, engine history and traceability of hours elapsed since the last overhaul remain the top priorities during a pre-purchase inspection. On the Cessna 182, check the condition of the landing gear and, on retractable-gear versions (182RG), the proper operation of the retraction system. On the PA-32, attention should focus on the condition of the rear door structure and on consistency between the stated payload and the actual empty weight measured at the last weighing.

Good to know — Always have the pre-purchase inspection carried out by an independent mechanic familiar with the type, and ask for the complete logbook and maintenance invoices before committing.

In all cases, comparing several listings of similar models and model years remains the best way to assess a fair price and spot a properly maintained aircraft, whether a Cessna 182 or a Piper PA-32.

Availability and resale value in Europe

Both models benefit from a significant installed fleet in Europe, with generally satisfactory spare parts availability thanks to the popularity of these aircraft in the United States, their home market. The Cessna 182, however, benefits from a slightly wider network of shops familiar with the type on the continent, as the Cessna brand has historically been very well represented in European clubs and flight schools, which simplifies routine maintenance and parts sourcing for owners.

The Piper PA-32, while less common in club fleets, holds a stable resale value thanks to its reputation as a reliable family single-engine aircraft and the relative rarity of its large-cabin architecture in its price category. A well-maintained aircraft with a complete technical file generally resells without major difficulty in either case, with differences in time to sell mostly explained by the listed price and overall condition rather than by the model itself.
