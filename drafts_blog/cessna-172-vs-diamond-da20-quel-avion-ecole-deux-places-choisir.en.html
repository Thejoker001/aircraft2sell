Cessna 172 vs Diamond DA20: which two-seat trainer to choose

Two different philosophies for a trainer

The Cessna 172 and the Diamond DA20 are two of the most widely used aircraft for private pilot training anywhere in the world, yet they come from radically different design philosophies. The 172 is an all-metal, high-wing four-seater that first appeared in the 1950s and remains essentially unmatched in total number of units built. The DA20 is a composite, low-wing two-seater born in the 1990s, designed from the outset as a modern, light and economical trainer.

For a club, a flight school or a private owner looking for a two-seat training aircraft, the choice between the two comes down mainly to the intended mission: pure initial training with an instructor and a student, occasional family trips, available operating budget, and personal preference in terms of flying feel.

The Cessna 172: the unkillable classic

The Cessna 172 has stood as the undisputed benchmark for private pilot training for decades. Its sturdy metal structure, high wing giving good stability and excellent downward visibility, and the near-universal availability of parts and mechanics trained on the type make it a reassuring choice for any club or owner.

Although certified for four occupants, the 172 is very often flown with just two on board for instruction, which leaves a comfortable weight and balance margin and makes the aircraft particularly forgiving of a beginner's mistakes. Its versatility also lets it serve for occasional family trips once the licence is earned, something a strict two-seater cannot offer.

The Diamond DA20: modern, frugal composite

The Diamond DA20 was designed from day one as a modern trainer: a lightweight composite structure, a bubble canopy offering panoramic visibility, side-by-side seating and light control forces that give a sportier feel than the 172. Its reduced fuel burn and lighter airframe make it a particularly economical aircraft on a per-hour basis, a major selling point for flight schools logging large volumes of instructional hours.

Its strictly two-seat mission makes it a focused teaching tool: it is not meant for family travel, but for stacking training hours with a very favourable cost-to-fun ratio. Many professional pilot training pipelines actually use the DA20 as a first type before students move on to more complex aircraft.

Budget comparison: purchase and operation

At purchase, a used Cessna 172 spans a very wide price range depending on age, avionics and airframe/engine condition, supported by an abundant supply on the global market. The Diamond DA20, on average newer and built in far smaller numbers, trades in a generally narrower price band, with fewer aircraft on the market at any given time.

Beyond the sticker price, per-hour operating cost tends to favour the DA20 for schools logging a high volume of instructional hours, while the 172 remains the right choice when you want an aircraft that can serve both for training and for trips carrying more than two people.

Which one for which teaching style and club use

The pedagogical choice largely depends on the student's goal and the club's strategy. The 172, more stable and more forgiving, suits a gradual training progression where you want to limit a beginner's workload. The DA20, more responsive on the controls, gets a future pilot used earlier to a level of handling precision closer to what more demanding aircraft will later require.

  • Cessna 172: versatile training, ability to carry passengers after the licence, very broad parts and mechanic network.
  • Diamond DA20: lower per-hour cost, sharper handling feel, excellent visibility for learning the traffic pattern.
  • Planned volume of instructional hours: often a more decisive factor for a school than the purchase price.
  • Intended use after the licence: family travel or progression toward a professional track.

Many clubs actually choose to run both types side by side in their fleet: the DA20 for the first dual-instruction hours at lower cost, and the 172 for stages that require carrying an extra passenger or for getting the student used to an aircraft more representative of the rental fleet they will fly afterward.

Buying either one used: what to watch for

Whether it is a 172 or a DA20, a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic qualified on the type remains essential, all the more so since these aircraft are often flown intensively for instruction and accumulate flight hours and landing cycles at a high rate.

On a 172, pay close attention to corrosion of the metal structure, engine history and adherence to the maintenance programme, as well as to the avionics fit depending on the model year. On a DA20, a composite inspection is critical: look for impact damage, delamination or undocumented repairs, especially on an aircraft that has trained many beginner students.

Good to know — An aircraft flown intensively at a flight school typically shows a high number of hours relative to its age: ask for the breakdown of annual hours and the full logbook before making an offer.

Used market and resale

The Cessna 172 enjoys an extremely liquid global used market, backed by a huge fleet and steady demand from clubs and private owners alike. The Diamond DA20 has a smaller but active market, particularly among training organisations looking for an economical aircraft to operate.

In both cases, a transparent maintenance history and complete documentation remain the best arguments for a quick, favourable resale, whether the aircraft served in private use or in a flight-school fleet.
