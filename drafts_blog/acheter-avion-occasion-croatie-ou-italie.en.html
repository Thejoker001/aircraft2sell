Buying a Used Aircraft in Croatia or Italy: Which Country to Choose

Market, prices, taxation, registration and maintenance: a comparison between Croatia and Italy to help you decide where to look for your used aircraft.

Two Adriatic markets, two different profiles

Croatia and Italy face each other across the Adriatic, but their used-aircraft markets have fairly distinct profiles. Italy has an established, long-standing light aviation fleet, with regular listings concentrated mainly in the north of the country (Lombardy, Veneto, Piedmont), the historical heartland of Italian general aviation. Croatia, whose civil aviation market has developed significantly since joining the European Union in 2013, has a smaller but growing fleet, driven in part by coastal tourism along the Dalmatian coast and a rising number of active flying clubs.

A buyer based in Italy may well find an interesting aircraft in Croatia, and vice versa, since the geographic proximity makes ferrying between the two countries relatively straightforward. Understanding the administrative specifics of each market before embarking on a cross-border transaction remains essential.

Size and activity of the used-aircraft market

The Italian used-aircraft market remains significantly larger in volume than the Croatian one, with a varied offering ranging from microlights to certified touring single-engine aircraft, alongside a still-active domestic production on some light segments. The density of flying clubs and airfields in the north of the country makes it easier to compare several similar aircraft before deciding.

The Croatian used-aircraft market is smaller in absolute volume, concentrated mainly around Zagreb and the coastal airfields of Dalmatia, but it remains active and attracts a growing number of foreign buyers drawn by the flying environment along the coast. Listings there tend to lean towards light used single-engine aircraft and microlights, sometimes with longer selling times due to a smaller pool of local buyers compared to Italy.

Price comparison

For a comparable model and equivalent maintenance condition, price levels between the two markets remain broadly close, with observed differences owing more to local supply and demand at a given moment than to any structural gap between the two countries. An aircraft with a complete, consistent maintenance logbook sells at a similar level whether it is based in Italy or Croatia.

In both cases, comparing several listings of similar models remains the best way to assess a fair price before committing, regardless of which country the aircraft is located in.

Seasonality plays a notable role in Croatia, where part of the flying activity is concentrated around the summer tourist season, which can affect seller availability and the volume of listings published depending on the time of year. The Italian market, more regionally diversified, generally shows less pronounced seasonal variation.

Taxation and purchase-related costs

The taxation applicable to the purchase of a used aircraft depends above all on the buyer's status (private individual or professional), their country of tax residence, and the country in which the aircraft will be registered after the transaction, rather than simply the country where the aircraft happens to be at the time of purchase. In a cross-border transaction between Croatia and Italy, two European Union member states, it is advisable to find out precisely about any intra-EU VAT formalities applicable between professionals, as well as the ownership transfer fees specific to each country, which can vary from one country to another.

Using a professional experienced in cross-border transactions (broker, lawyer specialising in aviation law) helps avoid procedural mistakes that can prove costly, particularly regarding VAT or documentation when the aircraft changes country of registration.

Registration and administrative procedures

In Croatia, civil aircraft registration falls under the Croatian Civil Aviation Agency (Hrvatska agencija za civilno zračno prometstvo, CCAA), under the 9A- prefix. In Italy, it is ENAC (Ente Nazionale per l'Aviazione Civile) that holds the national register, under the I- prefix. An aircraft purchased in either country can be registered in the other, depending on the buyer's choice: there is no obligation to keep the registration of the country of purchase. This change involves specific procedures with the destination authority, including submitting the complete technical file, checking the validity of the certificate of airworthiness, and, where applicable, a compliance inspection.

  • Check the consistency between the maintenance logbook, the technical file and the actual condition of the aircraft before any registration change.
  • Plan for administrative timelines, which can vary significantly between the Croatian CCAA and the Italian ENAC depending on current workload.
  • Check for any specific requirements of the destination country regarding onboard equipment.
  • Budget for ferrying costs if an inspection or remedial work is required before the transfer.

Maintenance network and parts availability

Italy has a denser, more specialised network of approved maintenance workshops than Croatia, concentrated mainly in the north of the country, covering most common light-aviation models. Croatia has a smaller network, mainly present around Zagreb and the main coastal airfields, which can make finding a specialist workshop take longer for some less common models.

For rarer models or specific engines, it is worth checking in advance on parts availability and the proximity of a competent workshop before finalising the purchase, regardless of the aircraft's country of origin. The distance between the two countries remains modest from north-eastern Italy to the Croatian coast, which makes ferrying for maintenance between the two countries relatively straightforward when needed.

The language barrier can also come into play: an Italian buyer not fluent in Croatian will often favour an English-speaking workshop, and a Croatian buyer will reason the same way in reverse. In both cases, technical documentation in English is generally accepted by approved workshops in both countries.

Which country to choose based on your profile

For a buyer who prioritises a wide choice and a dense maintenance network close to their home airfield, the Italian market often remains the simpler option, particularly for a common model. For a buyer sensitive to the flying environment and willing to widen the search to a still-developing market, Croatia can offer interesting opportunities, especially for leisure use focused on the Adriatic coastline.

Good to know — In all cases, a pre-purchase inspection by a qualified independent mechanic remains essential, whether the aircraft is located in Croatia, Italy or elsewhere in Europe.

Climate and intended use can also guide the choice: a buyer mainly looking for a leisure aircraft to fly along the Dalmatian coast will naturally lean towards the Croatian market, while a buyer prioritising proximity to a wide choice of models and workshops will often stay loyal to the Italian market. The two approaches are not mutually exclusive: nothing prevents searching for an aircraft in Croatia while planning to have it maintained in Italy, or vice versa, provided ferrying distances remain reasonable.

Ultimately, the country of purchase matters less than the actual quality of the aircraft and how well it has been maintained. Comparing listings from both markets widens your options and gives you a better negotiating position, whether on price or on the terms of the transaction.
