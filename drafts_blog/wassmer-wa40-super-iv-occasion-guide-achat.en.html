The WA40 Super IV, a French club four-seater

The Wassmer WA40 Super IV belongs to the family of four-seat touring aircraft built by the Wassmer workshops in Issoire, during the decades when this French manufacturer held a significant place in European light aviation. A low-wing, fixed-gear four-seater, the WA40 Super IV equipped many French flying clubs before gradually shifting toward mostly private ownership, as the fleet thinned out and dispersed over the decades.

Today it is an aircraft found mainly in a niche market, sought after by enthusiasts of classic French aviation rather than by buyers looking for a first modern touring aircraft. Its appeal lies as much in its history as in how it flies, but this particularity calls for a different buying approach than for a more recent, more widely available model.

A wood-and-fabric airframe: what you need to know

Like most Wassmer aircraft of this generation, the WA40 Super IV is built around a structure that is mainly wood, with fabric covering on certain parts of the airframe. This construction, typical of French light aviation from the 1960s and 1970s, requires specific vigilance that doesn't apply to an all-metal or all-composite aircraft: moisture, rot, delamination of glued joints and ageing of the fabric covering are all points that must be examined carefully.

A buyer unfamiliar with this type of construction is well advised to bring in a mechanic specifically experienced with wood-and-fabric airframes, rather than a general shop used only to modern metal airframes. How well the airframe has held up depends largely on how the aircraft was stored over its life: an aircraft kept in a dry hangar through the winters will almost always have aged better than one left outside for long periods.

What budget to plan for a WA40 Super IV

The market for the WA40 Super IV remains narrow, with the number of airworthy examples noticeably smaller than for the most widely distributed touring models. This relative scarcity directly affects the budget needed: prices vary significantly based on the real condition of the airframe, the documented maintenance history and the engine's status, far more than on the year of manufacture alone.

Beyond the purchase price, your budget should include a specialised pre-purchase inspection, possible refurbishment of the fabric covering or parts of the structure, and the search for spare parts, which can take noticeable time and expense for such a rare model.

Pre-purchase inspection points to watch

The pre-purchase inspection of a WA40 Super IV cannot be a generic check: it must be carried out by a mechanic experienced with wood-and-fabric airframes, able to identify the warning signs specific to this type of construction.

  • Checking for moisture, mould or rot in the wooden parts, often invisible without partial disassembly.
  • Condition of the fabric covering: flexibility, absence of cracks or brittle areas, date of the last recover.
  • Quality and traceability of previous structural repairs, documented in the logbook.
  • Complete engine history, including extended periods of inactivity, often more common on an aircraft of this type.
  • Storage conditions over recent years: enclosed hangar versus outdoor parking.
Good to know — On a wood-and-fabric aircraft, a rushed pre-purchase inspection almost always costs more after the purchase than before: always favour a specialist familiar with the type, even if it means travelling further.

Engine, SMOH and routine maintenance

As with any light touring aircraft of this generation, engine status remains a central point of negotiation. The number of hours elapsed since the last major overhaul (SMOH) gives a direct indication of the remaining potential before the next major overhaul, and therefore of the budget to plan for in the medium term.

On an aircraft as rare as the WA40 Super IV, routine engine maintenance generally poses no particular difficulty if the powerplant is a common, well-documented model. However, it's worth checking with an approved shop on the real availability of parts specific to this installation, which can sometimes differ slightly from the standard parts for the same engine fitted to other aircraft types.

Rarity, spare parts and specialist mechanics

The main constraint of owning a WA40 Super IV lies less in flying it than in the long-term maintenance logistics. With a small fleet of airworthy examples remaining, the availability of parts specific to the airframe — fittings, wood structural components, flight control elements — can take longer to source than for a widely produced production model.

Before buying, it is advisable to reach out to owner associations or mechanics specialised in classic French aircraft, who generally know the workarounds and the shops familiar with this type of structure. This step, often overlooked, largely determines how easy the aircraft will be to maintain once acquired.

Which use and which pilot profile

The WA40 Super IV appeals above all to a pilot passionate about classic aviation, who accepts the specific maintenance constraints of a wood-and-fabric structure and a rare model, in exchange for the pleasure of flying and maintaining an aircraft with real character. It is generally not the most sensible choice for a first aircraft ownership purchase intended for intensive touring use.

However, for regular leisure flying, a modest range requirement and an attachment to French aviation heritage, the WA40 Super IV remains an appealing option, provided the owner accepts devoting dedicated time and budget to its upkeep.

Used market and resale

By nature, the resale market for the WA40 Super IV is a niche one: few listings at any given time, and a limited circle of buyers who are usually already aware of the model's specifics. An aircraft with rigorously documented maintenance and a properly maintained wood-and-fabric structure will stand out far more easily in this narrow market.

Comparing the few available listings, talking to classic aircraft owner associations and seeking several opinions from specialist mechanics before making an offer remains the best strategy for assessing a fair price on this kind of rare aircraft.
