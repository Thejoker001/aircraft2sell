Writing an effective aircraft sale listing: structure and keywords

A well-written listing cuts down on pointless back-and-forth, filters out time-wasters and speeds up the sale. Here is how to structure the text, what information to include, and which keywords to use so a serious buyer contacts you directly.

Why wording matters as much as price

Two listings for an identical used aircraft, priced the same, can produce very different results depending on how they are written. A vague, incomplete or badly structured listing pushes the buyer to ask dozens of questions by message, or simply move on to a competing listing that already answers everything.

The text of a listing plays three roles at once: it informs, it reassures, and it filters. It informs by giving the exact technical specifications of the aircraft. It reassures by showing the seller knows their aircraft and has nothing to hide. It filters by discouraging unserious buyers and attracting those whose budget and project genuinely match what you are selling.

A good listing is not measured by its length but by its completeness: every missing piece of information is one more question per message, and every extra question delays the purchase decision.

Writing a title that works

The title is the first thing a buyer sees in a list of search results. It should contain the information needed to filter quickly, without having to open the listing.

  • Exact make and model — not just "light aircraft", but the precise model as buyers actually search for it (e.g. "Cirrus SR22", not "fast single-engine aircraft").
  • Year of manufacture — a common search filter, worth placing prominently in the title.
  • Total hours or a strong selling point — low hours or a notable recent upgrade (avionics, paint) can appear in the title if it is a genuine advantage.
  • General location — country or region, a frequent search filter for buyers trying to limit ferry-flight costs.

Avoid titles that only highlight price or rely on vague superlatives like "amazing" or "must see": they offer no usable information and resemble titles from unserious listings.

The structure of a listing that converts

Organise the body of the listing in a logical progression, from general information to detail, so every type of reader quickly finds what they are looking for.

1. One or two-sentence summary

From the very first line, state the model, year, total hours and the overall condition in brief. A reader who only skims these two sentences should already know whether the aircraft matches their search.

2. Technical specifications

Present the technical data as a clear list rather than a wall of text: airframe, engine, propeller, total time (TTAF) and time since overhaul (SMOH), installed avionics, last overhaul or major overhaul performed.

3. History and maintenance

Describe how the aircraft was used (private, flight school, flying club), the number of previous owners, and how consistently it has been maintained. Mention whether the flight logbook and engine logbook are complete with no gaps.

4. Equipment and options

List the equipment that sets your aircraft apart from a base model: GPS, autopilot, mode S transponder, recent paint, refurbished interior.

5. Reason for selling and terms

A single sentence on why you are selling (upgrading, ceasing flying, buying a different type) humanises the listing and answers an implicit question from the buyer. Also specify the practical terms: where the aircraft is based, availability for an inspection or test flight, documents provided with the sale.

Information every buyer expects

Certain pieces of information are systematically requested if they are missing from the listing. Including them upfront avoids a useless back-and-forth of messages and signals that the seller is serious.

  • Total airframe and engine hours, hours since overhaul (SMOH).
  • Validity of the certificate of airworthiness (CofA) and date of the last airworthiness review.
  • Status of the maintenance logbook — complete, continuous, available for review.
  • Current country of registration and status of the aircraft (imported, compliant).
  • Asking price, and whether it is negotiable or firm.
  • Where the aircraft is based and availability for a viewing.

Choosing the right keywords

Beyond readability for a human, the words used in a listing directly affect its visibility in platforms' internal search engines and in Google searches. Consistently use the terms buyers actually type, not approximate synonyms.

  • "used aircraft" — the dominant search term in English; use it rather than rarer phrasing like "pre-owned airplane".
  • The exact model name and variant — a buyer searching "Cessna 182 Skylane" will not find a listing that only writes "Cessna".
  • "engine hours" and "SMOH" — both phrasings are searched for; including both increases visibility.
  • "certificate of airworthiness" — the official term used by buyers who care about regulatory compliance.
  • Country or region name — many buyers filter geographically, so an explicit location keyword in the text helps you appear in those searches.
Tip — Naturally repeat the model name two or three times across the text (title, summary, technical specs) rather than once: this strengthens the listing's relevance without making it repetitive for a human reader.

The tone to use, and the one to avoid

Use a factual, precise tone, closer to a technical data sheet than a sales pitch. Buyers of used aircraft are usually pilots or enthusiasts who know the market and instinctively distrust overly salesy writing.

Avoid multiple exclamation marks, all-caps for emphasis, and unsupported adjectives ("immaculate condition" with no further detail). Favour verifiable statements instead: "repainted in 2024", "engine overhaul performed 80 hours ago", "Garmin avionics installed in 2023".

Being honest about an aircraft's weak points (normal wear, outdated equipment not yet upgraded) paradoxically strengthens the credibility of the whole listing, rather than weakening it.

Mistakes that sink a listing

  • Listing too short — a bare "Cessna 172 for sale, contact me" generates a flood of basic questions and discourages serious, time-pressed buyers.
  • Unexplained jargon — a first-time buyer may not know every abbreviation; defining "SMOH (hours since major overhaul)" once removes any ambiguity.
  • No price listed — "price on request" discourages a large share of buyers who filter by budget before even contacting the seller.
  • Information contradicting the photos or documents — any inconsistency between the text and the supporting documents instantly undermines trust.
  • Text copied from another listing — generic, visibly recycled text gives the impression the seller does not really know their own aircraft.

Checklist before you publish

Before putting your listing online, reread it as if you were a buyer who knows nothing about the aircraft's history.

  • Do the model, year and total hours appear within the first two lines?
  • Is the price clearly stated, with its currency?
  • Is the status of the airworthiness certificate and maintenance logbook specified?
  • Are the equipment items that set the aircraft apart listed?
  • Is the listing accompanied by at least eight to fifteen quality photos?
  • Are the contact details and location up to date?
Word of caution — An incomplete listing published too quickly can be fixed afterwards, but it may already have discouraged the most serious early visitors. Take the time to finalise it before publishing rather than filling it in gradually.
