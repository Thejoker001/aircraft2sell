Aircraft sale contract: clause by clause

Party and aircraft identification, price, inspection, warranties, transfer of ownership, termination, disputes: the clauses a used-aircraft sale contract should never skip.

Why a written contract is essential

Selling an aircraft, even a light one between private individuals, involves significant sums and obligations that can extend well beyond the signature date. A simple email exchange or a handwritten invoice is not enough to protect either the buyer or the seller if a dispute arises over the aircraft's condition, missed deadlines, or an unpaid balance.

A contract drafted clause by clause captures everything negotiated verbally in writing, spreads responsibilities fairly between the parties, and gives both sides a solid basis if a disagreement surfaces after delivery. It complements, without replacing, the administrative transfer-of-ownership paperwork required by the aircraft's registry authority.

This guide walks through the clauses found in nearly every serious used-aircraft sale contract, whether the aircraft is a light single, a helicopter, or a turboprop.

Identifying the parties and the aircraft

The first clause looks obvious, yet it is where errors that later complicate the registration transfer most often creep in. It should unambiguously identify:

  • The parties — full name and address, and for a company its legal form and registration number, together with the name and capacity of whoever signs on its behalf.
  • The aircraft — manufacturer, model, serial number, current registration, and year of manufacture.
  • The engine and propeller — make, model and serial number of each unit, to avoid confusion if a major component is later replaced.
  • Included equipment — avionics, covers, tools, spare parts, and technical documentation delivered with the aircraft.

A precise description prevents a disagreement over "what was actually included in the sale" from souring an otherwise cordial relationship between the parties.

The price and payment terms clause

This clause states the total agreed price, the currency, and the details of any deposit already paid. It should also cover:

  • The payment schedule: deposit at signing, balance at delivery or after a satisfactory inspection.
  • The accepted payment method and bank details, avoiding any cash payment for a significant amount.
  • Who bears ancillary costs: ferry flight, inspection, registration paperwork fees, and any applicable taxes.
  • What happens to the deposit if the sale does not go through for a reason attributable to either party.

It is advisable to release the balance of the price only once the transfer documents are complete and the agreed inspection has not revealed any undisclosed major defect.

The conformity and pre-purchase inspection clause

This clause often determines whether the buyer's commitment stands at all. It should provide for:

  • The buyer's right to have an independent mechanic of their choice carry out a pre-purchase inspection, within a defined timeframe and location.
  • The possibility to withdraw or renegotiate the price if the inspection reveals defects not disclosed by the seller.
  • How inspection costs are split, usually borne by the buyer unless otherwise agreed.
  • The seller's commitment to provide full access to the flight logbook, engine logbook and maintenance history before the inspection.
Good to know — What a thorough inspection should cover (documents, airframe, engine, test flight) is detailed in our dedicated guide to pre-purchase aircraft inspection.

The warranties and exclusions clause

Most used-aircraft sales between private individuals happen "as is", meaning there is no warranty of proper functioning beyond what the seller has expressly stated. The contract should make this clear to avoid any ambiguity:

  • A statement by the seller confirming the absence of known hidden defects and the accuracy of the flight and engine logbooks.
  • An explicit "as is" clause if no warranty of functioning is granted after delivery.
  • No warranty on items of normal wear and tear, unless otherwise agreed.
  • What happens to any remaining manufacturer or engine-maker warranty, and the conditions under which it transfers.

A deliberate misrepresentation by the seller about the aircraft's condition or history remains grounds for recourse by the buyer, regardless of how this clause is worded.

The transfer of ownership and risk clause

This clause fixes the exact moment when ownership of the aircraft, and the risk attached to it (damage, loss, destruction), passes from seller to buyer. That moment is not always full payment: it can be brought forward or pushed back depending on what the parties negotiate.

  • The precise date and place of the transfer of ownership, generally at actual delivery or upon full payment of the price.
  • Who bears the risk during the ferry flight to the aircraft's new base, and which insurance covers that leg.
  • The seller's obligation to keep their insurance active until the actual transfer, and the buyer's obligation to have their own cover in force from that moment.
  • The coordinated handover of the paperwork needed for the registration change with the relevant authority.

The exact administrative formalities for a registration transfer vary by country and registry; they are detailed in our guide on the mandatory documents for selling an aircraft.

The termination and withdrawal clause

Any sale can fall through after a preliminary agreement is signed, most often because of an unfavourable inspection, the buyer's financing falling through, or an inability to register the aircraft. The contract should anticipate these scenarios:

  • The conditions precedent under which the buyer can withdraw without penalty: unfavourable inspection, financing refusal, or the seller lacking clear title.
  • Whether the deposit is refunded depending on the reason for withdrawal.
  • The consequences of a payment or delivery delay attributable to either party.
  • A possible penalty clause for withdrawal without legitimate grounds.
Word of caution — A contract with no exit route in case of an unfavourable inspection leaves the buyer in a weak position: always insist on a condition precedent tied to the outcome of the pre-purchase inspection.

The governing law and dispute resolution clause

For a cross-border sale, common in Europe's light aviation market, it is essential to specify in advance which national law governs the contract and which court, or amicable resolution process, will apply if a disagreement cannot be resolved.

  • The governing national law, chosen by mutual agreement between the parties.
  • The competent court, or a required attempt at mediation or arbitration before any litigation.
  • The reference language of the contract if it is translated for one of the parties.
  • How official notices between the parties must be sent (a valid email or postal address for all formal communication).

These details, often tucked at the end of the document, prevent months of deadlock if a disagreement arises after the sale while buyer and seller live in two different countries.

Tip — Always have the contract reviewed by a legal professional familiar with aviation transactions before signing, especially for a cross-border sale or a high-value aircraft.
