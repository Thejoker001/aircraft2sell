War Risk and Hijacking Insurance: Aircraft Exposed to High-Risk Zones

When a war risk and hijacking endorsement is required, what it actually covers, what it costs, and how to secure it if your aircraft flies into or is based in a sensitive region.

What war risk and hijacking insurance actually is

War risk and hijacking insurance is a specialist endorsement, separate from standard aviation hull and liability cover. It protects the aircraft owner or operator against damage to the airframe, or financial loss, resulting from acts of war, insurrection, terrorism, hijacking, or confiscation by a civil or military authority in a country where the aircraft happens to be located or over which it is flying.

This endorsement isn't reserved for airlines flying scheduled routes through sensitive corridors. A private owner, a business aviation operator, or a co-ownership structure can face the same exposure the moment the aircraft flies into, stops over in, or is based in a region where political, military, or malicious risk is assessed as material by underwriters.

Why standard hull coverage isn't enough

A standard aircraft insurance policy generally covers accidental damage to the hull and third-party liability arising from normal operation of the aircraft. It is built around "ordinary" risks: mechanical failure, pilot error, weather, ground handling incidents.

War, terrorism, and hijacking follow a different logic for an underwriter: these are potentially catastrophic, geographically correlated risks that are much harder to pool than ordinary accidental claims. That is why almost every standard policy explicitly excludes these events unless a specific endorsement has been purchased.

The usual exclusions in "all risks" policies

Before assuming coverage is complete, every owner or operator should carefully read the exclusion clause of their contract. Wording varies between insurers, but it generally covers the same categories of event.

  • War and armed conflict — declared or not, between states or within a single state.
  • Terrorism — sabotage or malicious acts with a political, religious, or ideological motive.
  • Hijacking and unlawful seizure — the aircraft taken over by force or threat.
  • Confiscation and requisition — by a government or civil/military authority, in rare cases even the state of registration.
  • Riots and civil commotion — sometimes bundled with, sometimes excluded alongside, war risks.
Warning — Without a specific endorsement, an aircraft damaged or lost during a conflict, a hijacking, or a government seizure simply won't be indemnified under a standard policy, regardless of the insured value of the aircraft.

What counts as a high-risk zone to an underwriter

Aviation insurers rely on regularly updated lists of high-risk zones, built from geopolitical and security assessments. These lists shift with current events: a region considered stable can move onto a watch list within weeks if the political or military situation deteriorates.

An aircraft doesn't need to be permanently based in such a zone to be affected. A single transit flight, a technical stopover, or an extended overflight of airspace classified as high-risk can be enough to trigger the exclusion in a standard policy, even for a short, one-off trip.

That's why operators flying international missions, business aviation companies operating outside Western Europe, and owners planning to import or ferry an aircraft through certain regions should always check, before the flight, whether the planned routing crosses a zone excluded under their current contract.

What a war risk and hijacking endorsement covers

Once purchased, this endorsement reinstates the events normally excluded: damage to, or total loss of, the aircraft caused by an act of war, terrorism, hijacking, confiscation, or requisition by an authority. Some policies also add extended third-party liability cover for damage caused to others in a war or hijacking context.

Each contract still needs to be read carefully: indemnity limits, deductibles, and residual exclusions (such as military use of the aircraft or direct participation in a conflict) vary significantly between insurers.

The cost of this coverage and what drives it

The cost of a war risk and hijacking endorsement depends first on the geographic zone involved and the duration of exposure: a single overflight generally costs far less than a permanent base in a region rated as high risk. The type of aircraft, its insured value, and its use (private, business, cargo) also factor in.

This cost is never fixed for the life of the contract: an insurer can re-rate the premium, or withdraw the endorsement altogether, if the security situation in a zone deteriorates sharply. This is one of the least intuitive aspects of this type of coverage for an owner used to standard policies, whose terms remain stable for the duration of the contract.

Good to know — Cancellation clauses on these endorsements are often far shorter than on a standard policy, sometimes as little as a few days' notice if risk in a given zone escalates.

How to secure this coverage for your aircraft

The first step is to have a specialist aviation insurance broker assess your actual exposure precisely: zones routinely overflown, usual stopovers, home base, and the nature of the missions flown. This assessment determines whether a war risk and hijacking endorsement is needed, and at what level of cover.

  • Have a precise record drawn up of the aircraft's routes, stopovers, and usual parking locations.
  • Compare several specialist brokers: capacity available in this narrow market varies significantly between insurers.
  • Check cancellation and mid-term premium review clauses, not just the quote at inception.
  • Clarify the exact scope: some contracts distinguish ground parking from the aircraft being in flight.

For one-off use (a ferry flight, an import, an exceptional mission), it is sometimes possible to purchase a time-limited endorsement rather than a full annual extension, reducing cost for a narrowly defined need.

Good practice for an exposed aircraft

Beyond the insurance itself, several common-sense measures reduce the actual risk faced by an aircraft operating in or toward sensitive zones: avoiding unplanned stopovers at poorly secured airfields, keeping continuously informed of how the geopolitical situation evolves along planned routes, and keeping complete, up-to-date documentation of the aircraft (maintenance logbook, airworthiness certificate, registration), which can ease any recovery process if an incident occurs.

Finally, an owner considering the purchase of a used aircraft intended for this kind of international use should build the cost of this endorsement into their operating budget as early as the acquisition stage, alongside base insurance, maintenance, and registration — rather than discovering it later when planning a first flight into an exposed zone.

Advice — Never treat the absence of a past claim as a guarantee of no future risk: the need for a war risk and hijacking endorsement must be assessed zone by zone, mission by mission, and reviewed on every significant change of route.
