Real cost of ownership over 5 years: calculating aircraft TCO by category

The purchase price is only part of the bill. Here's how to calculate the total cost of ownership (TCO) of your next aircraft over 5 years, with a comparison across aircraft categories.

Why total cost matters more than purchase price

Two used aircraft listed at the same purchase price can end up representing very different budgets once the first five years of ownership have passed. The asking price, however decisive it may feel at the time of the decision, tells you nothing about remaining engine hours, the insurance cost tied to the category, or how often maintenance work will be required going forward. Thinking in terms of total cost of ownership (TCO) puts the purchase price back into its proper context: the real amount of money you will actually spend over a given holding period.

This approach is especially useful when comparing very different categories of aircraft — a light sport aircraft and a certified single-engine piston, for example — that serve the same mobility need but whose cost structures have nothing in common. It also helps avoid the classic disappointment of a buyer who focuses on the sticker price and discovers, a year or two later, that the annual maintenance budget far exceeds the original forecast.

The fixed costs of ownership

Some expenses are due every year regardless of how many hours are actually flown. They form the unavoidable base of the cost of ownership.

  • Parking or hangar — the cost varies widely by airfield, region and type of shelter (outdoor tie-down, shared hangar or private hangar).
  • Insurance — liability cover and, where relevant, hull insurance; the premium depends on the category, the insured value and the pilot's experience.
  • Mandatory annual inspection — periodic check whose cost depends on the aircraft's complexity and the labour time required.
  • Memberships and subscriptions — flying club membership, navigation database subscriptions or digital logbook software.

These fixed costs weigh proportionally much more heavily on an owner who flies little: an aircraft used only a few dozen hours a year will see its hourly cost climb mechanically, since fixed expenses are spread over fewer flight hours.

The variable costs tied to actual use

By contrast, other expense items scale directly with the number of flight hours flown and must be estimated from a realistic annual activity assumption.

  • Fuel — often the most visible line item, driven by the aircraft's hourly fuel burn and the fuel price at the home airfield.
  • Overhaul reserve — a regular set-aside to fund the engine's major overhaul as it approaches the manufacturer's recommended hour limit, whether on a certified aircraft or a Rotax-powered light sport aircraft.
  • Routine upkeep — oil changes, spark plugs, tyres, brakes and other wear parts, which scale with usage intensity.
  • Recurrent training and currency — dual flights with an instructor and rating renewals, worthwhile even when not strictly mandatory for the pilot's profile.
Good to know — Always start from a realistic annual flight-hour assumption, slightly below your initial ambitions, rather than overestimating activity and underestimating the real hourly cost.

How to calculate TCO over 5 years

Calculating TCO over 5 years follows a straightforward logic, as long as no expense item is left out.

The calculation steps

  • Add the aircraft's purchase price to the immediate ancillary costs: pre-purchase inspection, registration and transfer fees, any initial refurbishment.
  • List every annual fixed cost and multiply the total by 5.
  • Estimate the planned annual flight hours and derive the fuel cost and overhaul reserve over the period from it.
  • Add a contingency reserve, typically between 10 and 15% of total annual costs, to absorb unplanned work.
  • Deduct, where applicable, the aircraft's estimated resale value at the end of the 5 years to obtain a net cost of ownership.

The resulting figure represents the real cost you will have borne over the period, not just the capital committed at the time of purchase. This is the number that lets you compare two acquisition projects on a consistent basis, instead of stopping at the headline listing price.

Without claiming absolute precision — every aircraft, region and usage profile is different — clear patterns emerge when comparing the broad categories of light aircraft against one another.

This category-by-category view confirms a simple principle: an aircraft's purchase price and its real 5-year cost of ownership don't always follow the same ranking. A more expensive aircraft at purchase can turn out cheaper to operate if its remaining engine hours are high and its maintenance network well developed, and the reverse is just as true.

Common pitfalls that skew the calculation

Several mistakes come up repeatedly when a buyer estimates the real cost of ownership of their future aircraft for the first time.

Watch out — Forgetting the engine overhaul reserve is the costliest mistake: it is often the single largest expense of the first five years of ownership, and its due date should be known from day one via the engine logbook.
  • Basing the fuel estimate on a price observed at a single airfield, ignoring possible regional variation.
  • Overlooking the cost of ferrying the aircraft to its home airfield, which can be significant for a remote purchase.
  • Overestimating the annual flight hours actually flown, which artificially lowers the overall hourly cost.
  • Ignoring the insurance premium gap between two categories of aircraft at a comparable purchase budget.

A worked comparison example

Take a simplified, purely illustrative example: a buyer is weighing a recent multi-axis light sport aircraft against an older certified single-engine aircraft, both listed at a similar purchase price. For the same annual activity, the light sport aircraft will likely show lighter fixed costs (insurance, parking) and fuel burn, but the certified single can partly offset that gap if its remaining engine hours before the next major overhaul are high and its logbook flawless.

Conversely, a certified single nearing its engine hour limit, facing a major overhaul within the next two or three years, will see its 5-year TCO badly hit by that single reserve alone — sometimes enough to exceed that of the light sport aircraft despite its lower initial purchase price. This is exactly the kind of reversal that a proper TCO calculation reveals, where comparing listing prices alone would be misleading.

Using TCO to decide between two aircraft

Once the calculation has been done for each aircraft under consideration, the 5-year TCO becomes a far more reliable decision tool than the purchase price alone. It lets you compare different categories on a consistent basis and prioritise criteria according to your real goal: minimising total spend, maximising aircraft availability, or limiting the risk of an unplanned expense tied to a looming major overhaul.

In every case, this calculation should rely on verified data — a complete logbook, documented engine hours, recent maintenance invoices — rather than on the seller's statements alone. An independent pre-purchase inspection remains the best way to validate the assumptions in your calculation before committing to five years of ownership.

Tip — Build your own TCO spreadsheet for each aircraft you compare, using the same annual flight-hour assumptions, rather than relying on generic averages found online: only a personalised estimate reflects your real use.
