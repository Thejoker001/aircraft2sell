Used Piper Seminole: twin-engine trainer buying guide

Why the Seminole remains a reference for multi-engine training

The Piper PA-44 Seminole was designed from the outset as a light twin dedicated to flight training: counter-rotating propellers that remove the critical engine issue, handling close to the single-engine Piper Arrow whose airframe it largely shares, and predictable behaviour during simulated engine-out exercises. These qualities have made it one of the most widely used twins among flight schools and training centres for the multi-engine (ME) rating and the step up toward a professional career.

On the used market, you'll mainly find two main generations: the early models produced in the 1980s, and the relaunched generation from the early 2010s with fully digital avionics. This diversity of model years, combined with highly varied operating histories (intensive flight schools, clubs, private owners), explains a wide price range and sometimes very different hour totals from one aircraft to another.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used Seminole depends above all on the age of the aircraft, total airframe hours, the status of both Lycoming engines (remaining potential before overhaul on each one), and the level of avionics fitted. The oldest examples, coming from intensive flight-school use with reduced potential on one or both engines, trade at a noticeably more accessible entry level than more recent, digitally equipped versions, which represent a significantly larger investment.

Beyond the purchase price, your budget should include ancillary costs: an independent mechanic's pre-purchase inspection qualified on the type, registration and transfer fees, insurance (often higher on a twin than on a comparable single), and possibly an avionics refresh if the aircraft needs one.

What to check before buying

As with any used aircraft, a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic qualified on the type is essential before finalising a transaction. For a Seminole, a few points deserve particular attention given its twin powerplant and frequent flight-school use.

  • Complete history of both Lycoming engines: hours elapsed since the last overhaul on each one, compliance with the manufacturer's maintenance program, traceability of replaced parts.
  • Condition of the retractable landing gear and its hydraulic system, particularly stressed on a training aircraft with many cycles.
  • Condition of the airframe and flight controls, looking for signs of intensive use (repeated landings, simulated engine-out exercises).
  • Operation of the constant-speed propellers and their feathering system, essential for simulated engine-failure training.
  • Complete logbook and technical file for both engines and the airframe, with no grey areas in the aircraft's history.
Good to know — Never rely solely on the seller's statements about overall condition: a written independent inspection report, including oil analysis on both engines, remains the best protection against a bad surprise after the purchase.

Multi-engine training and owner-pilot rating

Flying a Seminole as a private owner requires holding a current multi-engine (ME) rating, obtained through dedicated training at an approved organisation. This rating is added to your base licence and needs regular practice, particularly on engine-failure procedures, to stay current over time.

Many used Seminoles come precisely from flight-school fleets, which has the advantage of guaranteeing closely tracked, well-documented maintenance, but also means carefully checking the number of cycles and hours accumulated, often higher than on an aircraft held by a private owner.

Maintenance and operating costs

The operating cost of a Seminole is naturally higher than that of an equivalent single, since you have to maintain two engines, two constant-speed propellers and a retractable gear. The main expense items remain the mandatory annual inspection, insurance, parking and, in the longer term, overhauling both engines, which can represent a significant expense if the two overhauls fall close together.

Having the aircraft maintained at an approved shop familiar with the type helps limit unpleasant surprises and preserve its resale value. Rigorous, well-documented maintenance records for both engines are a strong argument at a future resale.

Tip — Always ask for the last three maintenance invoices for both engines before making an offer: they give an accurate picture of how seriously the aircraft has been maintained and help you anticipate the next overhaul schedule.

How the Seminole compares to the competition

Within the light twin trainer category, the Seminole sits as a more affordable alternative to buy and maintain compared with more recent models such as the Diamond DA42, whose diesel or Austro powerplant noticeably changes the cost profile. It remains, however, very widely used, with a large installed fleet across Europe and North America, which makes parts availability and type-familiar maintenance shops easier to find.

For an owner looking primarily for a simple, proven twin that's economical to maintain within its category, the Seminole remains a sound choice. For more intensive use or a search for lower long-term operating costs, it can be worth comparing against more recent models before deciding.

Resale and the used market

The Seminole benefits from an active used market, driven by steady demand from flight schools and private pilots looking to keep their multi-engine rating current. A well-maintained aircraft, with a transparent history on both engines and up-to-date avionics, generally resells more easily and on better terms than one whose technical file has grey areas.

Comparing several listings of similar models remains the best way to assess a fair price, both for buying and for preparing a future resale. Price differences between two aircraft of the same model year are almost always explained by the remaining potential on each of the two engines and by usage history (intensive flight school versus private owner).
