Why the Ventus remains a benchmark performance glider

The Schempp-Hirth Ventus is one of the most respected performance gliders since its introduction in the mid-1980s. Designed for the 15-metre class and later offered in an 18-metre variable-span version, it has shaped generations of competition and cross-country soaring pilots with its high glide ratio and consistent, predictable handling. Its reputation for structural soundness in carbon-fibre construction, combined with handling qualities appreciated even by less experienced pilots, makes it a sought-after choice on the used market, whether for competition flying or performance soaring in a club or private ownership setting.

The used market today offers several generations of Ventus, from the original model through to the more recent Ventus 2 and Ventus 3, with meaningful differences in performance, equipment and price depending on model year. Before starting a search, it is essential to identify which generation fits your intended use (competition, cross-country flying, club ownership) and your budget.

The successive versions and their differences

The original Ventus, produced from the mid-1980s, laid the foundations for the lineage with a 15-metre span extendable to 16.6 metres on some versions. The Ventus 2, introduced in the 1990s, brought refined aerodynamics and a better glide ratio, offered in both 15-metre and 18-metre variable-span configurations as well as a two-seat version (Ventus 2cT two-seater and variants). The more recent Ventus 3 features a redesigned airframe and even higher performance, at a noticeably higher purchase price on the used market for recent examples.

Some versions, identified by the "T" or "cT" suffix (such as the Ventus 2cT), include a retractable turbo engine used as a sustainer or for self-launching. This power unit significantly changes the maintenance profile compared with a pure, unpowered glider, and should be factored into the overall budget.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used Ventus varies significantly depending on the generation, the condition of the airframe, the installed instrumentation (flight computer, variometer, navigation system) and whether a sustainer engine is fitted. An original Ventus some thirty years old, with basic equipment, trades at a noticeably more accessible entry level than a well-equipped recent Ventus 2 or 3, which can represent a far larger investment, particularly for powered versions.

Beyond the purchase price, the budget should include costs specific to soaring: a trailer sized for the glider, a pre-purchase inspection by someone qualified on the type, registration and transfer fees, glider-specific insurance, and possibly an instrumentation upgrade if the aircraft needs one. Maintaining a powered (cT) version requires an additional budget for the powerplant.

What to check before buying

As with any used glider, a pre-purchase inspection by someone qualified on the type is essential before finalising a transaction. For a Ventus, a few points deserve particular attention.

  • Condition of the carbon-fibre structure: look for any impacts, delamination, previous repairs or signs of damage at the canopy and ailerons, which can be hard to detect without a specialist inspection.
  • Operation of the flight controls and the span-extension mechanism on applicable versions, as well as play in the control surfaces.
  • For powered (cT) versions, complete engine history: hours elapsed since the last overhaul, number of extension/retraction cycles, traceability of replaced parts.
  • Condition of the water ballast system (valves, pipework, seals) and absence of corrosion at metal attachment points.
  • Complete logbook and technical file, with no grey areas in the aircraft's history, including any declared major repairs.
Good to know — Never rely solely on the seller's statements about overall condition: a written independent inspection report, including a check of the composite airframe, remains the best protection against a bad surprise after the purchase.

Variable span and water ballast: strengths to master

Some 18-metre versions of the Ventus offer variable span, allowing the pilot to switch between a 15-metre and an 18-metre configuration depending on the flying conditions sought. This system, valued in competition for its versatility, requires careful maintenance of the extension mechanisms and regular verification that they operate correctly, a point not to overlook during the pre-purchase inspection.

Water ballast, widely used across this lineage of gliders to optimise cruise speed depending on thermal conditions, also requires specific upkeep of the valves and pipework. A system that leaks or drains unevenly can noticeably affect performance and safety in flight, and must be corrected before any use in competition or cross-country flying.

Maintenance, major inspections and operating costs

The operating cost of a Ventus remains comparable to that of other composite performance gliders in its category, with periodic maintenance schedules set by the manufacturer and the relevant airworthiness authority, including major inspections at regular intervals to check the integrity of the airframe. For powered versions, additional costs apply to the powerplant: engine maintenance, tracking engine hours and following the manufacturer's overhaul schedule.

Having the aircraft maintained by an approved workshop familiar with the type, and keeping a rigorous, well-documented logbook, helps limit unpleasant surprises and preserve its resale value. A transparent maintenance history is a strong argument at a future resale, particularly for a competition glider whose history may include repairs following an incident.

Tip — Always ask for details of the most recent major inspections and, for powered versions, the engine hours and SMOH (hours since last overhaul) before making an offer.

Resale and the used market

The Ventus benefits from an active used market in Europe, supported by a large installed fleet within the soaring community and good parts availability thanks to the longevity of the lineage. A well-maintained aircraft, with a transparent logbook and a sound airframe, generally resells more easily and on better terms than one whose technical file has grey areas or poorly documented repairs.

Comparing several listings of similar models and generations remains the best way to assess a fair price, both for buying and for preparing a future resale. Price differences between two Ventus gliders of the same model year are almost always explained by airframe condition, the level of installed instrumentation and, for powered versions, the condition of the powerplant.
