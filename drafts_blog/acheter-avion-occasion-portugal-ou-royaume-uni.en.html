Two European markets with very different dynamics

Portugal and the United Kingdom represent two very different profiles for a buyer of a used light aircraft in Europe. The first is a smaller, still-developing market, with limited supply but sometimes attractive prices. The second is one of the most active and mature general aviation markets on the continent, with abundant supply, but a regulatory framework that has become distinct from that of the European Union since Brexit.

The choice between the two countries largely depends on your priorities: the size of the available supply, the simplicity of the administrative procedures, or the country where you plan to base and operate the aircraft day to day.

The Portuguese used-aircraft market

The Portuguese market for used light aircraft remains modest in size compared with the large markets of Central and Northern Europe. Supply is mostly concentrated on classic light single-engine aircraft (Cessna 172, Piper PA-28) and a handful of microlights, with fewer listings available at any given time. This relative scarcity can work in the seller's favour for sought-after models, but it can also make negotiation easier when local supply is quiet.

Portugal's generally mild year-round climate is a real advantage for flying light aircraft and for preserving airframes, particularly composite ones, which suffer less from humidity and frost than in parts of Northern Europe.

The post-Brexit UK market

The United Kingdom has one of the most active and best-stocked general aviation markets in Europe, with a very wide choice of aircraft across every category, a large installed fleet and a dense network of airfields. Since Brexit, the UK is no longer part of the European Union or of the EASA regulatory area for most airworthiness matters, which has introduced real differences with the rest of Europe, particularly around mutual recognition of certificates and registration transfer procedures.

For a buyer based in the European Union, acquiring an aircraft registered in the UK therefore means planning for import and registration-transfer procedures that, in some respects, are now closer to those for an aircraft coming from a third country than to those for a simple intra-EU transfer.

Good to know — The volume of listings available in the UK remains a strong argument for anyone looking for a specific model or configuration, but you need to factor the post-Brexit procedures into your purchase timeline if the aircraft is to be based in the EU afterwards.

Registration: ANAC versus the UK CAA

In Portugal, aircraft registration falls under the ANAC (Autoridade Nacional da Aviação Civil), with the CS- prefix, and operates within the common EASA regulatory framework applicable across all European Union member states. A transfer from another EU country therefore follows the usual harmonised rules between member states.

In the United Kingdom, the competent authority is the UK Civil Aviation Authority (UK CAA), with the G- prefix. Since the UK left the EASA system, certification and transfer procedures to or from the UK no longer automatically follow the same simplified processes as between two EU member states. An EU-based buyer looking to import a G-registered aircraft should check precisely with the authority in their destination country on the conditions for recognising the British airworthiness file.

  • Valid certificate of airworthiness — check this in both cases, with particular attention to its cross-border recognition for a British aircraft.
  • Bill of sale — required in both countries, dated and signed by both parties.
  • Insurance certificate — required before first use under the new registration, regardless of country.

Taxation and VAT: EU versus a third country

In Portugal, as elsewhere in the European Union, a sale between private individuals is generally not subject to Portuguese VAT, while a sale carried out by a professional or a company may be subject to it under the usual EU-wide rules.

The United Kingdom, no longer being an EU member, is now treated as a third country for customs and tax purposes in trade with the EU. An aircraft imported from the UK into an EU country may therefore be subject to import VAT, and the same applies in reverse for a European aircraft exported to the UK. It is strongly recommended to have your tax situation confirmed before any cross-border transaction involving the UK.

Climate, airfields and day-to-day use

Portugal offers a climate well suited to year-round use of an aircraft, with a generally high number of flyable days, a real advantage for airframe upkeep and consistency of flying. Its airfield network, however, is less dense than in the major Northern European countries.

The United Kingdom has a very dense network of airfields and a particularly developed general aviation culture, with numerous clubs and specialist maintenance workshops. The weather there is more variable, however, which can affect the aircraft's practical availability depending on the season.

Pre-purchase inspection and documents

Whichever country you choose, a pre-purchase inspection by a qualified independent mechanic remains essential before signing anything. It should cover the condition of the airframe, the consistency of the engine logbook, maintenance history and the compliance of the installed avionics.

  • Complete logbook and engine logbook, with no chronological gaps.
  • History of engine hours and remaining potential before the next major overhaul.
  • Checking consistency between the documents provided and the actual condition found during inspection.
Point of caution — For a British aircraft intended to move into the European Union, have a professional confirm in advance the precise conditions for recognising its airworthiness file: getting this wrong can significantly delay putting the aircraft into service.

Which country to choose depending on your profile

If your priority is administrative simplicity and you intend to stay within the European Union's regulatory framework, Portugal offers a more direct buying and registration process, at the cost of a more limited available supply. If your priority is model choice and a wide range of listings, the UK remains hard to beat, provided you factor the country's post-Brexit procedures into your timeline and budget, particularly if the aircraft is to be based in the EU afterwards.

Either way, compare several similar listings before committing: it remains the best way to assess a fair price, whichever country you buy in.
