Cessna 206 vs Cessna 182: which single-engine for bush flying or light cargo

Stationair or Skylane: a detailed comparison of the two Cessna single-engines to help you choose based on payload, terrain type and operating budget.

Two Cessna cousins, two different missions

The Cessna 182 Skylane and the Cessna 206 Stationair share the same technical lineage: an all-metal airframe, a high wing, fixed gear and Continental or Lycoming piston engines depending on the generation. It's easy to mistake one for the other at first glance, since their silhouettes are so similar. Yet these two single-engine aircraft address different needs as soon as payload, seating and the buyer's real intended use come into play.

The 182 remains primarily a versatile four-seater, popular for personal and family travel, while the 206 was designed as a true utility aircraft capable of carrying six occupants or cargo, taking off loaded from short strips, and operating on demanding terrain — African bush strips, unprepared runways, skydiving operations or flights over remote areas.

The Cessna 182 Skylane: the versatile four-seater

The Skylane has established itself as a benchmark among private owners looking for a comfortable single-engine aircraft that cruises quickly and is relatively simple to operate. Its generous power compared to a Cessna 172 lets it carry four occupants with baggage while keeping good performance, including at altitude for turbocharged versions.

On the used market, the 182 comes in many generations, from 1960s conventional-tail models to more recent versions fitted with digital avionics. This diversity translates into a wide price range, but the 182 generally remains more affordable to buy and maintain than a Stationair.

The Cessna 206 Stationair: the load hauler

The Stationair was designed from the outset as a "cargo" and utility evolution of the 182 family, with a reinforced airframe, an accessible rear baggage area and, depending on version, double cargo doors that make it easier to load bulky equipment or jump out skydivers. Its significantly higher payload capacity makes it the preferred working tool for bush operators, skydiving schools and light transport missions to remote strips.

This utility versatility comes at a price: the 206 burns more fuel, costs more to buy and requires somewhat more sustained maintenance than a 182, particularly on versions fitted with a turbocharger or reinforced landing gear for operating on rough strips.

Payload and cabin volume: the deciding factor

This is arguably the clearest point of difference between the two models. The 182 offers a comfortable payload for four people and their baggage, but quickly reaches its limits if you want to carry bulky equipment or more passengers. The 206, with its six-seat capacity and larger baggage volume, can carry considerably more weight and cargo, making it the aircraft of choice as soon as the mission goes beyond simple light passenger transport.

Before buying, it's essential to calculate the expected weight and balance for the typical mission you have in mind: a 182 loaded to its limit quickly loses safety margin, whereas a 206 retains more reserve under the same conditions.

Purchase budget and operating costs

For comparable equipment and generation, a Cessna 182 generally trades at a lower price than a Cessna 206, both at purchase and for routine maintenance. The heavier Stationair, often fitted with a turbocharger on certain versions, means higher fuel consumption and generally higher engine maintenance costs over the ownership period.

Insurance follows the same logic: a 206 used for intensive utility missions (skydiving, cargo, bush operations) may attract higher premiums than a 182 used for occasional personal travel. In all cases, the real cost per flight hour depends heavily on remaining engine potential and the intended frequency of use.

Short-strip and bush performance

  • The 206 generally benefits from a structure and landing gear designed to withstand repeated operation on unprepared strips.
  • The lighter 182 can also operate from short strips, but with a more limited load margin as soon as terrain or altitude becomes more demanding.
  • Turbocharged versions of both models keep better performance at altitude and in hot weather, an asset for tropical or mountainous missions.
  • A reinforced landing gear or low-pressure tyre kit, sometimes fitted on 206s dedicated to bush flying, deserves to be checked and documented during the inspection.
Good to know — For regular operation on rough strips or in hot, high-altitude conditions, always ask the seller for actual observed performance, beyond the figures in the flight manual.

Which aircraft for which mission

The choice between the 182 and the 206 should first be based on the aircraft's real intended mission. For personal or family use, with a need for comfort and cruise performance without the constraint of cargo, the 182 often remains the most balanced choice in terms of purchase and maintenance budget.

Conversely, as soon as the mission involves light cargo transport, multiple passengers, bush operations or skydiving, the 206 Stationair becomes the natural choice thanks to its superior payload and ruggedness, in exchange for a higher operating budget.

Buying either one used: points to watch

Whether it's a 182 or a 206, the pre-purchase inspection should pay particular attention to corrosion on the all-metal airframe, especially on aircraft that have operated for a long time near salt water or in humid climates. On a 206 used for bush or intensive skydiving operations, the landing gear, the cargo-area attachment points and the repeated takeoff/landing cycles deserve a thorough check by an independent mechanic familiar with the type.

Asking for the complete logbook, the engine history (hours since the last major overhaul) and, if possible, details of the missions flown (cargo, skydiving, training) makes it possible to objectively assess the aircraft's real wear before committing.

Comparing several listings of similar models and model years remains, as with any used aircraft, the best way to assess a fair price and spot a well-maintained aircraft.
