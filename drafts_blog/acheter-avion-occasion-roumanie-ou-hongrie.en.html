Buying a used aircraft in Romania or Hungary: which country to choose

Market, prices, taxation, registration and maintenance compared between Romania and Hungary, to help you decide where to look for your used aircraft in Central and Eastern Europe.

Two Central and Eastern European markets, two different profiles

Romania and Hungary both attract European buyers looking for used aircraft at prices more accessible than in Western Europe, while fully benefiting from the European Union's regulatory framework. Both countries are EU members, Hungary since 2004 and Romania since 2007, which immediately puts a transaction in either country on much simpler footing than a purchase outside the EU, with no customs formalities on import for a buyer based in another member state.

Each market carries a distinctive aviation heritage: Romania long produced its own light aircraft and gliders (notably through IAR), while Hungary has a strong historical tradition in gliding and amateur construction, with a very active network of airfields and flying clubs around Budapest and the country's major provincial towns.

Size and composition of the used market

The Romanian used market is supported by a large historical fleet of light aircraft and microlights, partly inherited from the national flying club and local production, together with a growing supply of Western single-engine aircraft imported by clubs and private individuals. The supply remains less standardised than in Western Europe, however, with a notable proportion of aircraft needing refurbishment before resale.

The Hungarian market is generally more structured, with a strong presence of gliders and tow planes linked to the country's local gliding tradition, alongside a solid supply of classic single-engine aircraft (Cessna, Piper) and experimental or kit-built aircraft, assembled by individuals within an active network of flying clubs. The capital Budapest and its surrounding region concentrate a large share of the available stock and of maintenance professionals.

Comparing the prices charged

In both countries, prices for a standard Western model (Cessna 172, Piper PA-28) generally remain lower than those seen in Western Europe for a comparable condition, which explains the growing interest of foreign buyers in both markets. Gliders and locally built aircraft, on the other hand, trade at highly variable levels, often difficult to compare for lack of a standardised price benchmark from one country to another.

Taxation, EU status and VAT

Romania and Hungary, both EU members, apply the Community's VAT rules for transactions between professionals, while a sale between private individuals is generally not subject to VAT, as elsewhere in the EU. A buyer based in another member state can therefore treat a purchase in either country as a standard intra-community transaction, with no import customs clearance or duties.

The main practical difference between the two countries lies more in local tax practices and administrative timelines than in the VAT regime itself. It is advisable to have your tax situation confirmed before the purchase, particularly if the aircraft has already changed registration country several times within the Union.

Registration: the Romanian and Hungarian registers

In Romania, aircraft registration falls under the Autoritatea Aeronautică Civilă Română (AACR), under the YR- prefix, following the common EASA rules applicable to any member state. Transferring registration from another EU country follows the standard intra-community procedure, with no additional customs steps.

In Hungary, the national civil aircraft register is managed by the Közlekedési Hatóság (Hungarian transport authority), under the HA- prefix, also within the common EASA regulatory framework. Transferring registration between the two countries, or from another member state to either one, therefore follows comparable procedures, governed by the same European airworthiness standards.

  • Valid certificate of airworthiness and ARC — check before signing anything, as its expiry determines the first flight under your name.
  • Bill of sale — dated, signed by both parties, mentioning the aircraft's serial number.
  • De-registration from the previous register — required before new registration for an aircraft transferred from another member state.
Good to know — Expect generally a few weeks for an intra-community registration transfer to Romania or Hungary, a timeframe comparable to any transfer between two EASA member states, depending on how complete the technical file submitted is.

Maintenance network and parts availability

Romania has an expanding network of approved workshops, particularly around major flying clubs and flight schools, with reasonable parts availability for the most common Western models. Maintaining locally built aircraft (IAR), however, can prove more complex, as original parts can be difficult to source abroad.

Hungary benefits from a well-established maintenance network, especially around Budapest, with recognised expertise on gliders and tow planes thanks to the country's strong local gliding tradition. Hungarian workshops are also well accustomed to amateur-built and experimental construction, a useful advantage for a buyer interested in this type of aircraft.

Ferrying and logistics from Western Europe

Ferry distance remains a factor to anticipate for both countries, which are geographically further from the main Western markets than Germany or Austria. A ferry flight from Romania or Hungary to Western Europe generally involves several refuelling stops and more thorough weather planning, particularly when crossing the Carpathian mountain ranges on a departure from Romania.

Both countries' membership of the Schengen area simplifies border formalities on a ferry flight to another EU country: Hungary has been part of it since the late 2000s, and Romania joined the Schengen area for air border checks in March 2024. In both cases, ferrying therefore takes place without customs checks at the Union's internal borders, which significantly simplifies logistics compared with an import from a non-EU country.

Which country to favour depending on your profile

For a buyer interested in a classic Western single-engine aircraft at an attractive price, with a wide supply and a local production heritage, Romania remains a solid option, provided you accept a sometimes less standardised supply and carefully check the actual condition of the aircraft you visit.

For a buyer oriented towards gliding, tow planes or amateur-built and experimental aircraft, Hungary offers the advantage of a network of flying clubs and workshops particularly experienced with these types of aircraft, with an overall more homogeneous and structured market.

Good to know — In all cases, a pre-purchase inspection by a qualified independent mechanic remains essential, whichever country you choose, to confirm the aircraft's actual condition beyond the seller's claims.

Ultimately, the choice between Romania and Hungary depends less on a raw price gap than on the type of aircraft you are looking for and how familiar the local network is with that type. Taking the time to visit several aircraft in person and to have the entire technical file checked remains the best safeguard before committing to a cross-border transaction of this kind.
