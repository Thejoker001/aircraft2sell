Decommissioning and loss of airworthiness: two distinct situations

Two situations, often confused, apply to an aircraft that no longer flies or can no longer legally fly. Decommissioning is a voluntary step taken by the owner: they decide to take the aircraft out of service, temporarily or permanently, without a technical fault or an authority decision forcing the move. Loss of airworthiness, by contrast, can result from a decision by the civil aviation authority (for instance after an unaddressed defect is found, a non-renewed airworthiness review certificate, or an accident), or simply from the certificate of airworthiness (CofA) not being kept current.

In both cases the aircraft loses its right to fly, but the process, the legal consequences and the prospects for returning to service differ substantially. This guide covers both situations from the point of view of an owner or a prospective buyer.

Why an owner chooses to decommission an aircraft

Voluntary decommissioning stems from very concrete motivations, rarely linked to a safety issue:

  • Prohibitive cost of bringing the aircraft back to compliance — a major overhaul or major structural repair whose cost exceeds the aircraft's value.
  • Restoration or preservation project — a historic aircraft kept without any short-term intention to fly it again.
  • Ceasing operations — end of commercial or club activity, with the aircraft held in storage pending a decision.
  • Spare parts — an explicit intention to part out the aircraft and resell the still-usable components (engine, avionics, landing gear).

The administrative steps to expect

The exact process and terminology vary by country and by the competent authority (aircraft registration remains governed nationally within the European Union, even where airworthiness falls under the EASA framework). Broadly speaking, an owner decommissioning their aircraft must notify the registration authority of the withdrawal from service, and may need to surrender or neutralise certain associated documents (registration certificate, certificate of airworthiness). The flight logbook and engine logbook should be carefully preserved: they remain essential for any future return to service or for a sale, even for parts.

Word of caution — The precise steps (forms, timelines, competent authority) vary from country to country: always confirm the exact procedure with the relevant civil aviation authority or registry before acting, rather than relying on a generic process.

Impact on registration and insurance

A decommissioned aircraft, or one whose airworthiness has been withdrawn, generally keeps its registration until it is explicitly deregistered, but it can no longer legally fly. On the insurance side, it is essential to notify the insurer as soon as the aircraft is decommissioned: an "aircraft in flight" liability policy no longer applies, and storage-appropriate cover (fire, theft, third-party ground damage) should replace it if the aircraft remains on a field or in a hangar.

Local taxation (annual aircraft taxes, where they exist) can also be affected by a decommissioning declaration: some countries provide an exemption or a reduction for an aircraft officially out of service.

Returning a decommissioned aircraft to airworthy condition

A voluntarily decommissioned aircraft, with a complete maintenance logbook and clearly identified reasons for the shutdown, can generally regain airworthiness after a full inspection, resuming any interrupted maintenance tasks, and the issuance of a new airworthiness review certificate by an approved organisation. The cost and duration of this return to service depend directly on how long the aircraft was grounded and on its observed condition: the longer the shutdown, the more thorough the return-to-service inspection will need to be.

For an aircraft whose certificate of airworthiness was withdrawn by the authority for a specific reason (unaddressed defect, non-complied airworthiness directive), returning to service necessarily requires documented resolution of that reason before any new certificate can be issued.

Selling a decommissioned aircraft or one without a valid CofA

A decommissioned aircraft can perfectly well be sold, but the transaction must be transparent about its real status. The listing and any discussion with a prospective buyer should explicitly state the absence of a currently valid certificate of airworthiness, the reason for decommissioning if known, and the state of the logbook. A serious buyer will always factor the cost of returning the aircraft to service into their offer — the asking price for a decommissioned aircraft must reflect this reality, not the price of a flight-ready aircraft.

Tip — Gather every document (flight logbook, engine logbook, maintenance history, correspondence with the authority) before listing the aircraft for sale: a complete file reassures the buyer and makes it easier for them to assess the cost of returning it to airworthy condition.

Selling for spare parts

When returning an aircraft to airworthy condition is not economically justified, selling it for spare parts (engine, propeller, avionics, landing gear, instruments) remains a way to extract value from an aircraft that will not fly again. Each component resold separately should come with its own documentation (operating time, overhaul history) to retain value on the used market. This route permanently ends any prospect of returning the complete aircraft to service — a decision to make with full awareness of its finality.

Mistakes to avoid

Several mistakes come up repeatedly when managing a decommissioning or a loss of airworthiness:

  • Skipping the notification to the authority — continuing to pay taxes or insurance premiums that no longer fit, through sheer administrative oversight.
  • Losing or misplacing the logbooks — a missing flight logbook or engine logbook can make returning to airworthiness far more expensive, or even impossible in some cases.
  • Selling without transparency — listing an aircraft without disclosing its real status exposes the seller to disputes and damages their reputation.
  • Letting the aircraft deteriorate without protection — an aircraft stored without minimal upkeep or protection (see also our hangar vs outdoor parking guide) loses value far faster than a properly preserved aircraft, even a decommissioned one.
Word of caution — Never state a specific regulatory deadline or amount without verifying it with the competent authority of the country of registration: these rules vary and change over time, and inaccurate information can prove costly to both buyer and seller.
