Becoming a Flight Instructor (FI): path and career options

Why become a Flight Instructor (FI)

Flight instructing attracts a wide range of profiles: passionate private pilots who want to share their experience at weekends, young pilots who see instructing as a stepping stone toward a professional career, and more experienced pilots who want to fly more while staying active in general aviation.

Beyond the teaching itself, the Flight Instructor (FI) rating opens up real opportunities: teaching within a volunteer-run flying club, joining an approved flying school, or combining instructing with another aviation role. For many young pilots it is also an almost unavoidable step before an airline career, since hours logged as an instructor count toward the total flight experience airlines require.

Teaching also pushes instructors to keep improving themselves: explaining a manoeuvre to a student forces you to revisit the fundamentals, sharpen your own technique and better understand common mistakes. Many instructors describe this two-way dynamic — teaching while continuing to learn — as one of the most rewarding parts of the job, well beyond the financial side or hour-building alone.

Requirements to become an FI

Before starting an FI course, candidates need to meet certain conditions. At minimum, a Private Pilot Licence (PPL) is required, together with a minimum amount of flight experience set by European regulation (EASA Part-FCL), generally expressed in total flight hours and hours as pilot-in-command — the exact thresholds depend on the specific instructor rating being pursued and should always be confirmed with the training organisation.

Training organisations (ATOs or DTOs) typically also carry out a pre-entry assessment, often a flight with a senior instructor, to check the candidate's flying standard, discipline and ability to explain manoeuvres clearly. A solid grasp of the regulatory framework and genuine people skills matter just as much as raw flying ability, since instructing is as much about pedagogy as it is about technique.

The FI training path

The FI(A) course combines theoretical and practical training. The theory covers the principles of flight instruction, learning psychology, how to prepare and run briefings, and a thorough review of the regulatory and technical subjects from the PPL syllabus.

The practical phase is flown with a flight instructor qualified to train instructors, and focuses on the candidate's ability to demonstrate basic exercises, spot and correct a student's mistakes, and run a complete lesson from briefing to debrief. The course ends with a flight test in front of an examiner, who assesses teaching ability specifically — separate from the flying skill already proven when the candidate earned their original licence.

Good to know — The exact entry requirements (minimum hours, theoretical subjects, course length) are set by EASA Part-FCL regulation and can change over time: always confirm the current requirements directly with your ATO/DTO or the relevant civil aviation authority.

Cost and duration of the training

The cost of an FI course varies widely depending on the training organisation, the country and the type of aircraft used. Expect to pay for flight time with a qualified training instructor, the theoretical course fee charged by the ATO/DTO, and the final skill-test fee. A realistic overall budget typically runs from several thousand to tens of thousands of euros, depending on the candidate's starting experience and the number of hours required.

Duration depends on the pace chosen: some candidates complete an intensive course over a few weeks, while others spread it over several months alongside a full-time job. Either way, it's worth asking for a detailed quote covering the planned number of flight hours, the hourly rate for both the training aircraft and the training instructor, and any extra cost if additional hours are needed before the final test.

Keeping and renewing the FI rating

An FI rating is not granted for life: it must be revalidated periodically, usually through a combination of recent teaching activity and/or refresher training, sometimes topped up with a flight check with an examiner. An instructor who stops teaching for an extended period should plan the revalidation process well ahead of the rating's expiry date.

Beyond the strict administrative renewal, many instructors also attend pedagogy seminars, flight-safety courses or additional training (human factors, in-flight stress management, new avionics) to stay current and keep delivering quality instruction to their students.

This renewal logic also encourages instructors to teach regularly rather than sporadically: an instructor who flies often with students naturally keeps their teaching reflexes and regulatory knowledge sharp, which makes each revalidation much easier and reduces the need for time-consuming refresher training.

Career paths: club, school, private instruction

Career paths for an FI are varied and often overlap. Within a club, instructing is frequently a part-time activity, sometimes voluntary or modestly paid, alongside another job — this is the most common route for passionate private pilots.

  • Flying club — often part-time instructing, a good entry point for sharing a passion without making it a main activity.
  • Professional flying school — a full salaried role, higher hour volume, students often working toward a professional licence.
  • Independent private instruction — travelling to aircraft owners for tailored training or currency checks.
  • A step toward a professional career — building hours and experience before moving on to an airline or business-aviation career.

Progressing to CRI, IRI or examiner

Once the base FI rating is held, several further qualifications become available. A Class Rating Instructor (CRI) rating allows teaching class ratings without delivering the initial licence. An Instrument Rating Instructor (IRI) rating opens the door to instrument-flight teaching, a highly sought-after skill. Some experienced instructors eventually move on to examiner (FE) status, authorised to conduct skill tests for other pilots and instructors.

These progressions generally require significant teaching experience plus specific additional training, but they considerably widen an instructor's scope of work and career opportunities — especially for those who want instructing to become a long-term main activity.

Tips for a successful instructor project

Before committing to an FI course, it is worth talking to several working instructors to understand the realities of the job: the schedule (often concentrated on weekends and holidays) and the real income, whether in a club or professional setting. Comparing several ATOs/DTOs on their teaching approach, the availability of training instructors, and their pass rate at the final test helps avoid unpleasant surprises.

Finally, it pays to think the whole project through: total training budget, availability to fly regularly once qualified (keeping teaching skills sharp takes practice), and the medium-term goal — club instructing, a professional career, or a stepping stone toward another rating. A well-thought-out plan makes the course far easier to complete and far more rewarding in the long run.

It is also worth checking with your club or a prospective employer before starting training: some sponsor all or part of the FI course in exchange for a minimum commitment to teach afterwards, which can significantly ease the upfront cost while securing a first instructing role as soon as the rating is obtained.
