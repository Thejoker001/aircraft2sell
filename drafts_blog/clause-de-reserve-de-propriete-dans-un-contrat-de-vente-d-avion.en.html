Retention of title clause in an aircraft sale contract

How it works, benefits for the seller, risks for the buyer and drafting best practices for a retention of title clause used in an aircraft sale.

What a retention of title clause is

A retention of title clause (also called a reservation of title clause) is a contractual provision that delays the transfer of ownership of a good until the buyer has paid the full price, even though the good may already have been delivered and put into use. Applied to the sale of an aircraft, it allows the seller to remain the legal owner of the aircraft until the buyer has paid the agreed price in full, even after physical delivery and the start of use.

This mechanism is widely used in professional sales and in transactions involving staged payments, external financing, or a balance due after an initial deposit. It differs from a simple payment guarantee: a retention of title clause acts directly on the nature of the right held by the buyer, not merely on an obligation to repay.

How it applies in practice to an aircraft sale

In an aircraft sale with a retention of title clause, the buyer generally takes physical possession of the aircraft, and may sometimes start operating it, without becoming its owner until the price is paid in full. The seller retains an enforceable ownership right, which in theory allows them to reclaim the aircraft in the event of non-payment, provided it is still identifiable and has not been altered to the point of losing its individual character.

For such a clause to take effect, it must be agreed in writing before or at the time of delivery, and clearly stated in the sale contract or the related invoice. A clause added after delivery, or one the buyer never formally accepted, is highly likely to be found unenforceable in the event of a dispute.

Good to know — Retention of title concerns legal ownership of the aircraft, not physical possession: the buyer may hold and sometimes use the aircraft without yet being its legal owner.

Why it matters for the seller

For a seller, particularly a professional or dealer who accepts staged payments, a retention of title clause is a far stronger safeguard than a simple debt claim. By keeping ownership of the aircraft, the seller holds effective leverage and a direct remedy if the buyer stops paying: in principle they can reclaim the aircraft rather than having to rely solely on a debt-recovery action, which is often longer and less certain, especially if the buyer runs into financial difficulty.

This mechanism is particularly relevant for a high-value asset like an aircraft, which can generally be resold on an active market in the event of non-payment, unlike other types of assets that are harder to move.

Risks and precautions for the buyer

For the buyer, accepting a retention of title clause means operating an aircraft they do not yet fully own, which can create several practical difficulties. It will often be hard to grant a valid security interest in the aircraft to their own lender before ownership is finally acquired, and any resale of the aircraft before the price is paid in full remains legally fragile, as the original seller may potentially assert their rights against a sub-buyer.

  • Check the exact conditions for lifting the clause — the precise date and mechanism by which ownership will actually transfer.
  • Plan how it interacts with insurance — the insurer and the loss-payee arrangement must match the actual allocation of rights in the aircraft.
  • Clarify custody of the original documents — bill of sale, certificate of airworthiness and other papers needed for final registration in the buyer's name.
  • Check the law governing the contract — recognition and the effects of a retention of title clause vary considerably from one country to another.

Drafting an effective clause

A solid retention of title clause must be drafted in precise, unambiguous terms: it should clearly identify the aircraft concerned (registration, airframe and engine serial numbers), explicitly state that ownership transfers only on full payment of the agreed price, and specify what constitutes that full payment (date, amount, currency, payment method).

It is also advisable to address the practical consequences of non-payment: how and where the aircraft is to be returned, who bears the ferry-flight costs, and what happens to amounts already paid by the buyer. For a cross-border transaction, specifying the governing law and competent jurisdiction in the contract significantly reduces the risk of a later challenge to the clause's validity itself.

Word of caution — A poorly drafted retention of title clause, ambiguous about the exact transfer date or inserted too late in the contractual relationship, can be found unenforceable by a court in a dispute, depriving the seller of the protection they were seeking.

Effect on registration and insurance

How the aircraft is registered during the retention-of-title period deserves particular attention. If the aircraft is already registered in the buyer's name with the relevant civil aviation authority while the seller remains the legal owner under the contract, that registration appearance can create uncertainty for third parties, such as a lender or a potential sub-buyer, who might reasonably rely on the public register.

To limit this risk, it is common to use a trusted escrow agent to hold the bill of sale, the deregistration power of attorney and the other documents needed for the final transfer until full payment is confirmed, rather than letting the buyer hold all the paperwork from the moment of delivery. On the insurance side, it is also worth making sure the policy covering the aircraft correctly reflects the respective interests of the seller and the buyer throughout the period during which ownership has not yet finally transferred.

For cross-border transactions involving eligible aircraft, registering the arrangement with the international registry established under the Cape Town Convention on International Interests in Mobile Equipment can also help make the retention of title enforceable against third parties at an international level, in addition to whatever national law governs the contract.

What happens in case of non-payment or dispute

If the buyer defaults on payment, a seller holding a validly constituted retention of title clause can in principle bring an action to recover the aircraft, provided it is still identifiable and can be located. Such action generally needs to be brought within a reasonable time after the default, and the outcome can become complicated if the aircraft has moved to another country, been resold to a third party acting in good faith, or if the buyer is subject to insolvency proceedings.

The situation becomes considerably more complex if the buyer becomes insolvent or enters formal insolvency proceedings: recognition and ranking of the seller's claim then depend on the applicable insolvency law, which varies significantly between countries and can, in some cases, limit the practical reach of the clause despite its contractual validity.

Good to know — Treat a retention of title clause as a contractual safety net rather than an absolute guarantee: its real effectiveness depends heavily on how quickly the seller acts and on the law applicable at the time of the dispute.

Best practices and legal support

Whether buying or selling, having the retention of title clause drafted or reviewed by a lawyer specialised in aviation law or international commercial law remains the best protection, particularly when the transaction involves parties based in different countries or bank financing. How the clause interacts with the airworthiness documents, insurance and registration should be thought through as a whole, not treated as an isolated contractual formality.

For a significant or cross-border transaction, using an independent escrow agent to hold both the funds and the key documents until full payment is confirmed is often a safer and easier-to-enforce alternative to a unilateral retention of title, particularly when the aircraft needs to be registered quickly in the buyer's name after the sale.
