Piper Cub vs Aviat Husky: which bush and leisure aircraft to choose

Two tailwheel icons, two generations

The Piper Cub and the Aviat Husky both belong to the family of light tailwheel aircraft, designed for recreational flying, gentle aerobatics and rough-field operations. The Piper Cub, which appeared in the 1930s, is the legendary ancestor of an entire generation of bush aircraft. The Aviat Husky, much more recent, carries that same spirit forward while benefiting from decades of aerodynamic and engine improvements, delivering noticeably superior performance.

Comparing these two aircraft means choosing between the heritage charm and absolute simplicity of a timeless classic, and the STOL (short takeoff and landing) capability and versatility of an aircraft designed from the outset for mountain and modern bush flying.

The Piper Cub: the historic benchmark

The Piper Cub has trained entire generations of pilots and remains one of the most recognisable light aircraft in the world today, with its iconic yellow livery and clean silhouette. Its fabric-covered tubular airframe, modest engine and direct controls make it a mechanically simple aircraft, pleasant to fly at low speed and particularly economical to operate.

On the used market, the supply of Cubs (and its many variants and later replicas) is large and covers a wide range of condition, airframe age, and original or modified engines. It's an aircraft sought for pure flying pleasure, slow cruises over the countryside, and keeping an aviation tradition alive, more than for raw performance.

The Aviat Husky: the modern bush heir

The Aviat Husky was designed to carry the Cub's spirit forward while addressing its performance limitations. Its sturdier airframe, more powerful engine and refined aerodynamics give it noticeably superior short-field takeoff and landing capability, along with a more comfortable payload and cruise speed for genuinely bush- or mountain-oriented use.

It appeals to pilots who want an aircraft capable of landing on short, uneven strips while retaining versatility for touring, aerial photography or reaching remote airfields, with a modern engine that's generally simpler to maintain over time than some of the older engines still fitted to original Cubs.

Performance and STOL capability

The performance gap between the two aircraft is usually the deciding factor in the choice. The Cub, with its modest original engine, remains a slow aircraft but a remarkably forgiving one at low speed, ideal for learning fine-grained stick-and-rudder skills and relaxed flying. The Husky, more powerful, offers significantly shorter takeoff and landing distances and a better ability to climb quickly over terrain, an important advantage for demanding bush or mountain use.

  • Piper Cub: modest engine, slow and forgiving flight, ideal for learning tailwheel handling.
  • Aviat Husky: more powerful engine, shorter takeoff and landing distances, better payload capacity.
  • Both aircraft require solid tailwheel skills, with particular care needed during taxi and crosswind landings.

Cabin comfort and visibility

The Piper Cub's cabin remains narrow, with tandem seating and minimalist controls, very much in the spirit of 1930s aircraft: little insulation, decent forward visibility but limited downward view while taxiing because of the long nose and the typical nose-up ground attitude of a tailwheel aircraft. The Aviat Husky, while also keeping a tandem configuration, offers a wider cabin, more comfortable seats and often more modern avionics depending on equipment fitted, making it a more pleasant aircraft for multi-hour flights.

This comfort difference matters little for a one-hour local flight around your home airfield, but becomes significant as soon as you plan longer legs, mountain flights with several stops, or regular use involving carrying gear for bush missions.

Budget comparison: purchase and maintenance

Acquisition budget varies widely depending on age, airframe condition, engine and equipment for both models. As a rule, a well-maintained original Piper Cub remains accessible as an entry-level budget into tailwheel recreational flying, while an Aviat Husky, more recent and more capable, requires a higher initial investment, offset by superior operational capability.

As always on the used market, the average list price matters less than the real condition of the airframe, the remaining engine potential and the consistency of documented maintenance in the logbook, which should always be checked aircraft by aircraft.

Which aircraft for which use

For a pilot who is mainly looking for the pleasure of classic flying, simplified maintenance and a controlled budget to fly regularly from conventional airfields, the Piper Cub remains a relevant and history-rich choice. It suits local recreational flying particularly well, with no performance requirement.

Conversely, a pilot planning genuinely bush-oriented use — short, unprepared strips, mountain terrain or remote airfields — or who wants more performance margin and payload to travel with gear, will find the Aviat Husky a far more suitable companion, in exchange for a higher acquisition budget.

Buying either one used: points to watch

For a Piper Cub, the airframe's age requires particular attention to the condition of the fabric covering, corrosion of the structural tubes hidden underneath, and the consistency of the restoration history, often extensive on aircraft several decades old. For an Aviat Husky, attention should focus on engine history, actual aircraft usage (repeated rough-field landings put heavy strain on the gear) and compliance of any STOL modifications made.

Good to know — Whichever model you choose, have a pre-purchase inspection carried out by an independent mechanic familiar with the type, in particular to check the fabric covering and structure on an older Cub, or gear wear on a Husky used in mountain terrain.

Comparing several examples of the same type and similar age remains the best way to assess a fair price and spot a genuinely well-maintained aircraft, whether a historic Cub or a modern Husky.
