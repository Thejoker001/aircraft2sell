Buying a Used Aircraft in Germany or the Czech Republic: Which Country to Choose

Market, prices, tax, registration and maintenance: a comparison between Germany and the Czech Republic to help you decide where to search for your used aircraft.

Two very different markets in Central Europe

Germany and the Czech Republic are neighbouring countries with a strong aviation culture, but fairly different market profiles. Germany has the largest light aviation fleet in continental Europe, built on a long-standing general aviation tradition and a very dense network of airfields and maintenance workshops. The Czech Republic, a smaller country, built its aviation reputation around a historic light aircraft industry (manufacturers such as Zlin Aircraft and Evektor) and a network of flying clubs federated under the Aeroklub České republiky, particularly active in microlights, gliding and light single-engine aircraft.

A German buyer can perfectly well find an interesting aircraft in the Czech Republic, especially in the microlight and motor-glider segment, where Czech supply has historically been very well structured. Conversely, a Czech buyer often widens their search towards Germany to access a broader choice of touring single-engine aircraft and twins.

Size and activity of the used market

The German used market is considerably larger in volume: it covers every category, from microlights to touring twins, with abundant supply of the classics (Cessna 172, Piper PA-28, Diamond DA40) and a high proportion of aircraft well equipped with recent avionics. The density of the flying club and workshop network makes it easy to compare several aircraft before buying.

The Czech market, smaller in absolute volume, stands out for its activity in microlights and motor-gliders, a segment where the country has recognised manufacturing and maintenance expertise reaching well beyond its borders. Supply of classic single-engines and twins is more limited than on the German market, but remains active and well connected to neighbouring markets (Germany, Austria, Poland, Slovakia).

Comparing the prices charged

For the same model and the same maintenance condition, prices observed in the Czech Republic are generally comparable to, or slightly lower than, those seen in Germany, a gap partly explained by locally more moderate maintenance labour costs. The difference is often more pronounced on microlights and motor-gliders, where local Czech supply and the proximity of manufacturer workshops favour more accessible prices.

Comparing several listings of similar models remains the best way to assess a fair price, regardless of which country the aircraft is located in. For Czech listings shown in koruna, always convert at the current exchange rate before comparing with German listings in euros.

Tax, VAT and currency

Since Germany and the Czech Republic are both EU member states, a transaction between the two countries generally falls under the intra-EU acquisition regime rather than an import from outside the EU, which significantly simplifies customs procedures. VAT treatment depends above all on the seller's status (private individual or VAT-registered professional), not solely on the country where the aircraft happens to be: a sale between private individuals is generally not subject to VAT, while a sale by a professional may be.

Another difference to keep in mind: the Czech Republic has not adopted the euro and keeps its own currency, the Czech koruna (CZK), with its own national VAT rate (commonly cited at around 21%, which should always be confirmed for the specific case of an aircraft with the Czech tax authority, the Finanční správa). Germany applies its own national VAT rate under the harmonised European regime. In both cases, it is advisable to have your tax situation confirmed before acquisition by a professional familiar with cross-border transactions.

Registration: LBA versus UCL

In Germany, every aircraft based and operated must be registered with the Luftfahrt-Bundesamt (LBA) under the D- prefix. In the Czech Republic, the competent authority is the Úřad pro civilní letectví (UCL), which issues registrations under the OK- prefix. An aircraft bought in either country can be re-registered in the other depending on the buyer's choice: keeping the registration of the purchase country is not mandatory.

  • For a transfer to either Germany or the Czech Republic, prior deregistration from the register of origin is generally required before the new registration.
  • The Certificate of Airworthiness (CofA) and its airworthiness review certificate (ARC) must be valid and recognised by the destination authority.
  • A liability insurance certificate covering operation in the destination country is required before first use under the new registration.
Good to know — Common EASA rules govern the transfer between EU member states and make it simpler than an import from outside the EU. Generally allow 2 to 8 weeks depending on how complete the file submitted to both authorities is.

Maintenance network and parts availability

Germany has a particularly dense and well-established network of approved maintenance workshops for most common models, with excellent parts availability thanks to the size of the market. The Czech Republic has also developed a competent workshop network, especially around major flying club bases (Letňany, Brno, Pardubice, Plzeň), with recognised know-how on microlights and motor-gliders thanks to the local presence of manufacturers.

For classic American single-engines (Cessna, Piper), parts availability can occasionally take longer in the Czech Republic than in Germany, where the larger market generally guarantees faster supply. For a microlight or motor-glider of Czech manufacture, the opposite can be true: original parts are often simpler to obtain directly from the local manufacturer.

The Czech continental climate, with cold, damp winters, warrants particular attention to winter storage conditions and possible corrosion during the pre-purchase inspection, regardless of the aircraft's ultimate country of origin.

Which country to favour depending on your profile

For a buyer interested in a microlight or motor-glider, the Czech Republic often offers a relevant choice and accessible prices, with recognised local maintenance know-how in these categories. For a buyer looking for a classic touring single-engine, a twin, or an aircraft very well equipped with recent avionics, the German market generally offers a larger volume of listings and a denser workshop network close by.

Good to know — In all cases, a pre-purchase inspection by a qualified independent mechanic remains essential, whether the aircraft is located in Germany, the Czech Republic or elsewhere in Europe.

Ultimately, the country of purchase matters less than the actual condition of the aircraft and how well it has been maintained. Comparing listings across both markets widens your options and gives you a stronger negotiating position, whether on price or on the terms of the transaction.
