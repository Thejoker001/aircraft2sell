Legal due diligence before buying a used aircraft

Beyond the technical inspection, buying a used aircraft requires a rigorous legal review: ownership, liens, mortgages and contract compliance. Here is how to run it properly.

Why legal due diligence is distinct from the technical inspection

When preparing to buy a used aircraft, attention almost always goes to its mechanical condition: engine hours, logbook, airframe condition. That technical inspection is essential, but it says nothing about a risk that is just as real: buying an aircraft encumbered by a debt, claimed by a third party, or simply sold by someone who has no right to dispose of it. That is exactly what legal due diligence addresses, and it must be carried out alongside the technical inspection — never instead of it.

An aircraft can be mechanically flawless and still legally impossible to acquire with peace of mind: an outstanding mortgage, a co-owner who never agreed to the sale, or an ongoing dispute between the operator and the owner. Without an upfront check, the buyer discovers these problems after signing, when it is too late to negotiate and sometimes too late to get a refund.

Verifying title of ownership and the chain of transfers

The first step is confirming that the person or company selling you the aircraft is actually its legal owner, or holds a clear mandate to sell it on the owner's behalf. Always request a copy of the previous bill of sale, and trace the chain of transfers back as far as possible, ideally to the factory delivery or the last entry in a reliable registry.

  • Exact identity of the registered owner on the registration registry, cross-checked against the seller's identity.
  • Consistency of the serial number (MSN) across the certificate of airworthiness (CofA), the logbook and the bill of sale.
  • No gap or unclear step in the chain of successive transfers.
  • If the seller acts through an agent (broker, management company), written proof of the authority to represent the owner.

If the aircraft is held by a company, also check that the person signing actually has the authority to bind that company for a transaction of this nature and size, by requesting a recent corporate registry extract and, where applicable, a board resolution authorizing the sale.

Liens, mortgages and other security interests on the aircraft

A used aircraft can carry security interests registered by a lender, an unpaid maintenance shop or a tax authority, which remain attached to the aircraft regardless of who currently owns it. Buying without checking these registrations exposes you to inheriting a debt the seller may not disclose, whether deliberately or not.

  • Search for mortgages or pledges registered against the aircraft with the registration registry or the relevant authority.
  • Confirmation that no maintenance-shop lien applies (a right of retention over the aircraft for an unpaid invoice).
  • A seller's warranty, built into the contract, guaranteeing the aircraft is free of any encumbrance at the time of sale.
  • For financed aircraft, written proof that any financing (lease, bank loan) has been settled and the corresponding release obtained.
Word of caution — A mortgage release must be obtained and verified BEFORE paying the balance, never on the seller's simple promise to sort it out after the sale.

Registration, registry status and administrative standing

Checking the registration registry complements the title verification. It confirms that the certificate of airworthiness (CofA) actually matches the physical aircraft inspected, that its status is current, and that no administrative procedure (suspension, pending deregistration) puts the transaction at risk.

It is also worth checking consistency between the current country of registration and the one intended after the sale: changing registries involves paperwork, delays and sometimes extra technical requirements (bringing the aircraft into line with another airworthiness authority) that are best anticipated before signing rather than discovered afterward.

Essential clauses in the sale contract

A well-drafted used-aircraft sale contract protects the buyer on points the technical inspection does not cover. Several clauses should always be included, whether the sale is between private individuals or arranged through a broker.

  • A warranty of title and freedom from encumbrances (title free of any lien or mortgage).
  • A condition precedent tied to a satisfactory pre-purchase inspection, with the deposit refunded in case of a major defect.
  • A precise, exhaustive list of documents to be delivered at closing (logbooks, certificates, manuals).
  • A clear split of responsibilities and costs between signing and actual delivery (insurance, ferry flight, storage).
  • Governing law and competent jurisdiction in case of dispute, particularly important for a cross-border transaction.

Having the contract reviewed by a lawyer specialized in aviation law before signing is a modest expense compared to the amounts at stake, especially for transactions above a few hundred thousand euros.

Special case: buying through a company or a co-ownership structure

When the aircraft is held in co-ownership or by a dedicated holding company — a common structure for light aircraft as well as business jets — legal due diligence must also cover the structure itself, not just the aircraft.

  • Confirmation that all co-owners or shareholders have given written consent to the sale, following the majority rules set out in the co-ownership agreement where applicable.
  • Verification that the owning company carries no debts or disputes (unpaid maintenance charges, litigation with a service provider).
  • Clarity on the nature of the deal: a transfer of shares in the owning company, or a direct transfer of the aircraft by the company — these two structures have very different legal and tax consequences.

Seller or aircraft located abroad: extra precautions

When the seller, the aircraft or the registration registry are located in a country other than the buyer's, due diligence must also cover the law applicable to the transaction and any customs or tax formalities tied to importing the aircraft. Rules of proof of ownership, searchable registries and required documents vary significantly from one country to another.

Tip — For any cross-border transaction, use a lawyer or advisor familiar with both the seller's country law and the law of the intended registration country, rather than a local generalist.

Checklist before signing

Before signing a final contract and paying the balance, make sure you have gathered and verified every item below. This list complements, without replacing, the technical checks carried out during the pre-purchase inspection.

  • Title of ownership confirmed, with a consistent chain of transfers up to the current seller.
  • No mortgage, pledge or lien registered, or a release obtained and verified.
  • Registration status current, with no pending suspension or deregistration procedure.
  • Written consent from all co-owners or shareholders in the case of shared ownership.
  • Sale contract reviewed by a legal professional, including the necessary warranties and conditions precedent.

Serious legal due diligence never replaces the technical inspection, but it protects against a different kind of risk: becoming the owner of an aircraft you could not, in reality, safely buy.
