Buying a Used Aircraft in Greece or Cyprus: Which Country to Choose

Market, prices, registration (HCAA or DCA), VAT and climate: a complete comparison between the two markets to help you decide where to search for and buy your used light aircraft in the eastern Mediterranean.

Why compare these two markets

Greece and Cyprus share a Mediterranean climate favourable to flying year-round, but their used-aircraft markets differ greatly in size and maturity. Greece benefits from a more developed network of flying clubs and schools, spread across the mainland and numerous islands, while Cyprus, a much smaller island member state, has a narrower but active market, driven mainly by flying schools and a handful of private operators.

For a European buyer, the choice between the two countries depends as much on available supply volume as on registration procedures and tax specifics unique to each state. This guide breaks down each dimension to help you point your search toward the market best suited to your project.

The Greek market in brief

The Greek used-aircraft market remains modest compared with the larger markets of northern Europe, but it is structured around a significant number of flying clubs and schools, particularly around Athens, Thessaloniki and some tourist islands. The supply consists mainly of classic touring single-engine aircraft (Cessna 172, Piper PA-28) and a growing microlight segment, driven by the rise of recreational flying.

The country's particular geography, with numerous inter-island routes, favours regular use of light aircraft, which sometimes translates into higher annual flight hours than the European average for locally based aircraft. This should be factored in when assessing the remaining airframe and engine potential of a given aircraft.

The Cypriot market in brief

The Cypriot market is markedly narrower, reflecting the country's small size and limited light-aircraft fleet. Used-aircraft supply there is sparse, concentrated mainly around flying schools based in Larnaca and Paphos, plus a handful of private owners. Buyers mostly find classic training single-engine aircraft, with a more limited choice of models and model years than in Greece.

This shallow market depth often means longer search times to find an aircraft matching specific criteria, and sometimes reduced negotiating leverage due to insufficient local benchmarks. Cypriot buyers frequently broaden their search to other European markets, notably Greece, to access a wider choice.

Price comparison by aircraft type

In both countries, price ranges remain broadly aligned with those seen elsewhere in the European Union for comparable aircraft, with a somewhat more developed entry-level supply in Greece thanks to the greater number of listings available. The real gap between two listings almost always depends more on airframe condition and remaining engine potential than on the country of origin.

Registration: HCAA versus DCA

In Greece, every aircraft based and operated within the country must be registered with the Hellenic Civil Aviation Authority (HCAA), under the SX- prefix. In Cyprus, the competent authority is the Department of Civil Aviation (DCA), which issues the 5B- prefix. In both cases, buying an aircraft already registered in the country results in a simple update of the registered owner; an aircraft imported from another country requires prior deregistration from the register of origin.

  • Valid certificate of airworthiness and ARC — check this before signing, in both countries.
  • Bill of sale — dated, signed by both parties, stating the serial number, required by both the HCAA and the DCA.
  • Liability insurance certificate — required before first use under the new registration.
Good to know — Common EASA rules govern the transfer between EU member states and make it simpler than an import from outside the EU. Generally allow 2 to 8 weeks in both countries, depending on how complete the submitted file is.

VAT and taxation in both countries

A sale between private individuals is generally not subject to VAT, either in Greece or in Cyprus. However, a sale carried out by a professional or a company may be subject to it in both countries, and an aircraft imported from a country outside the European Union remains subject to import VAT, calculated on its customs value. The main difference lies in the standard rate applicable when VAT is due: Cyprus applies one of the lowest standard rates in the European Union, while the Greek rate sits at a noticeably higher level. It is recommended to have your tax situation confirmed before acquisition, particularly if the aircraft has already changed country of registration several times.

Climate, infrastructure and real-world use

Both countries share a Mediterranean climate that allows flying a large number of days per year under stable conditions, a real benefit for training and for limiting wear linked to winter conditions. Greece, however, offers a markedly denser network of airfields, spread across the mainland and islands, along with a more developed fabric of approved maintenance workshops, which can make the technical follow-up of an aircraft easier once acquired. Cyprus, owing to its small size, concentrates its aviation infrastructure around a limited number of airfields.

For a buyer who does not intend to base the aircraft in the country of purchase, these considerations matter less: what remains essential is the quality of the technical file and the ease of the registration transfer to the destination country, two points that are comparable between the two markets thanks to the harmonised EASA framework.

Which country to choose based on your profile

If you are looking for the widest choice of listings to compare several similar aircraft and negotiate confidently, the Greek supply volume is a clear advantage. If you are already based in Cyprus or specifically want a locally registered aircraft, the Cypriot market remains worth exploring despite its more limited depth, broadening the search to Greece or other neighbouring markets if needed. In both cases, the rule remains the same: the actual condition of the aircraft and the consistency of its maintenance logbook matter more than its country of origin.

Tip — Whichever country you choose, never finalise a purchase without an independent pre-purchase inspection: it is the only reliable way to verify that the declared condition matches the aircraft's actual technical state.
