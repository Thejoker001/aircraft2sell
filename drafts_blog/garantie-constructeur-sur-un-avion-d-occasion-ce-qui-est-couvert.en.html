Manufacturer warranty on a used aircraft: what is covered

Parts, engine, airframe, avionics: understand exactly what remains covered under a manufacturer warranty after the first sale, and how to check it transfers before buying a used aircraft.

Understanding how manufacturer warranty works

A new aircraft leaves the factory with a manufacturer warranty, just like a car or any industrial equipment. This warranty covers manufacturing or material defects for a defined period, usually expressed in months or flight hours, whichever comes first. On the used market, the key question is not whether a warranty once existed, but whether it is still active and transferable at the time of purchase.

Each manufacturer (Cessna, Cirrus, Diamond, Piper, Beechcraft, or engine makers such as Continental and Rotax) sets its own durations and conditions. There is no single European standard: the only reliable source is the original warranty contract and its riders, not a general estimate.

What is typically covered

Aviation warranty contracts usually distinguish several separate scopes, each with its own duration and conditions.

  • Airframe and structure — manufacturing defects in the fuselage, wings and structural elements, subject to normal use of the aircraft.
  • Engine — often covered by a separate warranty from the engine maker (Continental, Lycoming, Rotax, Pratt & Whitney depending on the model), with its own engine-hour thresholds.
  • Avionics and electronic systems — generally warranted by the equipment maker (Garmin, Avidyne...) rather than the airframe manufacturer, often with a shorter duration.
  • Landing gear and flight controls — parts and assembly defects, excluding normal wear.
  • Propeller — covered separately by the propeller manufacturer, with its own duration.

The general rule is simple: each major component can have its own warrantor, its own duration and its own exclusions. An aircraft "still under warranty" is never fully covered on every system at the same time.

What is excluded in almost every case

Exclusions are almost always broader than the coverage itself. Before relying on a remaining warranty, you should systematically check the following points.

  • Normal wear of consumable parts (tires, brakes, filters, spark plugs) is never covered.
  • Damage from a hard landing, a runway excursion or a propeller strike is excluded, even during the warranty period.
  • Maintenance that does not follow the manufacturer's program, or that is carried out by a non-approved shop, can void all or part of the warranty.
  • Modifications or equipment added after delivery (third-party STCs, replacement avionics) often fall outside the original scope.
  • Commercial or rental use when the aircraft was sold for private use can void the warranty depending on the contract terms.
Word of caution — Never assume a warranty is "normally still active": only the contractual document, read in detail, states the exact conditions for keeping the coverage valid.

Warranty transfer to a second owner

A used aircraft changing hands while its warranty is still running does not automatically get the same transfer terms as the first buyer. Several manufacturers apply specific rules for resales.

  • Some contracts provide for an automatic, identical transfer if the aircraft is resold before a certain number of hours or months.
  • Others require formal notification to the manufacturer within a set deadline after the sale, or the remaining coverage is lost.
  • Some programs charge an administrative transfer fee, or reduce the transferred coverage period compared with the original duration.
  • Engine and propeller warranties, purchased separately, sometimes follow different transfer rules than the airframe warranty.

There is no universal rule applicable to all manufacturers: each case must be verified with the relevant manufacturer or engine maker, never assumed by analogy with another model.

How to verify remaining warranty before buying

Checking for an active remaining warranty should be part of the buying process just like the technical pre-purchase inspection. It relies on documents, not on the seller's word.

  • Request a copy of the original warranty certificate and all riders, not a verbal summary.
  • Check that the aircraft (or engine) serial number exactly matches the one listed on the warranty document.
  • Contact the manufacturer or its approved dealer directly with the serial number to confirm the real coverage status — a paper document can be outdated.
  • Cross-check the engine hours shown in the logbook against the warranty's hour threshold: a warranty based on months can remain active even if the hour threshold changed after a prior sale.
  • Verify that the maintenance history in the logbook follows the program required by the manufacturer to keep the warranty valid.
Tip — Include the manufacturer warranty check as part of the pre-purchase inspection carried out by your independent mechanic: they can confirm that the maintenance performed so far meets the contract's conditions.

Manufacturer warranty, engine warranty and insurance: don't confuse them

First-time buyers often confuse three distinct protections. The manufacturer warranty covers manufacturing defects for a limited period after production. An extended engine warranty (often offered as an option by the engine maker or a third party) covers mechanical failures beyond the original warranty, for a premium. Aircraft insurance covers accidental damage, theft or liability, independently of any manufacturing defect.

These three protections can coexist on the same aircraft without overlapping: an engine failure caused by a manufacturing defect falls under the manufacturer or engine maker's warranty, while a failure caused by an impact falls under insurance, and premature wear outside the warranty period may require a separately purchased extended warranty.

Remaining warranty: a real negotiation lever

A used aircraft still covered by a transferable manufacturer warranty represents real value, provided it can be documented and validated by the manufacturer before signing. This remaining warranty should factor into price negotiation, just like overall condition and remaining engine potential.

Conversely, a seller who cannot produce any warranty documentation, or who stays vague about the coverage status, should be treated as if the aircraft were no longer covered at all — the burden of proof always falls on whoever claims the warranty is still active.
