Corrosion on a used aircraft: how to detect it

Corrosion is one of the most costly — and hardest to spot — causes of devaluation on a used aircraft. Here's where to look, which signs should raise concern, and how a pre-purchase inspection tracks it down.

Why corrosion is a serious threat

On a used aircraft, corrosion is not a cosmetic issue: it attacks the structural strength of metal parts directly. A corroded airframe, spar or attachment fitting gradually loses its ability to carry the loads the manufacturer designed for, sometimes with no visible outward sign. This is exactly why buyers and approved mechanics fear this defect so much: unlike an engine failure that reveals itself in flight, corrosion progresses silently.

Repair cost varies enormously depending on which part is affected and how far the damage has spread. A localized surface treatment stays affordable; replacing a spar, a wing attachment fitting or part of a fuselage, on the other hand, can represent a bill equal to a significant share of the aircraft's value, or even lead to an economic write-off. That's why checking for corrosion should be an absolute priority during any pre-purchase inspection, even before discussing price.

The most exposed areas on an aircraft

Certain areas account for the majority of corrosion cases found on light aircraft, because they combine moisture, friction or electrolytic contact between different metals.

  • Fuselage belly and floors — where moisture, fuel residue and de-icing fluid tend to collect.
  • Wing spars and ribs — hard to access, inspection often limited to inspection panels.
  • Landing gear attachments — exposed to water, salt and runway chemical splash.
  • Around the battery — chemical corrosion linked to electrolyte fumes in case of a leak.
  • Junctions between different materials — rivets, screws or steel fittings in contact with aluminium, prone to galvanic corrosion.
  • Exhaust and engine compartment — heat, soot and condensation speed up corrosion on nearby sheet metal.

The different types of corrosion to know

Not all types of corrosion look alike, and some are far harder to detect than others.

Uniform corrosion

The easiest to spot: a generalized surface attack, often linked to prolonged exposure to moisture without adequate protection. It shows up as a dull, powdery or whitish appearance on aluminium.

Pitting corrosion

This creates small, localized craters that may look harmless on the surface but extend deep into the metal, weakening the part far more than its outward appearance suggests.

Intergranular corrosion

The most insidious form: it progresses inside the metal's own structure, along the grain boundaries, without always producing a visible surface sign. It particularly affects certain ageing aluminium alloys and often requires non-destructive testing to confirm.

Galvanic corrosion

This appears where two different metals touch in the presence of moisture (for example a steel screw on an aluminium structure), creating an electrochemical reaction that preferentially attacks one of the two metals.

Filiform corrosion

Typical of painted or coated surfaces, it spreads as thin filaments under the coating, often starting from a paint chip or an untreated scratch.

Visual warning signs to watch for

Even without disassembly, several visual clues let a trained eye suspect active or past corrosion.

  • Whitish or powdery deposits on aluminium, blistering or bubbling under the paint.
  • Recent, localized paint on a single area, inconsistent with the rest of the airframe — a possible sign of a hidden repair.
  • Loose rivets, reddish streaking around steel fasteners.
  • New sealant or filler on a single panel only, always worth checking.
  • Persistent damp smell in the fuselage or cabin, often revealing chronic leaks.
Word of caution — An aircraft fully repainted just before being listed for sale is not necessarily a red flag, but it's a good reason to systematically ask for invoices and a breakdown of the work done before negotiating.

Factors that promote corrosion

Corrosion risk depends heavily on the aircraft's life history, far more than on its age in years or displayed flight hours.

  • Long-term outdoor parking, especially in a humid climate or near the coast.
  • Irregular use — a rarely flown aircraft builds up more internal condensation than one flown regularly.
  • Frequent flights over the sea without systematically rinsing the airframe with fresh water.
  • Storage in an unheated or poorly ventilated hangar, which encourages condensation cycles.
  • Neglected preventive maintenance — skipping the periodic anti-corrosion treatments recommended by the manufacturer.

Asking the seller about the aircraft's geographical history (country, proximity to the coast, type of hangar) is therefore just as much a part of due diligence as reading the logbook.

How a mechanic searches for corrosion

During a pre-purchase inspection, the search for corrosion follows a progressive method, moving from a visual check toward deeper testing tools if any doubt remains.

  • Full visual inspection through the inspection panels, with a flashlight and sometimes an inspection mirror.
  • Use of a borescope to examine areas inaccessible without disassembly (spars, wing boxes).
  • Tap testing or pick testing to detect delamination or a weakened area under the paint.
  • Non-destructive testing (dye penetrant, eddy current) on critical parts if doubt persists.
  • Cross-checking with the logbook: any prior mention of treated corrosion must be documented and verified on site.
Tip — Have this inspection carried out by an independent mechanic familiar with the aircraft type, never by a provider recommended by the seller, and ask for a written report detailing every area examined.

Corrosion found: consequences and negotiation

Not all corrosion carries the same severity. Surface corrosion that's superficial and cheap to treat is a very different matter from corrosion affecting a structural part, which can call the whole purchase into question.

Situation foundSeverityLikely impact on negotiation
Light surface corrosion, outside structural areasLowLocal treatment to budget for, minor price reduction possible
Pitting corrosion on a non-structural partModeratePart replacement, negotiate on the repair cost
Intergranular corrosion or corrosion on a structural part (spar, fitting)HighFurther expert assessment required, major renegotiation or walking away
Untreated corrosion with gaps in the logbook on the subjectHighA broader red flag on the aircraft's overall maintenance rigor

Either way, an inspection report costing out the repair is your best negotiating argument — or, if needed, the best reason to walk away without regret from a deal that's simply too risky.

Frequently asked questions

Is corrosion always visible to the naked eye?

No. Surface corrosion can be visible, but intergranular corrosion or corrosion hidden under a coating, inside a spar or behind a trim panel stays invisible without partial disassembly, a borescope or non-destructive testing.

Is an aircraft stored near the coast more exposed?

Yes. Salt air strongly accelerates corrosion, especially on metal-structure aircraft that are poorly protected or not rinsed after flying over the sea. The aircraft's basing history is a key point to ask the seller about.

Does detected corrosion automatically rule out the purchase?

Not necessarily. Minor surface corrosion, treated in time, can remain without consequence. The extent, whether it affects a structural part, and the treatment method determine the severity, to be assessed by an independent approved mechanic.
