Pilatus PC-12 vs Cessna Caravan: which turboprop single to choose

Two turboprop singles, two philosophies

The Pilatus PC-12 and the Cessna Caravan (208) have dominated the turboprop single segment for decades, yet they follow different design logics. The PC-12 was conceived as a comfortable, high-performing passenger transport aircraft, sitting somewhere between a light business jet and a versatile turboprop. The Caravan, by contrast, was developed above all for operational ruggedness: the ability to operate from unprepared strips, simple maintenance, and suitability for both cargo and passenger work.

Choosing between the two therefore comes down to defining the intended mission precisely: comfort and cruise performance for passenger transport from prepared airfields, or versatility and ruggedness for mixed missions, light cargo, or operations on unpaved strips.

It is also worth noting that, although these two aircraft are often grouped in the same commercial segment of turboprop singles, they overlap only partially in terms of customer base: the PC-12 targets an owner or operator looking for a level of service close to business aviation, while the Caravan appeals more to commercial or utility operators for whom aircraft availability and controlled operating costs take priority over cabin comfort.

The Pilatus PC-12: versatility and prestige

The PC-12 has established itself as a benchmark in private and regional transport thanks to its spacious pressurized cabin, extended range and cruise performance superior to the Caravan's. Its interior, closer in feel to a light business jet, makes it a preferred choice for executive transport, regional executive links, or air ambulance missions requiring comfort and speed.

On the used market, several generations of the PC-12 are available, with notable evolutions in avionics, engine and cabin fit-out over the years. This diversity means aircraft can be found across a wide range of budgets, but it also requires carefully checking the generation and upgrades each specific aircraft has received.

The PC-12's reputation also rests on its mission versatility: beyond private transport, it is widely used for air ambulance work, certain regional commercial links and government missions, which speaks to the ruggedness of its airframe despite its comfort-oriented positioning.

The Cessna Caravan: ruggedness and simplicity

The Cessna Caravan was designed for intensive, versatile use: cargo transport, regional links, skydiving operations, humanitarian missions or operations from unprepared strips. Its unpressurized airframe, relatively simple mechanics and reinforced fixed gear make it a favourite among operators who prioritize availability and ease of maintenance above all.

Stretched-cabin versions (Grand Caravan) are also available, offering greater payload capacity and further widening the range of possible missions. On the used market, the Caravan benefits from a large global fleet and a very extensive parts and maintenance network, which generally makes day-to-day operation easier.

This wide global distribution also translates into a great variety of available used configurations: pure cargo versions, mixed passenger/cargo versions, fit-outs specific to skydiving or government missions. A buyer should therefore clearly identify the actual configuration of the aircraft being considered, which can significantly influence its future resale value.

Budget comparison: purchase and operation

At a comparable age and specification, the PC-12's acquisition budget is generally higher than the Caravan's, consistent with its pressurized cabin, superior cruise performance and closer positioning to business aviation. The Caravan, being simpler in design and unpressurized, typically offers a more accessible entry price and lower operating costs per flight hour, which makes it a frequent choice for commercial or utility operators.

Beyond the sticker or asking price, it is always worth factoring in the real cost per flight hour, which depends on the aircraft's age, remaining engine potential before overhaul, and the planned utilization rate.

The cost structure of these two aircraft also differs on a point that is often underestimated: the PC-12's pressurization adds systems (pressurization pack, seals, specific periodic checks) that represent an extra maintenance line compared with the Caravan, whose lack of pressurization simplifies the list of checks to budget for each year.

Which aircraft for which mission

The choice between the PC-12 and the Caravan should start with the aircraft's actual intended mission. For regular passenger transport on regional routes, or a use case close to business aviation, the PC-12 offers a clear advantage in comfort, cruise speed and image.

  • PC-12: well suited to passenger transport, executive links and missions requiring a comfortable pressurized cabin.
  • Caravan: suited to cargo, operations on unprepared strips, skydiving and intensive utility missions.
  • Operating budget per flight hour: often more decisive than the initial purchase price.
  • Need for raw payload capacity (bulky cargo, modular configuration) rather than cabin comfort.

Conversely, an operator looking above all for ruggedness, availability and controlled maintenance costs will find in the Caravan an economic case that is hard to match, particularly for mixed passenger/cargo missions or demanding airfields.

Between these two extreme profiles, many buyers settle the question based on secondary criteria: an occasional need to carry bulky cargo, the frequency of flights to unprepared airfields, or simply a preference for the comfort of a pressurized cabin on longer trips. Taking the time to list the missions genuinely expected over the next two to three years remains the best way to guide this choice.

Used market and resale value

Both aircraft benefit from a substantial global fleet and strong liquidity on the used market, which generally makes resale easier than for less common models. The PC-12 tends to retain a high residual value on more recent generations, particularly those fitted with the most modern avionics, while the Caravan benefits from steady demand from commercial and utility operators worldwide, including older examples still actively in service.

In both cases, comparing several listings of similar generations and specifications remains the best way to assess a fair price on the European market.

Buying either one used: points to watch

Whether it is a PC-12 or a Caravan, the aircraft's commercial or utility usage history deserves particular attention: total flight hours, the type of missions flown (cargo, skydiving, operations on unprepared strips) and the rigour of maintenance tracking. For the PC-12, check the avionics generation and any cabin upgrades carried out. For the Caravan, the condition of the landing gear and airframe after intensive use on rough strips should be examined closely.

Tip — Always have the pre-purchase inspection carried out by an independent mechanic familiar with the type, and request the complete flight logbook and maintenance invoices before committing.

In all cases, the annual operating cost should be assessed precisely before buying, as it varies significantly between these two turboprops depending on the intended mission.
