Buying a Used Aircraft in Serbia or Hungary: Which Country to Choose

Markets, prices, registration, taxation and pitfalls to avoid: a complete comparison to help you choose between Serbia and Hungary for buying a used light aircraft.

Two light-aviation markets with different profiles

Serbia and Hungary share a common border but have fairly different light-aviation market structures. Hungary, a member of both the European Union and the Schengen area, has an active network of airfields and a long-standing recreational aviation tradition, with a used-aircraft supply made up mainly of classic single-engine aircraft and gliders, a legacy of a historically well-developed gliding community in the country.

Serbia, outside the European Union, has a smaller market in volume but an active one, driven by a handful of significant flying clubs and a supply that includes both Western-designed aircraft and aircraft of Eastern European origin, sometimes less sought after elsewhere in Europe but robust and well known to local mechanics.

Prices and aircraft availability

In Hungary, prices for classic aircraft (light singles, gliders) remain broadly aligned with the Central European average, with relatively liquid supply for the most common models. In Serbia, availability of a sought-after Western model can be more limited, but some aircraft trade at a more accessible entry level, particularly Eastern European-origin aircraft that are less prized on the Western European market.

Tip — As elsewhere in Europe, the asking price depends above all on engine history (remaining engine hours before the major overhaul) and the condition of the logbook, far more than on the registration country itself.

Registration: a non-EU aircraft vs a European register

The most structural difference between the two countries concerns the regulatory framework for registration. Hungary applies the common rules of the European Union Aviation Safety Agency (EASA), which simplifies the transfer of an aircraft already registered in another member state: the procedure essentially consists of deregistration from the register of origin followed by new Hungarian registration.

Serbia, outside the European Union, has its own national register and its own airworthiness requirements, managed by its civil aviation authority. Importing an aircraft registered in an EU member state onto the Serbian register, or the reverse, involves a more extensive file than a simple intra-EU transfer, with certificate of airworthiness (CofA) procedures to be handled under the Serbian framework rather than the European one.

  • Hungary — simplified transfer between EASA states, standard file (certificate of airworthiness, ARC, bill of sale, insurance certificate).
  • Serbia — procedures specific to the national Serbian register, to be anticipated with more margin in the timeline, especially for an aircraft coming from the EU.

Taxation and customs regime

In Hungary, a sale between EU-resident private individuals generally follows the same principles as elsewhere in the Union: no Hungarian VAT for a standard private transaction, subject to each case's specific tax situation. A sale by a professional or a company, however, may be subject to VAT.

In Serbia, a non-EU country, an aircraft imported from the European Union or exported to the EU must be treated as an international customs operation, with corresponding import or export formalities and taxation specific to the Serbian regime. It is recommended to have your tax and customs situation confirmed before any cross-border transaction between the two countries or with a third country.

Point of caution — Never extend the tax rules of an EU member state to a non-EU country like Serbia: the two customs regimes are fundamentally different and must be checked separately with the competent authorities of each country.

Pre-purchase inspection and documents to check

In both countries, a pre-purchase inspection by a mechanic independent of the seller remains the best protection against unpleasant surprises, whether in an EASA-governed market or a non-EU national register. The points to check remain similar in nature, even though the regulatory frameworks differ.

  • Complete airframe and engine logbook, with no chronological gaps, regardless of the aircraft's country of origin.
  • Engine hours elapsed and status relative to the major overhaul (SMOH), key information for assessing future operating costs.
  • Validity of the certificate of airworthiness (CofA) under the framework of the aircraft's current registration country.
  • For an Eastern European-origin aircraft in Serbia, availability of spare parts and technical support from local workshops familiar with the type.

Logistics: transport, ferry flights and language

Hungary, part of the Schengen area, makes it easier to travel for an in-person viewing of the aircraft and for a possible ferry flight to another EU country, with no customs formality at the border. Serbia, outside Schengen and the EU, requires planning for the customs and immigration formalities of an international trip, both for a buyer travelling there and for ferrying the aircraft once the sale is completed.

On the language side, English is generally well spoken in aviation circles in both countries, but local administrative and technical documents may require translation, particularly for a file intended for a foreign authority after the purchase.

Pitfalls to avoid in both countries

In Hungary, the most common mistake remains neglecting to verify actual deregistration from the register of origin for an imported aircraft, a classic error in any poorly prepared intra-EU transfer. In Serbia, the most common pitfall is underestimating the time and complexity of a non-EU import or export procedure, or signing a bill of sale without first having the aircraft's technical file confirmed as compliant with the Serbian framework.

Tip — In both cases, never sign a bill of sale before seeing the original logbooks and having a professional confirm the consistency between the technical file and the targeted registration status.

Which country to choose for your project

For a buyer who prioritises administrative simplicity and an easier future resale within the European Union, Hungary offers a more predictable regulatory framework, inherited from the common EASA rules. For a buyer more drawn to an Eastern European-origin aircraft at a more accessible budget, or who is willing to manage a more complete import or export procedure, Serbia can be an interesting option, provided the administrative timelines are anticipated and the customs file is validated in advance.

In any case, comparing several similar listings remains the best way to assess a fair price, whether the aircraft is based in Hungary, Serbia or elsewhere in Europe.
