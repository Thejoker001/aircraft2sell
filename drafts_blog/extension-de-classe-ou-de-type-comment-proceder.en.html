Class or type rating extension: how to proceed

Just bought (or considering buying) an aircraft of a model you have never flown before? Here is the procedure for legally obtaining the right to fly it, whether it requires a class rating extension or a type rating.

Why an extension is required before flying a new model

A private pilot licence (PPL) or light aircraft pilot licence (LAPL) does not automatically grant the right to fly any aircraft. Every licence is issued with privileges limited to a specific class of aircraft (single-engine piston land, for example) and, for more complex or heavier aircraft, to a specific type. Buying a used aircraft outside your current class — a twin when you have only flown single-engines, or a model with retractable gear and a variable-pitch propeller when your experience is limited to fixed-gear basics — therefore requires additional training before you can legally act as pilot in command.

This requirement is not mere administrative formality: it addresses a genuine safety need. Every family of aircraft has its own handling characteristics, specific emergency procedures and potential pitfalls for a pilot discovering the model for the first time. Skipping this step, or treating it lightly, exposes you to real safety risk as well as legal risk in the event of an inspection or an incident.

Class extension vs. type rating: what's the difference

European regulations distinguish two regimes depending on the complexity and mass of the aircraft in question.

  • Class rating extension — applies to relatively simple single-engine or multi-engine aircraft, grouped into broad families (single-engine piston land, multi-engine piston land, single-engine seaplane, etc.). A single class rating covers every model within that family.
  • Type rating — required for more complex or heavier aircraft (above a given maximum mass threshold, or multi-pilot aircraft), where each specific model requires its own distinct rating, not transferable to another type even a closely related one.
  • Between the two, certain high-performance single-engine aircraft (high power, retractable gear, variable-pitch propeller, pressurised cabin) require additional differences training within the class itself, without going as far as a separate type rating.

In practice, the vast majority of light touring and travel aircraft sold used (classic single-engine piston aircraft, light piston twins) fall under the class rating extension regime, which is simpler and faster to obtain than a type rating.

Extending your licence to a new aircraft class

To add a new class to your licence, training is generally carried out with an instructor qualified on the model in question, within a declared training organisation (DTO) or an approved training organisation (ATO). It includes a theoretical component focused on the specifics of the new aircraft (limitations, systems, performance) and a practical flight component, whose duration varies considerably depending on your prior experience and how close the new model is to aircraft you already fly.

A pilot moving from a simple fixed-gear single-engine to a single-engine with retractable gear and a variable-pitch propeller will typically require shorter training than a pilot approaching a multi-engine aircraft for the first time, where asymmetric engine failure procedures and twin-specific weight-and-balance management come into play. At the end of the training, a skill test (a practical flight check, sometimes called a check ride) with an examiner validates the new class, which is then entered on your licence by the competent authority.

Good to know — A class rating you already hold remains valid as long as you meet recency requirements (recent flight experience, a current medical certificate): a class extension is a one-off step, not a periodic renewal in itself.

Obtaining a type rating

A type rating mainly concerns complex or multi-pilot aircraft, less common in the used market aimed at private owners but present for certain turboprops and business jets. The training path is more structured than for a class extension: it often relies on a flight simulator dedicated to the exact type of aircraft, in addition to real flight training, with a generally higher volume of theoretical and practical hours.

Unlike a class rating, a type rating is generally not transferable to another model, even one from a closely related manufacturer or a later generation of the same aircraft: each distinct type requires its own rating, although some manufacturers offer streamlined differences training between very similar variants of the same base type.

Cost and timeline to expect

The cost and duration of an extension depend heavily on the model targeted, the training organisation chosen and your starting experience. A class extension on a classic single-engine aircraft is generally measured in a few flight hours spread over a few days to a few weeks, for a budget significantly lower than that of an initial licence. A type rating on a complex aircraft represents a noticeably higher investment in time and budget, largely due to the use of a dedicated simulator and a denser theoretical programme.

For a precise budget tailored to your case, the most reliable approach is to contact a training organisation (DTO/ATO) directly approved for the model in question: rates vary by country, by organisation and by the availability of the aircraft or simulator used for training.

Planning the extension before a used purchase

If you are considering buying a used aircraft outside your current class, it is advisable to plan this step before finalising the transaction rather than afterwards. There are several practical reasons for this: confirming that an instructor or training organisation qualified on this exact model is available within a reasonable distance, estimating the real budget and timeline to obtain the extension, and avoiding becoming the owner of an aircraft you cannot yet legally fly for several weeks.

  • Contact a training organisation qualified on the model in question before signing, to confirm feasibility and timeline.
  • Include the training cost in the overall purchase budget, alongside insurance or the pre-purchase inspection.
  • Check that your medical certificate and recent flight experience are current, often prerequisites before starting training.
  • If possible, take one or more introductory flights on the model before buying, to confirm it genuinely matches your flying expectations.

Common mistakes to avoid

The first mistake is underestimating the time required and signing the purchase expecting to fly "within the week" on a model significantly different from your current experience. The second is neglecting differences training on a high-performance aircraft by relying solely on a generic class extension, when specific systems (retractable gear, variable pitch, pressurisation) require genuine practical familiarisation, regardless of the strict regulatory requirement.

Point to watch — Never rely solely on the hours shown in the seller's logbook to estimate your own proficiency on a model: your extension must be validated by an instructor qualified on that exact type of aircraft, never inferred from the previous owner's experience.

Finally, always check with the civil aviation authority of your country of registration or licence-issuing state for the exact procedure applicable to your case: the precise requirements (minimum hours, skill test format) can evolve and depend on your individual experience, which no generic guide can replace.
