Why the DR300 is still sought after

The Robin DR300 belongs to the line of low-wing wood-and-fabric aircraft developed by Avions Pierre Robin in the 1970s, in direct continuity with the earlier DR250/DR253 and in parallel with the famous DR400 that would later succeed it. Recognisable by its roomy cabin, its "Jodel"-type combined ailerons and flaps, and its particularly light mixed wood-and-fabric structure, the DR300 still appeals to owner-pilots and flying clubs looking for an economical, pleasant-to-fly four-seater, equally well suited to touring and local flying.

On the used market, the DR300 comes in several versions depending on the original engine and equipment. Its popularity in France and neighbouring countries makes it a relatively common type, with a large enough installed fleet to find parts and mechanics familiar with it, which makes day-to-day operation considerably easier than with rarer aircraft.

DR300 variants and their uses

The DR300 was produced in several versions distinguished mainly by engine power and some cabin fittings. The more modestly powered versions suit training or light touring use well, while the more powerful versions offer better loaded performance, useful for family trips with baggage or for longer legs.

Before buying, it is worth precisely identifying the variant on offer and comparing its airworthiness file with the information given by the seller: some examples may have been modified or re-engined over the decades, which should be documented in the aircraft's technical file.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used DR300 depends above all on the age of the airframe, engine hours and proximity to a major overhaul, the condition of the fabric and structure, and the level of avionics fitted. The oldest examples, with reduced engine potential and basic avionics, trade at an accessible entry level, while well-restored aircraft with recently re-covered fabric represent a larger investment.

Beyond the purchase price, your budget should include ancillary costs: an independent mechanic's pre-purchase inspection by someone qualified on the type, registration and transfer fees, insurance, and possibly a fabric or cabin interior refresh if the aircraft needs one.

What to check before buying

As with any used aircraft, a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic qualified on the type is essential before finalising a transaction. For a DR300, a few points deserve particular attention because of its wood-and-fabric construction.

  • Overall condition of the wood structure: look for any traces of moisture, rot or poorly documented repairs, which can be hard to detect without a specialist inspection.
  • Condition of the fabric covering and the date of the last full re-covering, a significant expense to plan for if it is approaching the end of its life.
  • Complete engine history: hours elapsed since the last major overhaul, compliance with the manufacturer's maintenance program, traceability of replaced parts.
  • Condition and compliance of the installed avionics with current regulatory requirements.
  • Complete logbook and technical file, with no grey areas in the aircraft's history or its certificate of airworthiness.
Good to know — Never rely solely on the seller's statements about overall condition: a written independent inspection report, including a careful look at the wood structure and fabric covering, remains the best protection against a bad surprise after the purchase.

Wood-and-fabric structure: what to know

The wood-and-fabric construction of the DR300 is one of its most notable features compared with metal aircraft of the same era. It gives a light structure and well-regarded flying qualities, but requires specific maintenance attention: monitoring moisture, periodically checking glued joints, and re-covering the fabric on a schedule that depends on use, storage and climate conditions.

An aircraft kept in a hangar and maintained by a shop experienced with wood-and-fabric construction generally keeps its structure in better condition over time than one parked outdoors. Before buying, ask the seller where and how the aircraft has been stored over the years — this information is often more revealing than the flight hours alone.

Maintenance and operating costs

The operating cost of a DR300 remains comparable to other light four-seaters in its category, with one specific point related to the care of its wood-and-fabric structure. The main expense items remain the mandatory annual inspection, insurance, parking and, in the longer term, the engine's major overhaul once the hourly potential is reached.

Having the aircraft maintained at an approved shop experienced with this type of construction helps limit unpleasant surprises and preserve its resale value. Rigorous, well-documented maintenance records, especially for anything related to the structure and fabric covering, are a strong argument at a future resale.

Tip — Always ask for the last three maintenance invoices before making an offer: they give an accurate picture of how seriously the aircraft has been maintained, particularly regarding structural work.

Resale and the used market

The DR300 benefits from an active used market, particularly in France where it remains well established among flying clubs and private owners. A well-maintained aircraft, with recently re-covered fabric, a transparent history and a complete logbook, generally resells more easily and on better terms than one whose technical file has grey areas.

Comparing several listings of the DR300 and related models such as the DR400 remains the best way to assess a fair price, both for buying and for preparing a future resale. Price differences between two aircraft of the same model year are almost always explained by the condition of the fabric covering, remaining engine potential and the quality of the avionics fitted.
