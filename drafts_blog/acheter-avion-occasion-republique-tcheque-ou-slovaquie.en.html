Buying a Used Aircraft in the Czech Republic or Slovakia: Which Country to Choose

Market, prices, registration, taxation and pitfalls to avoid: a complete comparison to help you decide where to buy your used light aircraft between the Czech Republic and Slovakia.

Two neighbours, two very different market sizes

The Czech Republic and Slovakia share a long common history and apply the same EASA regulatory framework as EU members, but their used aircraft markets have nothing in common in terms of volume. The Czech Republic hosts a genuine light aviation industry: several microlight and light aircraft manufacturers are based there (Evektor, Zlin Aviation, Shark Aero, TL-ULM, among others), which feeds a dense, varied second-hand market, especially active in microlights, motor gliders and touring single-engines.

Slovakia has a much smaller market, concentrated around a handful of key airfields such as Bratislava, Piešťany or Žilina. Supply there is dominated by gliders and microlights, often coming from former flying clubs, with less variety in high-end single-engines or twins. Slovak buyers frequently widen their search to the neighbouring Czech Republic, precisely because the choice is wider for an equivalent budget.

For a buyer who values depth of supply and the ability to compare several similar aircraft, the Czech Republic generally remains the richer destination in Central Europe. For a buyer looking above all for a simple aircraft at a contained price, Slovakia can offer good opportunities, provided a longer search is accepted.

Price comparison by aircraft type

Prices in the Czech Republic are broadly close to, or even slightly lower than, those seen in Slovakia for comparable categories, especially for microlights where local Czech production drags prices down thanks to an abundant supply of recent used models. Engine history (hours remaining before major overhaul) and the state of the avionics always remain the most decisive factors, far ahead of the country of sale alone.

Registration: ÚCL versus Dopravný úrad

In the Czech Republic, civil aircraft registration is handled by the Úřad pro civilní letectví (ÚCL), the Czech civil aviation authority, which assigns the OK- prefix. In Slovakia, it is the Dopravný úrad (Transport Authority of the Slovak Republic) that keeps the national register, under the OM- prefix. In both countries, a purchase between private individuals where the aircraft is already registered locally simply requires updating the registered owner, while an aircraft imported from another country must first be deregistered from its register of origin.

  • Valid certificate of airworthiness and ARC — check this before signing, in both countries.
  • Bill of sale — dated, signed by both parties, stating the serial number, required by both the ÚCL and the Dopravný úrad.
  • Liability insurance certificate — required before first use under the new registration, in both the Czech Republic and Slovakia.
Good to know — Both countries apply the same common EASA rules for registration transfers between EU member states: expect 2 to 8 weeks depending on how complete the file submitted to the ÚCL or the Dopravný úrad is.

VAT and taxation: two similar intra-EU regimes

Since the Czech Republic and Slovakia are both EU members, VAT treatment follows the same common logic in both countries: a sale between EU resident private individuals is generally VAT-exempt, while a sale by a professional or a company may be subject to it. An aircraft imported from a non-EU country remains subject to import VAT in both cases, calculated on its customs value at the applicable national rate.

The practical difference between the two countries mainly lies in the availability of professionals used to cross-border formalities: the more mature and more international Czech market, thanks to its local aviation industry, has more brokers, accountants and workshops specialised in international aircraft acquisition than the Slovak market.

Maintenance, workshops and amateur-built aircraft

This is one of the areas where the gap between the two countries is clearest. The Czech Republic has a dense network of Part-145 and Part-CAO approved workshops, particularly around the industrial sites linked to local manufacturers, with excellent parts availability for models produced in the country. The Czech tradition of amateur building and microlight maintenance also makes it easier to access experienced technicians for this type of aircraft.

Slovakia, with a smaller market, has a more limited workshop network, mainly concentrated around the main airfields. For some specific models or rare parts, it is not uncommon to need a technician to travel, or to send the aircraft to the Czech Republic for a major overhaul or complex repair, the geographical proximity and historical ties between the two countries making this a natural solution.

Pre-purchase inspection and documents

Whether the aircraft is Czech or Slovak, a pre-purchase inspection by a certified mechanic independent of the seller remains the best protection against unpleasant surprises. It covers the condition of the airframe, the consistency of the engine logbook, maintenance history and the compliance of the avionics with current European requirements (transponder, 8.33 kHz radio), common to both countries thanks to the application of EASA standards.

  • Up-to-date airframe, engine and propeller logbooks, with no chronological gaps, whether kept in the Czech Republic or Slovakia.
  • History of maintenance invoices from the last three years; plan for a translation if the logbooks are written in Czech or Slovak.
  • No undisclosed structural damage, verifiable via the maintenance file and a thorough visual inspection.
Point of caution — Never finalise a purchase, in the Czech Republic or in Slovakia, without having seen the original logbooks and having their consistency checked by a professional independent of the seller.

Which country to choose depending on your profile

The choice between the Czech Republic and Slovakia depends above all on your project and your budget. The Czech Republic appeals to buyers looking for a large, varied market, a dense maintenance network backed by a genuine local aviation industry, and good resale liquidity, especially for microlights and motor gliders. Slovakia can suit a buyer looking for a simple aircraft at an attractive entry price, accepting a more limited supply and a less dense maintenance network, which sometimes involves travelling to the Czech Republic for some maintenance work.

In all cases, the decisive factor remains the quality of maintenance and the transparency of the aircraft's history, far more than the country of registration alone. Comparing several listings in both countries, at an equivalent budget and category, remains the best way to make an informed choice.

Tip — If you are considering an import between the two countries or from another EU country, have the administrative formalities confirmed in advance by a specialist professional: this avoids unpleasant surprises after signing.
