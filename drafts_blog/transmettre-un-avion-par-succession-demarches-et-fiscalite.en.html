Passing on an aircraft through inheritance: procedures and tax

Legal status, documents to gather, procedures with the civil aviation authority, applicable tax rules and managing joint ownership: what you need to know when an aircraft is part of an estate.

Why planning ahead for an aircraft's inheritance matters

A touring aircraft, a microlight or a share in an aircraft co-ownership often represents significant value within an estate, but it also brings specific complications when it comes to inheritance. Unlike a property or a bank account, an aircraft keeps generating fixed costs (insurance, parking, maintenance) even while grounded, and its value can deteriorate quickly without regular upkeep during what is often a long probate process.

Planning ahead, either during the owner's lifetime or as soon as the estate is opened, helps prevent the aircraft from losing value or becoming a source of conflict between heirs, especially when they have different wishes: keeping the aircraft, selling it quickly, or transferring it to a single heir in exchange for financial compensation to the others.

An aircraft is a specific kind of movable asset in an estate

Legally, an aircraft is a movable asset, much like a car or a boat, and it therefore enters the estate's assets just like any other property of the deceased. It must be declared in the estate inventory drawn up by the notary, with a value estimated as of the date of death, usually produced by an expert or based on comparable listings on the used-aircraft market.

If the aircraft was co-owned with other pilots, only the deceased's share enters the estate: the other co-owners keep their respective shares, but the co-ownership agreement should be reviewed carefully, since some include a pre-emption right for the other co-owners in the event of a co-owner's death.

Documents the heir needs to gather

As soon as the estate is opened, it is worth gathering the documents that precisely identify the aircraft and its administrative status, even before deciding on its future.

  • Certificate of airworthiness (CofA) and certificate of registration — to confirm the aircraft is still in order and identify the relevant authority.
  • Flight logbook and maintenance logbook — a complete history of flights and work carried out, essential for valuing the aircraft and for any future sale.
  • Engine hours and SMOH — time since the last major overhaul, a key factor in both value and the cost of returning the aircraft to service.
  • Current insurance policy — notify the insurer of the owner's death without delay, to avoid any gap in cover during probate.
  • Co-ownership agreement or the articles of the holding structure — if one exists, to understand the clauses that apply on a shareholder's death.

Steps with the civil aviation authority after a death

The owner's death must be notified to the civil aviation authority that issued the registration, so that the register reflects the actual ownership situation while the estate is being settled. Depending on the country of registration, this notification may be a simple declarative step or require a death certificate and a notarial document confirming heir status.

Until the estate is settled, the aircraft generally cannot be resold or transferred to a new owner: only once the register has been updated in the name of the heir or heirs (or the estate's joint ownership) can a sale, a lifetime gift-split or a registration transfer to another country be considered. It is advisable to liaise with the notary handling the estate and, in parallel, with the civil aviation authority, to coordinate the timelines specific to each procedure.

Applicable tax: inheritance tax and VAT

The aircraft's value forms part of the estate's taxable assets and is therefore subject to inheritance tax under the rules applicable in the deceased's country of tax residence, just like any other movable asset. The applicable rates and allowances depend on the family relationship between the deceased and each heir, as well as any relevant international tax treaties if the aircraft or the heirs are located in different countries.

Inheritance itself is not, by its nature, a transaction subject to VAT: it is a transfer of ownership with no financial consideration between the deceased and the heirs. The question of VAT can, however, arise later if the aircraft is subsequently sold by the heirs to a third party, depending on the seller's status (private individual or professional) and the rules applicable in the relevant country. Using a notary, and for complex or international estates a tax adviser specialised in aviation, helps secure this step.

Managing joint ownership between heirs before a sale or split

When several heirs are involved, the aircraft becomes jointly owned as soon as the estate is opened: each heir holds a theoretical share of the aircraft, and none of them can dispose of it alone. This situation, usually temporary, needs careful handling because fixed costs (insurance, hangarage, preventive maintenance) keep accruing and should in principle be shared between the joint owners in proportion to their shares.

Three outcomes are generally possible: selling the aircraft to a third party and splitting the proceeds between the heirs, one heir buying out the others' shares to keep the aircraft, or maintaining an organised joint ownership under a written agreement if several heirs wish to keep sharing its use. In the latter two cases, an independent technical inspection and an up-to-date valuation remain advisable to set a fair buy-out price or share value between the parties.

Good to know — If heirs remain in persistent disagreement over the aircraft's future, a court-ordered sale remains the default outcome under general inheritance law; in most cases it is far better to reach an amicable agreement, since contentious proceedings significantly extend both the timeline and the fixed costs to be covered.

Planning ahead: gifting, holding structures and insurance

To avoid having to deal with these questions under pressure at the time of death, several tools allow an owner to plan an aircraft's transfer during their lifetime. An early lifetime gift-split lets the owner distribute ownership of the aircraft between prospective heirs on terms of their own choosing, often with a more favourable tax treatment than a transfer on death if done early enough.

Holding the aircraft through a dedicated structure (a civil company or equivalent depending on the country) can also simplify the transfer, by substituting the transfer of company shares for the transfer of the aircraft itself, with governance rules set in advance for the transition period. Finally, checking that the aircraft's insurance policy properly covers the period between death and the settlement of the estate, and keeping a complete, centralised technical file up to date, remains the best way to limit complications for the heirs when the time comes.

Tip — Before making any final decision about the aircraft's future, have an independent mechanic carry out a pre-purchase-style inspection if you intend to keep it: an aircraft left grounded for a long time while the estate is being settled may need reconditioning before it can fly again.
