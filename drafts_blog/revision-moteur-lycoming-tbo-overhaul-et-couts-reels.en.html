Lycoming Engine Overhaul: TBO, Overhaul Options and Real Costs

Understanding TBO, the different overhaul options and the real budget to plan for a Lycoming engine, whether buying or during ownership.

Lycoming TBO: a recommendation, not a legal limit

The TBO (Time Between Overhauls) published by Lycoming for each engine series is a manufacturer recommendation, expressed in flight hours and usually combined with a calendar limit. For private use under EASA regulation, exceeding the hourly TBO does not generally require an immediate overhaul if the aircraft is operated under Part-NCO: the operator can keep flying beyond it, subject to a suitable maintenance programme and closer monitoring of engine condition. Commercial use, or operation under a structured maintenance organisation, on the other hand, usually requires strict compliance with the manufacturer's TBO.

In every case, TBO remains an essential reference point: beyond it, the statistical risk of a failure linked to wear of internal components (rings, bearings, valves) increases, even though a well-maintained engine, flown regularly and properly broken in, can sometimes exceed this limit noticeably without incident. The decision to fly beyond TBO should always rest on independent technical advice, not simply on a wish to defer a large expense.

The main Lycoming engine series

Lycoming powers the large majority of Europe's light aircraft fleet, from the Cessna 172 and Piper PA-28 to the Diamond DA40 and Cirrus SR20/SR22 depending on the engine fitted. The most common series on the used market are the four-cylinder O-320 and O-360 families (with their fuel-injected IO-320 and IO-360 variants), widely fitted to training and touring single-engines, as well as the more powerful six-cylinder O-540 and IO-540 families found on heavier or turbocharged aircraft.

The recommended TBO varies by series and exact engine version: it is generally higher on low-compression normally aspirated engines than on turbocharged or high specific-power versions, which experience greater thermal and mechanical stress. Always check the precise TBO applicable to your exact serial number against the manufacturer's documentation or your workshop, rather than relying on a generic figure found online.

Overhaul options: overhaul, remanufactured, new engine

When a Lycoming engine approaches the end of its useful life, several options are available to the owner, with different cost and warranty levels.

The standard overhaul

The engine is fully disassembled, every part inspected against manufacturer criteria, worn or out-of-tolerance parts are replaced, and the engine is rebuilt and bench-tested. This is the most common option for a private owner, with a good cost-to-reliability ratio when carried out by a competent, approved workshop experienced on the series concerned.

The exchange or remanufactured engine

Lycoming offers, directly or through its network, factory-remanufactured engines built to near-new tolerances, supplied in exchange for the old core (exchange programme). The logbook generally restarts at zero hours, with a manufacturer warranty, at a higher cost than a standard overhaul but with peace of mind and traceability that is often valued at resale.

The new engine

The most expensive option, usually reserved for cases where the engine has suffered major damage, or for an owner wanting maximum reliability and a full warranty from the first flight.

The real budget for a Lycoming engine overhaul

The cost of an overhaul varies significantly with engine series, cylinder count, the presence of a turbocharger or fuel injection, and the extent of parts needing replacement. A so-called "beyond service limits" overhaul, involving crankshaft or case replacement, costs noticeably more than a standard overhaul within normal wear tolerances. In general, a normally aspirated four-cylinder from the O-320/O-360 family represents the most accessible budget, while a turbocharged six-cylinder from the IO-540 family sits well above.

Beyond the engine itself, budget for accessories often overhauled or replaced at the same time (magnetos, fuel pumps, carburettor or injection system, flexible hoses), as well as the aircraft's downtime during the work, which can range from several weeks to several months depending on the workshop chosen and parts availability.

Choosing the overhaul workshop

The quality of the overhaul workshop is the single most decisive factor for engine reliability after overhaul. Favour an approved workshop (Part-145 or equivalent Part-CAO), specialised in Lycoming engines, able to provide a complete overhaul file with the list of replaced parts, inspection records and bench-test results.

Point of caution — Be wary of unusually low quotes for a full overhaul: they often hide a partial overhaul or the omission of parts that should be systematically replaced per manufacturer specifications, which can compromise engine reliability and resale value.

Extending engine life to TBO

A well-maintained Lycoming engine reaches its full TBO more easily, and sometimes exceeds it without incident under permissive regulation. The best practices most consistently recommended by workshops include flying often enough (an infrequently flown engine often ages worse than one used regularly, due to internal corrosion and drying seals), periodic oil analysis to catch abnormal wear before it worsens, and strictly following manufacturer start-up and cooling procedures, in particular avoiding thermal shock during descent.

  • Fly regularly, ideally at least once a month, to limit internal corrosion.
  • Track oil analysis and cylinder-by-cylinder compressions at every maintenance check.
  • Follow manufacturer warm-up and cooling procedures.

Reading SMOH when buying a used aircraft

SMOH (hours since the last major overhaul) is one of the most closely watched figures when buying a used Lycoming-powered aircraft, but it should never be read in isolation. A low SMOH on an engine that has flown little since its overhaul, with long periods of inactivity, can present more risk (internal corrosion, dried-out seals) than a higher SMOH on an engine flown regularly and well maintained.

  • Check the full file from the last overhaul: workshop, parts replaced, bench-test result.
  • Cross-check SMOH with annual flight hours since the overhaul: an infrequently flown engine ages differently from a regularly used one.
  • Ask for the latest oil analyses and the cylinder-by-cylinder compression history recorded in the logbook.

Impact on resale value

An engine recently overhauled by a recognised workshop, with a complete file, is a strong selling point and generally justifies a higher valuation than an engine approaching TBO with no overhaul planned. Conversely, a buyer who anticipates a near-term overhaul logically factors that future cost into negotiation, which can translate into a substantial discount on the asking price.

In every case, transparency about the engine's real condition, documented through a complete maintenance logbook and regular analyses, remains the best lever for defending an aircraft's price at resale, whether the engine is young or nearing the end of its potential.
