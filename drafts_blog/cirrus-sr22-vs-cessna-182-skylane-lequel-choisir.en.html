Cirrus SR22 vs Cessna 182 Skylane: which one to choose

Modern composite with parachute, or rugged high-wing metal all-rounder: a head-to-head comparison between the Cirrus SR22 and the Cessna 182 Skylane to help you pick the high-performance single-engine that fits your use and your budget.

Two philosophies for the same mission: travel and performance

The Cirrus SR22 and the Cessna 182 Skylane both target pilots who want to move up from a basic trainer to a single-engine capable of real family or business travel, with more power, comfort and payload. Yet the two aircraft embody radically different design philosophies: the SR22 is a modern composite, born in the 1990s, built around integrated avionics and a backup parachute system. The 182 Skylane descends directly from the historic Cessna line, with a classic metal structure, high wing and reinforced gear, produced without interruption since the 1950s.

Choosing between the two comes down to deciding between technological modernity and proven mechanical simplicity, between rough-field versatility and pure cruise performance.

The Cirrus SR22: composite, parachute and integrated avionics

The Cirrus SR22 has established itself as the benchmark modern high-performance single-engine thanks to its aerodynamic composite airframe, its factory-standard integrated avionics (Garmin Perspective displays on recent versions) and above all its CAPS parachute system, which in theory can bring the whole aircraft down safely in a serious emergency. This approach contributed heavily to its commercial success and, for several years, made it the best-selling single-engine aircraft in the world by value.

On the used market, SR22 listings vary widely by year, engine type (naturally aspirated or turbocharged depending on the version) and avionics level, which makes it possible to find an aircraft for fairly different budgets, while still generally sitting in a premium segment compared to the 182.

The Cessna 182 Skylane: the rugged high-wing metal all-rounder

The Cessna 182 Skylane is the more powerful version of the 172, with a classic metal airframe, a high wing offering excellent visibility, and a particularly rugged landing gear able to cope with rough or unpaved strips. Produced continuously for decades with many engine and avionics updates along the way, the 182 enjoys a reputation for reliability and mechanical simplicity that makes it a reassuring choice for many owners.

The used 182 market offers a very wide choice, from older examples with analogue avionics to newer versions fitted with glass cockpits. Its maintenance, based on a metal structure well understood by mechanics everywhere, is generally seen as simpler and cheaper than that of a composite airframe.

Performance and payload capacity

The Cirrus SR22 generally posts higher cruise speeds than the Cessna 182 Skylane, thanks to its aerodynamically optimised airframe and more powerful engine, particularly on turbocharged versions capable of flying at high altitude. The 182, for its part, offsets a more modest cruise speed with payload capacity that's often appreciated and a versatility for landing on varied terrain that the SR22 doesn't offer under the same conditions.

  • Cirrus SR22: higher cruise speed, modern integrated avionics, CAPS backup parachute.
  • Cessna 182 Skylane: excellent tolerance for rough strips, metal structure that's simple to inspect and repair.
  • Both aircraft suit family travel with good payload capacity for their category.
  • The choice of turbocharged engine (on either model) strongly influences the maintenance budget.

Purchase budget and operating costs

The Cirrus SR22 generally sits at a higher purchase budget than the Cessna 182 Skylane for a comparable model year, due to its premium positioning and strong demand on the global market. Its maintenance costs also include the periodic repack of the CAPS system and a more expensive composite repair in the event of damage, compared to the easily repairable aluminium skin of the 182.

Beyond the sticker price, you should always factor in the real cost per flight hour, which depends on the aircraft's age, remaining engine potential before the major overhaul, and intended frequency of use, for both models.

Buying used: points to watch

For a Cirrus SR22, the pre-purchase inspection should pay particular attention to the condition of the composite airframe (looking for impacts, delamination or undocumented previous repairs), the CAPS parachute's repack date and the short-term cost this implies, as well as the complete history of the integrated avionics. For a Cessna 182, attention should focus more on aluminium structure corrosion, the condition of the reinforced landing gear and the engine history, especially if the aircraft has regularly flown from unpaved strips.

Good to know — Always request the complete maintenance logbook and have the pre-purchase inspection carried out by an independent mechanic familiar with the relevant type, Cirrus or Cessna, before committing.

In both cases, consistency between the displayed hours, the logbook and the maintenance invoices remains the best indicator of an aircraft's real reliability.

Resale and the used market

The Cirrus SR22 benefits from a historically solid resale value, driven by continuous production and strong international demand for this modern, well-equipped type. The Cessna 182 Skylane, meanwhile, benefits from a larger market in terms of the volume of examples available and a reputation for ruggedness that appeals to a different buyer, sometimes more oriented toward rough-field or mountain use.

In both cases, a well-maintained aircraft with a complete maintenance logbook and up-to-date avionics generally sells faster and at a better price than a comparable but poorly documented example.

Which model to choose for your profile

The Cirrus SR22 will particularly suit a pilot who favours cruise speed, modern integrated avionics and the peace of mind brought by the backup parachute, for flights mainly carried out from airfields with paved runways. The Cessna 182 Skylane will appeal more to an owner looking for mechanical simplicity, a more predictable maintenance cost, and the ability to access rough strips, in exchange for a more modest cruise performance.

In either case, the total budget to plan for (purchase, maintenance, insurance and, for the SR22, the parachute repack) should be assessed precisely before committing, rather than relying solely on the advertised list price.
