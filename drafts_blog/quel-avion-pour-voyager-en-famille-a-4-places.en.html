Why the right model matters for a family of four

Flying as a family of four puts real constraints on a light aircraft that a brochure rarely shows. A "four-seat" certification doesn't automatically mean four adults plus luggage over a meaningful distance: the baseline performance figures on the spec sheet often hide a tighter reality once the tanks are full and the baggage compartment is loaded. Before settling on a model for this kind of mission, look closely at usable payload, the range you actually get with four people on board, cabin comfort over a multi-hour leg, and the ownership budget over time — not just the horsepower number on the listing.

Three aircraft come up again and again when families search the used market for a four-seat travel plane in Europe: the Cessna 172, the Cirrus SR20 and the Piper Archer (PA-28-181). Each follows a different design philosophy, with its own trade-offs on purchase budget, cruise performance and onboard safety. This comparison walks through the strengths and limits of each, to help you zero in on the model that actually fits your family's flying.

Cessna 172: the unkillable all-rounder

The Cessna 172 remains the most-produced light aircraft in the world, and that massive production run translates directly into a deep used market across Europe, easy parts availability, and mechanics familiar with the type in almost every country. Its tough metal airframe, stable handling and straightforward flying characteristics make it a reassuring first purchase for a family stepping into aircraft ownership, without the surprises that come with rarer types.

On the flip side, the 172 isn't the fastest aircraft in its class, and real-world payload with four occupants can be tight depending on the model year and engine fitted. On many examples, loading four adults plus bags forces a meaningful fuel reduction, which eats directly into usable range. It's a trade-off to plan for based on the trips you have in mind: excellent for shorter family hops, more constraining on long legs at full weight.

Cirrus SR20: modern comfort and CAPS safety

The Cirrus SR20, the smaller sibling of the SR22, brings a composite airframe, integrated modern avionics depending on the model year, and above all the CAPS emergency parachute system, which many families find particularly reassuring on legs over mountains, water or terrain that would be hard to reach in the event of an engine failure. Cabin comfort is also a strong selling point for family flying: ergonomic seats, careful sound insulation and a cockpit designed with long trips in mind, which cuts down on fatigue on a multi-hour flight with kids on board.

The SR20 does cost more to buy and to maintain than a comparable Cessna 172 or Piper Archer. Maintaining the parachute system is a specific line item to budget for over time, just as with its bigger sibling the SR22, and it needs to be factored in early when evaluating the purchase price of a used example.

Piper Archer (PA-28-181): simple and roomy

The Piper Archer, the 180-horsepower member of the PA-28 Cherokee family, often lands as a useful middle ground between the Cessna 172 and the Cirrus SR20: payload is generally a touch higher than a comparably-powered 172, the metal airframe is simple to maintain, and overall running costs stay well below those of a composite aircraft carrying a parachute system. Its low wing makes boarding easier for kids and adults alike, and gives better upward visibility in cruise — a detail families notice on sightseeing legs.

As with the 172, the used market is broad, with plenty of model years and avionics fits available, which makes it fairly easy to match your purchase budget to the equipment level you actually want, rather than settling for the narrower pool you sometimes find with newer or rarer types.

Budget comparison: purchase and ownership

For most families, budget is the first real filter. The Cessna 172 and the Piper Archer, both metal-airframe aircraft with conventional piston engines, show broadly comparable entry and running costs, and both sit below a Cirrus SR20, whose composite airframe, integrated avionics and parachute system add to both the purchase price and the day-to-day maintenance bill.

Which aircraft fits which family profile

Choosing between these three should start with how the family actually intends to use the aircraft: how often you'll fly, typical trip length, number of kids and bags, and how sensitive you are to the annual maintenance bill.

  • Cessna 172: a solid first family purchase, well suited to regular shorter trips with a controlled maintenance budget.
  • Cirrus SR20: a good fit for families who prioritise cabin comfort and an extra safety margin on demanding routes.
  • Piper Archer: a solid compromise for a family that wants more usable payload than a 172 without stepping up to composite-level costs.
  • In every case, check the real payload with full fuel and your actual four occupants, not just the minimum-configuration figure in the listing.

A family that flies occasionally over short distances will get an excellent cost-to-use ratio from the 172 or the Archer. A family that regularly covers long legs, especially over mountains or water, will find in the SR20 a comfort and safety argument that justifies the higher purchase and running cost.

Buying used: what to check before flying with the kids

Whichever model you settle on, buying a used aircraft meant for regular family use calls for extra diligence — several people's safety depends directly on the aircraft's actual condition, not on how good the listing photos look.

Good to know — Always have the pre-purchase inspection carried out by an independent mechanic familiar with the type, and ask for the complete logbook plus maintenance invoices before you commit.

For the Cessna 172 and the Piper Archer, pay particular attention to airframe corrosion, engine history and the engine hours (SMOH) logged since the last major overhaul. For the Cirrus SR20, also check the condition of the composite structure and the expiry date of the parachute system's pyrotechnic components. In every case, ask for a copy of the certificate of airworthiness (CofA) and compare several listings of similar models and model years before you decide on a fair price.
