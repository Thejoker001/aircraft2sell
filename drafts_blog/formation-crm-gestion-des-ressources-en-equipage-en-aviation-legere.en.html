CRM training: Crew Resource Management in light aviation

What CRM is, and why it also matters in light aviation

Crew Resource Management, or CRM, refers to the set of non-technical skills that allow a crew, or a single pilot, to make the best possible use of every resource available — human, material and informational — to carry out a flight safely. Communication, situational awareness, decision-making, workload management and error management are its core components.

Long seen as a discipline reserved for commercial air transport, CRM in fact applies just as much to general aviation. A large share of accidents in general aviation stem not from a technical failure of the aircraft, but from a chain of imperfect decisions or communications: a poor assessment of weather conditions, hesitation to cancel a flight, or a poor division of tasks between pilot and passenger during an instructional flight.

A little history: where CRM comes from

The concept of CRM emerged in the 1970s following several major airline accidents whose investigations revealed that the crew had all the information needed to avoid disaster, but that failures in communication, hierarchy or perception prevented timely action. Airlines then developed dedicated training focused on human factors, which gradually became mandatory across most professional training programmes.

General aviation adopted these principles with some delay, but the underlying logic remains the same: a private pilot, whether alone on board or accompanied by a non-pilot passenger, remains subject to the same cognitive biases and workload overload mechanisms as a professional crew, often with fewer institutional safeguards to offset them.

The pillars of CRM

CRM rests on several complementary skills, which are developed and refined through experience and ongoing training.

  • Communication — conveying information clearly and completely, and making sure it has been properly understood, whether with a co-pilot, a passenger or air traffic control.
  • Situational awareness — maintaining an accurate picture of the flight environment at all times: position, weather, aircraft status, remaining fuel, surrounding traffic.
  • Decision-making — assessing the available options and choosing the safest course of action, including when it means turning back or diverting.
  • Workload management — prioritising tasks by urgency and importance, particularly during critical phases of flight.
  • Leadership and assertiveness — being able to make a clear decision, but also to voice disagreement or concern without hesitation whenever flight safety is at stake.

CRM for single-pilot operations: the factor often forgotten

Flying solo does not exempt a pilot from CRM principles — quite the opposite. With no second pilot to catch an error or share the workload, the single pilot on board must develop rigorous personal discipline: systematic checks, anticipating degraded situations, and above all, the ability to recognise their own limits before they become a problem in flight.

Some instructors use the term "single-pilot resource management" to describe this adapted version for the solo pilot: making full use of the resources available (a flight plan prepared in advance, checklists, up-to-date weather information, radio assistance) rather than managing everything from memory and in the moment.

Good to know — A non-pilot passenger can also become a useful resource if briefed beforehand: watching for surrounding traffic, reading out a checklist, or simply reporting any discomfort or unusual observation.

Error management and decision biases

A major part of CRM is accepting that human error is inevitable, and building practices that catch and correct it before it has consequences. Several decision biases recur frequently in the analysis of general aviation incidents.

Common biases worth knowing

  • "Get-there-itis" — the drive to reach the destination at all costs, which pushes a pilot to downplay warning signs (weather, fuel, fatigue).
  • Overconfidence from experience — an experienced pilot may underestimate a risk they have handled without difficulty in the past, even though conditions were actually different.
  • Normalisation of deviance — gradually accepting small departures from procedures because they have never caused a serious consequence so far.
  • Social or image pressure — hesitating to cancel or interrupt a flight for fear of a passenger's or peer's judgement.

Recognising these biases does not always neutralise them in the moment, but being aware of them before the flight, during preparation, helps set clear personal limits and stick to them.

Putting CRM into practice at a flying club

CRM can be practised both individually and collectively within a flying club. A structured pre-flight briefing, including an open discussion of the weather, how the pilot is feeling that day, and any time pressures, is already a simple and effective form of CRM.

Reports of incidents or unusual situations, often shared informally among club members, also play an important role: they allow the group to learn collectively from mistakes without waiting for an accident to happen. Encouraging a culture where reporting a mistake or a moment of hesitation is viewed positively, rather than punished, supports continuous safety improvement within the club.

Word of caution — A pre-flight briefing rushed through or treated as a mere formality loses most of its value. Taking a few extra minutes to genuinely discuss the day's conditions remains one of the simplest and most worthwhile CRM habits.

Getting trained in CRM: where to turn

Several routes exist for building CRM skills in light aviation. Some instructors and flying schools offer dedicated modules on human factors, sometimes built directly into PPL training or post-licence refresher courses.

Specialised organisations and some national aviation federations also run seminars or workshops dedicated to human factors, often built around real case studies and scenario-based exercises. These group formats have the advantage of exposing participants to different perspectives on the same situation, which enriches individual reflection.

Finally, self-directed learning also has its place: reading accident investigation reports (often published by aviation safety authorities), personally reviewing one's own decisions after each flight, and regularly discussing with more experienced pilots are all practices accessible at any time, without requiring formal training.

CRM: a habit to build flight after flight

CRM is not a skill acquired once and for all through a one-off course, but a discipline built and maintained over time, flight after flight. Consistently taking a moment for mental preparation before every flight, staying alert to one's own limits, and fostering open communication with everyone involved in the flight — whether a co-pilot, an instructor or simply a passenger — remains the best way to reduce the risk of human error.

Tip — Before every flight, make a habit of asking yourself three simple questions: am I genuinely ready today, do I have all the information I need, and what margin will I have if things go wrong. This short mental exercise remains one of the most concrete applications of CRM for a general aviation pilot.
