Cleaning and detailing an aircraft before a sale

Exterior and interior cleaning, photos, up-to-date paperwork: how to prepare a used aircraft before a sale to maximise perceived value and sell faster.

Why detailing affects the sale price

A buyer forms a first impression before even opening the technical file: a clean airframe, a clear canopy and a tidy cabin suggest a well-maintained aircraft, while a neglected interior immediately raises doubts about the owner's rigor on the rest of the maintenance — even when nothing mechanical is actually wrong. This perception bias directly affects how quickly the aircraft sells and, to some extent, the final negotiated price.

Detailing is never a substitute for a solid technical file, but it determines how many visits and calls your listing will generate. A clean, well-presented used aircraft stands out in a search where photos are the buyer's first filter.

Exterior cleaning: airframe, canopy, gear

Exterior cleaning should be done a few days before the first visits, not the night before a sale that is already under way — it also reveals defects (a leak, early corrosion) that are better found and addressed before a buyer or their mechanic discovers them during inspection.

  • Full airframe wash with a product suited to the paint/aluminium, no abrasive that could dull the finish.
  • Polishing the canopy and windows with a plexiglass-specific product, never an ordinary glass cleaner that can haze or micro-scratch it.
  • Careful degreasing of the landing gear and wheels, the most visible areas during a ground visit.
  • Treating exhaust staining under the fuselage, often the first detail a trained eye notices.
  • Minor paint touch-ups (chips, micro-scratches) if the cost stays reasonable against the presentation gain.

Interior cleaning: cabin, seats, instruments

The interior is often where a buyer spends the most time studying details during a visit — it is also the area most revealing of how the aircraft was actually used.

  • Shampooing or cleaning the seats according to the material (leather, fabric), with a product suited to it that won't degrade it.
  • Careful dusting of the instrument panel and instruments, with no liquid product sprayed directly onto the avionics.
  • Removing persistent odours (smoke, mould) — a flaw that can make a buyer walk away on the spot, before any technical review.
  • Checking and tidying the cabin's contents: nothing should suggest neglected upkeep or habitual clutter.
  • Cleaning carpets and mats, especially in the high-traffic areas near the doors.

Engine and engine bay: proceed with caution

A clean engine bay inspires confidence, but cleaning this area requires more caution than elsewhere: the wrong degreaser can mask a recent oil leak that the buyer, or their mechanic during the pre-purchase inspection, will specifically be looking for.

Word of caution — Never clean the engine bay right before an announced pre-purchase inspection: an engine that looks "too clean" raises suspicion with an experienced mechanic, who expects to see light traces of normal use (minor oil residue, break-in marks).
  • Light dusting of the cowling and visible external areas, no aggressive degreaser on internal components.
  • Note any existing oil traces in the logbook rather than hiding them — transparency reassures far more than suspicious cleanliness.
  • Check that the displayed engine hours and time since overhaul (SMOH) match the logbook before any visit.

Photos and listing presentation

Photos are the buyer's first contact with your aircraft, often before they even read the description. Good detailing only pays off if it is captured properly.

  • Outdoor photos in diffuse natural light (avoid bright sunlight, which creates harsh reflections on the airframe and canopy).
  • Angles covering the whole aircraft: front three-quarter, side profile, rear, plus close-ups of the gear, the closed cowling and the instrument panel.
  • A clear background, with no hangar clutter or tools visible that would distract from the main subject.
  • No editing that hides a real defect — a misleading photo costs credibility from the very first visit.

Up-to-date documents and logbook

An immaculate aircraft with an incomplete file remains hard to sell. Prepare the paperwork alongside the physical cleaning, not after a successful first visit.

  • An up-to-date logbook, filed chronologically, with no missing page or unexplained erasure.
  • A valid certificate of airworthiness (CofA) and current airworthiness review, ready to show from the first visit.
  • Invoices for the latest work gathered in a single file, physical or digital, easy to hand over.
  • An up-to-date list of applicable airworthiness directives and their compliance status.

What you should never do

Some well-intentioned practices actually hurt the sale or the buyer's trust.

  • Never use abrasive products or high pressure directly on avionics or seals.
  • Never hide a visible leak or wear mark instead of documenting it — it will be found at inspection, and concealment costs far more in trust than the defect itself.
  • Never delay cleaning until the night before the first visit: rushed detailing leaves streaks and misses defects that should have been fixed beforehand.
  • Never present the aircraft without checking that its apparent condition matches the data stated in the listing (engine hours, year, equipment).

Final checklist before publishing the listing

Before publishing or updating your listing, go through this list to make sure the aircraft is ready to be shown at its best.

  • Airframe, canopy and gear cleaned and dried the same day or the day before the photos.
  • Clean, odour-free interior, dust-free instrument panel.
  • Engine bay lightly dusted, with no aggressive degreasing hiding a real trace.
  • A complete set of photos, natural light, varied angles, clear background.
  • Logbook, CofA and recent invoices filed and ready to show.
  • Listing description consistent with the actual condition observed after detailing.
Tip — Good detailing combined with a transparent technical file generally shortens the time to sale: serious buyers move faster when they have no doubts to clear before the pre-purchase inspection.
