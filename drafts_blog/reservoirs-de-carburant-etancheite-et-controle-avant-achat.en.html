Fuel tanks: sealant condition and pre-purchase checks

Leaks, corrosion, sender units and aging sealant: what to check on a used aircraft's fuel tanks before committing to a purchase.

Why fuel tanks deserve special attention

Fuel tanks are one of the most commonly underestimated items when buying a used aircraft. Unlike a paint defect or outdated avionics, a sealing problem is hidden inside the wing or fuselage structure and is not always visible during a simple static walk-around. Yet an untreated fuel leak can lead to a prolonged grounding of the aircraft and a significant repair bill.

On many light aircraft, the tank is an integral part of the wing structure, commonly called a "wet wing". In this case, the seal relies on a sealant compound applied during manufacturing, which ages over time due to thermal cycles and vibration. On other aircraft, flexible bladder tanks or removable rigid tanks are installed within the structure, each with their own aging characteristics.

Integral, bladder or rigid tanks: what it changes

The type of tank fitted on an aircraft directly determines the kind of risks to monitor and the cost of any potential repair. Before any inspection, it is useful to know which type of tank you will be dealing with.

  • Integral tank (wet wing) — the wing structure itself serves as the tank; sealing depends on the compound applied between panels and around rivets. A full re-sealing is a long and costly operation.
  • Bladder tank — a sealed pouch housed inside a structural bay; replacing it is generally simpler than a full wet-wing re-seal, but the bladder itself has a limited service life.
  • Removable rigid tank — often found on older aircraft or certain light types; easy to remove for inspection, but prone to internal corrosion if the aluminium is not properly treated.

The maintenance logbook should specify the type of tank fitted, as well as any prior work (partial or full re-seal, bladder replacement, corrosion treatment). The absence of this information in the file is itself a signal worth pursuing with the seller before going any further.

Seepage, leak and corrosion: knowing the difference

Not every trace of fuel found on a tank represents the same level of severity. Certification bodies and maintenance shops generally distinguish several categories, ranging from minor seepage with no operational consequence up to an active leak requiring immediate grounding of the aircraft.

What a mechanic looks for during the inspection

  • Dried fuel stains or bluish/greenish deposits under the wing, around rivets or inspection panels — a sign of past seepage, even if the tank appears dry on the day of the viewing.
  • A persistent fuel smell in the hangar or near the spars, revealing slow migration through the sealant.
  • Visible corrosion around drain plugs, fuel senders or fittings — often a consequence rather than the cause of a failing seal.
  • Condition of the sealant at access points: cracking, lifting or excessive hardening point to a re-seal that will be needed in the medium term.
Word of caution — A seller may have cleaned off visible traces before a viewing. Always ask for the tank refuelling and draining history recorded in the logbook, and compare the dates of the last interventions related to sealing.

How a leak check is carried out

The leak check is an integral part of any serious pre-purchase inspection. It generally combines a thorough visual examination, a check after a full fuel top-off and an extended parking period, as well as a verification that the tank vents are working correctly.

  • Filling the tanks completely, then observing over several hours or even overnight, looking for any sign of seepage.
  • Checking the tank vents: a blocked vent can create a pressure differential that artificially worsens apparent seepage or disrupts engine fuel supply.
  • Inspecting from the inside, where possible, via inspection panels, to examine the condition of the sealant, internal baffles and sender mounts.
  • Cross-checking against the flight logbook: any fuel consumption abnormally higher than the aircraft's usual figures should be questioned.

This step should be entrusted to an approved mechanic independent of the seller, as part of the broader pre-purchase inspection of the aircraft, not handled in isolation.

Senders, gauges and the fuel feed system

Beyond sealing itself, fuel gauge sender units, selector valves and the associated filters deserve a specific check. A faulty sender can mask an actual fuel level that differs from what is shown on the panel, which is a direct operational risk and not merely a resale-value concern.

Check the consistency between the gauge reading and a visual check or manual measurement of the actual level, as well as the correct operation of the tank selector valve on aircraft fitted with one. A history of fuel filter and fuel line replacement, recorded in the logbook, is also a good indicator of the overall seriousness of the aircraft's maintenance.

Repair cost and negotiation

The cost of a tank repair varies widely depending on the type of aircraft, the type of tank involved and the extent of the work required: a localized repair of isolated seepage is nothing like a full re-seal of a wet wing across the entire wingspan, which involves complete removal of equipment, stripping of the existing sealant and a new certified application. This type of work means a prolonged grounding of the aircraft, often for several weeks.

Tip — If a sealing defect is identified during the pre-purchase inspection, obtain a written quote from an approved shop before negotiating the sale price. A documented cost estimate is a far stronger negotiating argument than a verbal estimate.

In all cases, a confirmed sealing defect should never be dismissed on the grounds that "it doesn't affect the flight": it reflects aging of the structure or sealing materials that will worsen over time and usage cycles.

Frequently asked questions

Does minor seepage prevent an aircraft from flying?

It depends on the classification given by the mechanic and the limits defined by the manufacturer. Minor seepage may sometimes be tolerated temporarily with increased monitoring, while an active leak requires immediate grounding until repaired.

Is re-sealing an integral tank a permanent fix?

A re-seal carried out properly by an approved shop has a service life of several years, but it depends on storage conditions, humidity and how the aircraft is used. It does not remove the need for regular checks at periodic inspections.

Should the tanks always be drained and inspected before purchase?

It isn't systematic, but it is strongly recommended whenever there is doubt about the maintenance history, the age of the sealant, or if suspicious traces are found during the initial viewing.
