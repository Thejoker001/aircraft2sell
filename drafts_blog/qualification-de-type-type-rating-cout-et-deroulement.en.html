Type Rating: Cost and Training Process

Prerequisites, training programme, costs to budget for and validity: everything you need to know before signing up for a type rating to fly a multi-engine aircraft, turboprop or business jet.

What is a type rating

A type rating is a training programme and certification specific to a particular aircraft model, which is added on top of an existing pilot licence. Unlike a licence, which certifies general skills (private pilot, commercial pilot), a type rating covers the systems, procedures and handling characteristics of one specific aircraft: the same pilot will need a different type rating for a Cessna Citation, a King Air or a Pilatus PC-12, even if they already hold the licences needed to fly multi-engine or turboprop aircraft.

The type rating sits within the EASA regulatory framework in Europe (or the equivalent FAA framework in the United States) and governs the legal right to fly or act as commander on a given aircraft, in addition to often being a requirement set by the operator's or owner's insurer.

When is a type rating mandatory

A type rating is required to fly most complex multi-engine aircraft, all turboprops above a certain mass or complexity threshold, and the whole range of business jets and airliners. Light touring single-engines (Cessna 172, Piper PA-28, Cirrus SR22) generally do not require one: moving between two models in that category is handled through a simple differences briefing or familiarisation, not a full type rating.

  • Business jets and airliners — a type rating is always mandatory, regardless of the model.
  • Turboprops — a type rating is required for most models above a certain maximum mass, depending on the intended type of operation (private or commercial).
  • Complex multi-engine aircraft — some high-powered or turbine-powered twins require a specific type rating in addition to the standard multi-engine rating.
Good to know — The exact list of aircraft requiring a type rating depends on current regulations and the certified maximum mass. Always check the precise status of the model you are targeting with an approved training organisation or the relevant civil aviation authority before committing.

Prerequisites before enrolling

Before enrolling in a type rating, a pilot must already hold a licence suited to the category of the target aircraft (commercial or airline transport licence, depending on the case), as well as the associated base ratings: an instrument rating (IR) and a multi-engine rating (ME) for almost all relevant types. A minimum number of total flying hours, or hours on a given aircraft category, may also be required by the training organisation or by regulation, and this varies by target model.

  • A licence suited to the category (CPL or ATPL depending on the intended type of operation).
  • A valid instrument rating (IR).
  • A valid multi-engine rating (ME) for most relevant types.
  • A medical certificate of the class required for the intended type of operation.

How the training unfolds

A type rating generally runs in three main phases: a theoretical part covering the aircraft's systems (engines, avionics, hydraulic and electrical systems, normal and emergency procedures), a practical part on a representative flight simulator for the model (a full flight simulator or a certified training device), and then a line check or check flight phase on the real aircraft, depending on the organisation's programme.

Total duration varies widely depending on the aircraft's complexity and the pilot's prior experience, typically ranging from two to several weeks for the theory and simulator portion, followed by a variable number of check flight hours depending on the type of operation (private or commercial) and the specific requirements of the manufacturer or training organisation.

Tip — Always ask the training organisation for a precise breakdown of the programme (hours of theory, simulator and check flight) before enrolling: this schedule varies considerably between centres for the same aircraft model.

The cost of a type rating

The cost of a type rating varies widely depending on the aircraft model, the training organisation chosen and the country where the training takes place. It mainly depends on the cost of access to the dedicated flight simulator for the model (often the single largest expense), the length of the theoretical and practical programme, and ancillary costs (accommodation, travel to a training centre that is often abroad).

Light to mid-size turboprop (e.g. King Air, Pilatus PC-12)€15,000 to €35,000

Point of caution — These figures are general approximations and vary considerably by organisation, country and current commercial conditions. Always request a detailed, up-to-date quote from the target training organisation rather than relying on a generic estimate.

On top of this initial training cost come the recurring costs of maintaining currency (periodic simulator checks) and, for a private owner, the often significant cost of insuring an aircraft that requires a type rating, which depends directly on the pilot's experience on that specific type.

Validity and keeping the rating current

A type rating is not held for life: it must be kept current through periodic simulator or flight checks, at a frequency set by the applicable regulations (generally annual for type proficiency, with additional checks depending on the type of operation). A pilot who lets their rating lapse generally has to repeat part of the initial training programme to regain it, which can cost close to the initial type rating depending on how long the lapse has been.

Good to know — If you are considering buying an aircraft that requires a type rating, build the cost and schedule of the initial training, as well as upcoming periodic checks, into your overall operating budget from the very start of the project.

Choosing a training organisation

The choice of training organisation (often a specialised centre approved by the aircraft manufacturer, or an independent approved training organisation) affects the cost, the availability of simulator slots and the quality of instruction. Manufacturers frequently have their own centres or partnerships with dedicated training centres for their models, which generally guarantees a simulator kept up to date with the latest avionics versions.

  • Check the organisation's approval with the relevant civil aviation authority for the licence and rating you are pursuing.
  • Compare simulator slot availability, often the limiting factor in the training schedule.
  • Ask about the pass rate and feedback from former trainees on the instruction provided.

Link with buying a used aircraft

For a prospective owner considering buying a used turboprop or business jet, the cost and schedule of the type rating must be built into the overall project budget, alongside the purchase price, maintenance and insurance. Some sellers or brokers even offer to include the new buyer's training in the deal negotiation, which can represent a notable saving compared with enrolling separately with a training organisation.

It is also worth checking the flight history of the aircraft you are considering and the consistency of its logbook before committing to a long, costly type-specific training programme: a thorough pre-purchase inspection remains essential, type rating or not.

Tip — Plan the financing of the type rating as early as you put together your purchase file: some aircraft financing solutions allow this cost to be folded into the overall plan, which simplifies cash flow management during the first year of operation.
