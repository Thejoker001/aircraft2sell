Buying a used aircraft in Norway or Finland: which country to choose

Market, prices, registration, tax and climate: a comparison between Norway and Finland to help you decide where to look for your used aircraft.

Two Nordic markets, two very different operating realities

Norway and Finland share a harsh Nordic climate, a vast, sparsely populated territory and a light aviation culture shaped by long distances between airfields. Yet they remain two very different markets in administrative terms: Norway, a member of the European Economic Area (EEA) but not of the European Union, follows a tax and customs regime distinct from that of Finland, a full member of both the EU and the eurozone.

For a buyer planning to operate in the far north, whether for touring, inter-island transport or utility flying on skis or floats, comparing both markets helps clarify the practical constraints specific to each country before committing to a listing.

Size and profile of the used market

The Norwegian used-aircraft market is marked by a high proportion of aircraft adapted to short, unpaved strips, as well as a significant number of floatplanes and ski-equipped aircraft, consistent with a geography of fjords, mountains and long winter seasons. Classic touring single-engines remain present, but the fleet includes a notable share of STOL aircraft used to connect remote areas.

The Finnish market, meanwhile, rests on a particularly well-established flying-club tradition, with a large number of training and touring aircraft rigorously maintained within structured clubs. Winter-equipped aircraft (skis, engine preheating) are also common, as Finland has a large number of small frozen lakes used as makeshift winter airstrips.

Comparing prices

Price levels between the two countries remain broadly comparable for an equivalent model and maintenance condition, with no systematic premium or discount on either side. Variations seen mostly reflect the relative scarcity of certain specific configurations (floatplanes, skis, preheating engines) and the local supply available at the time of the search.

In both countries, aircraft specifically equipped for winter operations (skis, preheating engines, reinforced propellers) carry a value that depends heavily on the condition of this additional equipment, which should be appraised separately from the airframe itself.

A different status regarding the European Union

The most structural difference between the two countries concerns their status regarding the European Union. Finland is a full EU member state, which simplifies intra-community transactions between professionals and aligns applicable taxation with common European VAT rules.

Norway, on the other hand, belongs to the European Economic Area but remains outside the EU customs union and EU VAT regime. Importing an aircraft from another EEA or EU country into Norway may therefore involve customs formalities and a specific Norwegian VAT, distinct from those applicable between two EU member states. It is advisable to check this point carefully with a professional before any cross-border transaction involving Norway.

Registration: Luftfartstilsynet versus Traficom

In Norway, aircraft registration is handled by the Luftfartstilsynet (Civil Aviation Authority of Norway), under the LN- prefix. In Finland, it is Traficom (the Finnish Transport and Communications Agency) that manages the register, under the OH- prefix. Finland, as an EU member state, fully applies EASA rules for registration transfers between member states. Norway, while participating in the EASA system through its EEA membership, may apply additional administrative steps specific to its non-EU status, particularly regarding customs.

  • Valid certificate of airworthiness and ARC — a precondition for any flight under the new registration, in both Norway and Finland.
  • Deregistration from the register of origin — required before the new registration if the aircraft changes country.
  • Complete technical file — airframe, engine and propeller logbooks to be submitted to the new authority.
  • Checking customs status — a particular point of attention for any aircraft entering Norway from an EU country.
Good to know — Allow a variable timeline depending on how complete the file is and, for Norway, on any applicable customs formalities; checking early with the relevant authority avoids unpleasant scheduling surprises.

Climate, seasonality and maintenance

Both countries share long, harsh winters with a direct impact on how aircraft are kept and configured. An aircraft based in Norway or Finland often spends part of the year on snow or ice, which calls for particularly attentive maintenance of the engine (preheating before start-up), landing gear and components exposed to salt and humidity along the Norwegian coast.

Seasonality also shapes the used-aircraft market itself: transactions tend to concentrate in spring and summer, when prospective buyers can more easily travel for a pre-purchase inspection and test flight under good conditions.

Maintenance network and parts availability

Norway has a network of workshops spread along its coastline and main valleys, with recognised expertise in floatplanes and ski-equipped aircraft, a specialty less common elsewhere in Europe. Finland has a well-structured workshop network built around its flying clubs, with good parts availability for the most common touring and training models.

In both countries, terrain and long distances between airfields can extend turnaround times for an aircraft based far from an approved workshop. It is therefore worth checking the proximity of a competent workshop before committing to a listing, particularly for an aircraft fitted with specific modifications (skis, floats, preheating engine).

Which country to favour depending on your profile

For a buyer interested in floatplane or ski-equipped flying, or in operating short strips in mountain or fjord areas, the Norwegian market offers supply and local expertise that is hard to match elsewhere in Europe. For a buyer looking for a classic touring or training aircraft, rigorously maintained within a structured flying club, the Finnish market deserves particular attention.

Norway's customs status, outside the European Union, is a decisive factor for a professional buyer or for any transaction involving several EU countries: Finland, fully integrated into the European single market, considerably simplifies this kind of operation. Conversely, a buyer based in Norway or planning to operate the aircraft there long-term is not affected by this difference for a purely local purchase.

Overall ownership costs should also factor in ferrying: distances within both Norway and Finland can be considerable, and an aircraft spotted far from your home base will add extra cost and delay before it can first be used. This factor, often underestimated in Nordic regions where road or air journeys can be long, is worth quantifying before settling on one listing over another.

Good to know — In all cases, a pre-purchase inspection by a qualified independent mechanic remains essential, whether the aircraft is located in Norway, Finland or elsewhere in Europe, with particular attention paid to corrosion and the condition of winter-specific equipment.

Ultimately, widening your search to both Nordic markets often provides access to complementary supply, as each country has different specialties. Taking the time to carefully compare listings available in Norway and Finland, while factoring in each country's customs status, remains the best strategy for finding the aircraft best suited to your needs.
