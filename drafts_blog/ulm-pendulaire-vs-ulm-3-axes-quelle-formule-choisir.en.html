Weight-shift vs 3-axis microlight: which to choose

Two major microlight families, two very different flying philosophies. A complete comparison to help you choose the option best suited to your budget, your flying style and your purchase project.

Two microlight families, two flying logics

Microlight aircraft cover several categories of aircraft that are very different in design and flying technique. The two most widespread major families are the weight-shift microlight (also called a "trike") and the 3-axis microlight, which is closer to a classic light aircraft in its control logic. Choosing between the two is not just a matter of budget: it is above all a matter of flying philosophy, since the sensations and piloting techniques differ considerably.

Before buying a used aircraft in either category, it is essential to have genuinely experienced both during introductory flights, as the two formulas are not flown the same way and do not suit the same pilot profiles.

The weight-shift microlight: simplicity and sensation

A weight-shift microlight consists of a flexible delta wing suspended above a motorised trike carrying the pilot (and sometimes a passenger), the engine and the landing gear. Control is achieved by shifting the trike's weight relative to the wing via a control bar, giving flying sensations often described as very direct and close to free flight.

Its mechanics are deliberately simple: few controls, no complex control surfaces to coordinate, and a generally lighter structure that is less expensive to buy than a comparable 3-axis aircraft. In exchange, weight-shift microlights are more sensitive to turbulent weather conditions and offer less comfort (open or semi-enclosed cabin depending on the model) than a 3-axis aircraft fitted with a genuine enclosed cell.

The 3-axis microlight: versatility close to a light aircraft

The 3-axis microlight follows the classic architecture of a light aircraft: a rigid airframe, with elevator, rudder and ailerons controlled independently via a stick or yoke and rudder pedals. Flying it is therefore much closer to flying a certified light aircraft, which often eases a later transition toward a private pilot licence for pilots considering progression.

This formula generally offers greater comfort (enclosed, sometimes heated cabin), better habitability for two occupants, and cruise performance often superior to a comparable weight-shift aircraft. In exchange, it requires a generally higher purchase and maintenance budget, due to more complex mechanics (structure, flight controls, avionics).

Training and licence: separate paths

In France and several European countries, the microlight brevet/licence is split by class, and flying either weight-shift or 3-axis microlights each requires its own specific training with an instructor qualified in the relevant class — one does not automatically grant the right to fly the other. A pilot holding a weight-shift rating who wishes to move to 3-axis (or vice versa) must complete additional training dedicated to the new class, even though prior aeronautical experience generally eases the learning process.

  • Each class (weight-shift, 3-axis, but also paramotor and gyroplane) is distinct and requires its own training.
  • Training time varies with the pilot's prior experience, but generally remains more accessible and faster than a classic private pilot licence.
  • Check with a local microlight school for the precise requirements applicable in your country before committing to buying an aircraft.

Budget comparison: purchase and maintenance

The used acquisition budget generally remains more accessible on the weight-shift side, whose simpler mechanics also keep routine maintenance costs down. The 3-axis microlight, with its more elaborate airframe and often more onboard avionics, requires a higher initial investment and generally higher maintenance costs, particularly if the aircraft is fitted with electronic instruments or a more powerful engine.

Which formula for which use

The choice between weight-shift and 3-axis depends above all on what you are looking for in your microlight flying. For short local flights, strong sensations and a controlled budget, the weight-shift microlight remains a very popular formula, particularly for discovering powered free flight in its purest form.

  • Weight-shift: ideal for short leisure flights, direct flying sensations and a more accessible entry budget.
  • 3-axis: relevant for longer trips, more comfort, and for pilots considering a later progression toward certified light aircraft.
  • Personal tolerance for turbulent weather conditions, often more pronounced in weight-shift than in 3-axis.
  • Whether or not you need to carry a passenger in comfortable conditions over an extended period.

In all cases, one or more introductory flights in each formula before buying remain the best way to confirm your real preference, beyond the theoretical characteristics presented here.

Buying either one used: points to watch

Whether it's a weight-shift or a 3-axis microlight, the pre-purchase inspection must pay particular attention to the structure (the wing and its fabric for weight-shift, airframe and covering for 3-axis), the engine (operating hours, maintenance history, engine logbook) and the overall condition of the landing gear. For a weight-shift aircraft, pay particular attention to the condition of the wing fabric (wear, UV exposure, tears) and the date of its last replacement, a maintenance item specific to this formula.

Good to know — Always ask for the complete maintenance logbook and, if possible, have a pre-purchase inspection carried out by an independent mechanic familiar with the formula in question before committing.

Comparing several listings of similar models and model years, within the same family, remains the best way to assess a fair price and spot a well-maintained aircraft, whether weight-shift or 3-axis.
