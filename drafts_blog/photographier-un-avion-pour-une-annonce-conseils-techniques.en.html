Photographing an aircraft for a listing: technical tips

Sharp, well-lit, complete photos are the difference between a listing that generates calls and one that sits idle. Here is a practical method for photographing your aircraft like a professional, without expensive gear.

Why photos decide whether a listing works

A potential buyer spends only a few seconds looking at the cover photo before deciding whether to open the full listing or scroll to the next one. For a used aircraft, that decision usually comes down to a single visual cue: does the aircraft look well maintained, clean, and taken seriously by its owner? A set of blurry, dark or badly framed photos immediately suggests the opposite, even if the aircraft is mechanically excellent.

Conversely, a listing with fifteen to twenty sharp photos, shot from multiple angles, outside and inside, builds instant trust. It shows the seller has nothing to hide and treats the transaction seriously. This matters even more for a used aircraft, where the buyer has to commit to a purchase often worth tens or hundreds of thousands of euros before being able to travel and inspect a shortlist of candidates in person.

Photos never replace a physical pre-purchase inspection or a test flight, but they decide who makes it that far in the process. Spending an hour or two on a careful photo session is one of the highest-return moves you can make to sell faster and at the right price.

What gear you actually need

A professional camera is not required. A recent smartphone with a decent sensor produces results that are more than good enough for an online listing, as long as you follow a few simple principles. What really matters is light, stability and framing, not raw sensor resolution.

  • A microfibre cloth and glass cleaner — clean the windscreen, windows and fuselage right before the shoot; dust and smudges show up badly in photos.
  • A lightweight tripod or a stable surface to brace against — avoids motion blur, especially for interior shots where light is weaker.
  • A wide-angle lens (native or clip-on for a phone) — useful for the cabin and cockpit, but use it sparingly outside to avoid distorting the aircraft's proportions.
  • A torch or your phone's flashlight — to light the engine bay and shadowed areas under the cowling without a direct flash, which creates glare on metal.
  • A step stool or small ladder — to get slightly elevated angles, which read more flattering than photos shot at ground level.

If you have a DSLR or mirrorless camera, favour a lens around 24-35mm full-frame equivalent for overall shots: it gives a natural perspective, close to what the human eye perceives, without the distortion of an overly wide lens.

Choosing the right time and light

Light is the single biggest technical factor, well ahead of equipment choice. Avoid shooting at midday under harsh sun, which flattens colour, creates hard shadows under the fuselage and produces aggressive reflections on the canopy and polished metal surfaces.

The best windows are early morning and late afternoon, when light is softer, warmer and more directional. It reveals the shape of the fuselage and gives the aircraft a sense of depth without excessive contrast. A lightly overcast sky, without being flat grey, also gives very even lighting that is ideal for detail shots.

  • Avoid shooting in direct sunlight between 11am and 3pm, especially in summer.
  • Keep the sun behind you or slightly to the side, never facing the lens.
  • On overcast days, move slightly closer to compensate for the lower natural contrast.
  • For interior shots, open doors and windows to let in as much natural light as possible before adding supplementary lighting.

The essential exterior angles

A complete set of exterior shots should let a buyer mentally reconstruct the whole aircraft, as if walking around it. Vary your angles rather than taking several near-identical front shots.

  • Three-quarter front view — the ideal cover photo, showing the nose, one wing and the side of the aircraft in a single frame.
  • Full profile — side-on, the whole aircraft in frame, to give an accurate sense of proportions and silhouette.
  • Three-quarter rear view — tail surfaces, rudder and any vertical fin clearly visible.
  • Underside of the fuselage and landing gear — crouched down or with the phone held low, to show tyre condition, gear legs and wheel fairings.
  • Close-up of each wing — leading edge, flaps, ailerons and tip, to spot any impact damage or repair marks.
  • Propeller or engine intake — blade condition, no nicks or signs of corrosion.

Cabin, cockpit and engine bay

The interior of a used aircraft is often scrutinised at least as closely as the exterior, since the condition of the upholstery and avionics reveals how the aircraft was actually used and maintained day to day.

  • Full instrument panel — a single wide shot from the pilot's seat showing all instruments, radios and installed avionics.
  • Close-ups of recent instruments — GPS, autopilot, mode S transponder: this equipment has a strong influence on value and deserves a dedicated photo.
  • Seats, carpets and trim — from several angles, showing the real condition rather than hiding visible wear.
  • Engine bay with cowling open — overall cleanliness, no signs of oil leaks, condition of visible hoses and wiring.
  • Engine and airframe serial numbers — a sharp, legible photo of each data plate, letting the buyer cross-check the information against the engine logbook and flight logbook.
Word of caution — Never hide a visible defect (corrosion, crack, impact mark) by avoiding photographing it. A buyer who discovers it during the pre-purchase inspection, after seeing a "perfect" listing, loses trust and negotiates harder, or walks away entirely.

Photographing documents and reassuring details

Beyond the aircraft itself, a few document photos speed up the buyer's decision by answering their most common questions upfront.

  • Cover page of the flight logbook, showing total accumulated hours.
  • Latest entry in the engine logbook, with hours since overhaul (SMOH) clearly readable.
  • Currently valid certificate of airworthiness (CofA).
  • Latest invoice or significant maintenance report, if it highlights recent work (avionics, paint, engine overhaul).

Always blur sensitive personal information (full address, phone number, banking details) before publishing these document photos in a public listing.

Mistakes that drive buyers away

A handful of mistakes come up again and again in listings that sit online for months without generating a serious enquiry. Fixing them takes little extra effort compared to the benefit.

  • A single photo, or too few — a listing with fewer than eight photos feels incomplete and triggers distrust.
  • Cluttered background — a messy hangar, other aircraft, maintenance equipment scattered around: clear the area before shooting, or pick an angle that isolates the aircraft.
  • Photos shot in the rain or with a dirty fuselage — better to wait for a good weather window than publish dull, unflattering photos.
  • Tight crops that cut off part of the aircraft — wings or tail out of frame, which prevents the buyer from appreciating the full silhouette.
  • Heavy filters or editing — artificially boosted saturation or contrast looks suspicious and hurts the listing's credibility.
  • No interior photo — a buyer who only sees the outside often assumes the worst about cockpit condition.

Organizing and naming your files before publishing

Once the shoot is done, take a few minutes to sort through the shots before uploading them to your listing. Delete blurry duplicates, crop slightly if needed to balance the composition, and arrange the photos in a logical order: overall exterior views first, then technical details, then the interior, then documents.

This order naturally guides the buyer's eye and mirrors the sequence they would follow if viewing the aircraft in person: a general impression first, then a closer look. A visually well-structured listing inspires more trust than a simple gallery of photos in random order.

Tip — Refresh your photos if the aircraft stays listed for several months: dated photos (fallen leaves, snow) make a listing look stale, which can make a buyer suspect there is a hidden problem.
