Aircraft for Flight Schools: Which Model to Choose

Ruggedness, operating cost, ease of maintenance and resale value: the criteria that really matter for a flying club or ATO investing in a training aircraft.

Criteria specific to a flight school

An aircraft bought for a flight school is not purchased with the same priorities as a personal touring aircraft. It will fly intensively, be handled by inexperienced students, absorb hard landings and change pilots constantly throughout the day. The decisive criteria therefore become airframe and landing gear ruggedness, simplicity of the controls, tolerance to piloting errors, hourly operating cost, and the long-term availability of spare parts.

Ease of maintenance matters almost as much as flying qualities: a school fleet grounded for an unavailable part or overly complex upkeep quickly loses profitability, especially when the aircraft is shared by several instructors in continuous rotation.

The classic single-engine trainers

The Cessna 172 and the Piper PA-28 remain the world's reference training aircraft, for very concrete reasons: simple mechanics well known to every certified mechanic, predictable and forgiving flying characteristics, and an abundant spare-parts market that limits downtime. The Diamond DA40, more modern and better equipped with glass cockpit avionics, is winning over an increasing number of schools that want to train students directly on a digital cockpit representative of the aircraft they will fly later in their careers.

For dual instruction and early training, these three models cover the vast majority of a general-purpose school's needs, from the first flight through solo and on to private pilot licence preparation.

Microlights and light aircraft: an economical alternative

Many schools, particularly for introductory flights and first-flight experiences, turn to two-seat microlights or light aircraft such as the Tecnam P2002/P2008. The main appeal is a significantly lower hourly cost than a classic certified single-engine, thanks to reduced fuel burn and lighter maintenance requirements under the applicable microlight category rules. The downside is a more limited payload and, for some models, greater sensitivity to crosswind, which can complicate the first few hours of training in gusty conditions.

Many schools combine both families: microlights for lower-cost introductory flights, then a certified single-engine for progression towards the licence and subsequent ratings.

Operating cost and flight hours

Total hourly cost (fuel, scheduled maintenance, overhaul reserve, insurance) is the figure most scrutinised by school managers, since it feeds directly into the rate charged to students. A classic single-engine generally burns more fuel than a microlight or a modern fuel-injected light aircraft, but compensates with reliability proven over decades of intensive school use worldwide.

Tip — Always calculate the full hourly cost over the aircraft's expected fleet lifespan, engine overhaul reserve included, not just the price of fuel per litre.

Maintenance and parts availability

A school fleet flies far more hours per year than a personal touring aircraft, which mechanically brings the engine overhaul and periodic inspections closer. Choosing a widely used model (Cessna 172, Piper PA-28) generally guarantees a larger network of approved workshops and a wider stock of spare parts, reducing costly downtime.

  • Check the real availability of critical parts (landing gear, engine) from the manufacturer or PMA-approved suppliers.
  • Favour a Part-145 / Part-CAO workshop experienced with the type, ideally close to the home airfield.
  • Plan ahead for the cost and downtime of a major overhaul over the expected ownership period.

Buying new or used for a school fleet

Buying used remains the most common way to start or renew a school fleet, provided the aircraft's usage history is well documented: a Cessna 172 or Piper PA-28 that has already served in a flight school is not a problem in itself, as long as the logbook reflects rigorous upkeep and the remaining engine hours before overhaul (SMOH) justify the asking price.

Buying new mainly appeals to ATOs that want a uniform fleet with a manufacturer warranty and identical avionics across every aircraft, a real asset for simplifying instructor training and maintenance, at the cost of a significantly higher initial investment.

Resale value and fleet lifespan

The most widespread models (Cessna 172, Piper PA-28) hold relatively stable resale value on the used market, precisely because demand from schools and flying clubs remains steady over time. An aircraft used intensively in a school accumulates more hours, but this is logically reflected in the resale price, especially if maintenance has been rigorous and well documented throughout its use.

Tip — A complete logbook and a recent major overhaul add more value to a school aircraft than a low total-hours figure that is poorly documented.

Our recommendation by school profile

For a general-purpose school training students from first flight through to the private pilot licence, the Cessna 172 or the Piper PA-28 remain the safest choice: proven ruggedness, manageable maintenance cost and an abundant parts market. For a school that wants to train students directly on digital avionics, the Diamond DA40 is a sound investment despite a higher operating cost. For lower-cost introductory flights or first-flight experiences, the two-seat microlight remains the most economical option, often combined with a certified single-engine for the rest of the training path.

Tip — Always have an independent pre-purchase inspection carried out before adding an aircraft to your school fleet; its history of intensive use makes this step even more decisive than for a private purchase.
