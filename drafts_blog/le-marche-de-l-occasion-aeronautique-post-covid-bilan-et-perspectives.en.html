Post-Covid Used Aircraft Market: Review and Outlook

Rising prices, tight stock, a gradual return to normal: how the European used aircraft market went through the health crisis, and where it is heading in 2026.

The initial 2020-2021 shock on the used aircraft market

At the start of the health crisis, the European used aircraft market slowed sharply: travel restrictions, the temporary closure of many flying clubs and general uncertainty held back transactions for several months. Many sellers chose to delay listing their aircraft, preferring to wait for better visibility, which mechanically reduced the volume of listings available on a market that was already structurally smaller than the car market.

At the same time, demand did not collapse as durably as feared: some potential buyers, faced with limits on international travel, rediscovered the appeal of light aviation for regional trips, maintaining a base level of demand that turned out to be decisive for what followed.

The rapid rebound and its effect on prices

As soon as the main restrictions were lifted, demand recovered faster than supply could rebuild. This imbalance put upward pressure on used-aircraft prices, particularly for well-equipped touring single-engine aircraft and high-demand types such as high-performance single-engines. Selling times, already short on some segments before the crisis, shortened further for aircraft in good overall condition with a clear maintenance history.

This dynamic also benefited sellers who had kept their aircraft on the market during the most uncertain period: many saw their listings close faster, and sometimes at a higher price than originally expected — a reversal of the balance of power compared with previous years, when buyers had more room to negotiate.

Parts shortages and longer major overhaul lead times

The global logistics disruptions seen during and after the health crisis hit aviation as they hit many other industrial sectors: longer lead times for spare parts, reduced availability of certain engine components, and consequently longer major overhaul lead times at many approved maintenance workshops. For a buyer, this context made it even more important to check precisely, before signing anything, the number of engine hours remaining before the next major overhaul (often summarised by the SMOH indicator — hours since major overhaul), since an aircraft nearing that milestone can now involve a longer wait and a more uncertain repair cost than before.

This situation gradually rebalanced valuations between aircraft: a used aircraft with high remaining engine potential and a clean logbook commanded a more pronounced price premium than before the crisis, while aircraft nearing their major overhaul became harder to negotiate.

Market segments affected unevenly

Not every light aviation segment experienced the crisis and its aftermath the same way. Classic touring single-engines and microlights, the most financially accessible segments, saw a quick revival of interest, driven partly by newly qualified pilots drawn to recreational flying during this period. Light twins and turboprops, more expensive to buy and to operate, saw a more gradual recovery, more closely tied to the general economic confidence of professional and corporate buyers.

The used business jet segment also followed its own particular dynamic, with sustained demand from buyers seeking more flexibility and control over their travel — a trend that has partly persisted beyond the crisis period itself.

Light helicopters and turboprops used for utility missions (aerial work, training, regional connections) followed an intermediate trajectory: less exposed to pure leisure demand, they were mainly sensitive to the pace of recovery of the professional activities they serve, with a later but generally more stable restart than the one seen in the more speculative segments of the market.

Logbooks and traceability: a more scrutinised criterion

The combination of tighter supply and longer maintenance lead times has made buyers more demanding about the documentary quality of aircraft offered for sale. A complete, consistent logbook with no chronological gaps has become an even stronger selling point than before the crisis, as has an up-to-date certificate of airworthiness and its associated airworthiness review certificate.

  • Consistent engine and airframe logbooks — any inconsistency between declared hours is now a more systematic point of negotiation than before the crisis.
  • Parts supply history — check that no repair was left pending for lack of part availability, a more frequent scenario since 2021.
  • Up-to-date airworthiness certificate status — a particularly sensitive point since renewal lead times at some workshops have sometimes lengthened.
Tip — In the current context, never negotiate solely on the stated build year: remaining engine potential and logbook quality weigh more heavily on a used aircraft's real value than they did before the crisis.

A gradual normalisation since 2024-2025

Since 2024-2025, the market has shown signs of gradual normalisation: spare parts supply has broadly improved compared with the tightest point of the crisis, major overhaul lead times have partially eased at many workshops, and the volume of listings available on the European used-aircraft market has rebuilt compared with the trough seen in 2020-2021. Price gaps between comparable aircraft, which had sometimes widened during the most acute period, are tending to narrow again.

This normalisation nevertheless remains uneven across segments and regions: some highly sought-after aircraft types continue to sell quickly, while other, more niche segments are returning to selling times closer to those seen before 2020.

Specialist online listing platforms have also changed how buyers and sellers meet in this market: the greater visibility these tools offer has made it easier for sellers to reach buyers in other European countries than before, and for buyers to compare a wider range of listings before deciding, which has itself helped gradually narrow the price gaps observed between regions for the same type of aircraft.

Outlook for buyers and sellers in 2026

For a buyer in 2026, the market generally remains more seller-friendly than in the years before the health crisis, even though the tension has eased compared with the 2021-2022 peak. Comparing several listings for the same aircraft type, systematically checking the logbook and remaining engine potential, and not focusing solely on the advertised price remain the best habits for buying under good conditions.

For a seller, the context generally remains favourable in the most sought-after segments, provided a complete and up-to-date documentary file is presented: it is precisely this traceability criterion, which has become more decisive since the crisis, that now makes it possible to stand out and sell faster.

Word of caution — Never rely on a general market trend to value a specific aircraft: actual condition, remaining engine potential and logbook quality remain, today as before the crisis, the factors that determine the value of a given used aircraft.
