Used Piper Cherokee Six: Buying Guide

A six-seat single-engine aircraft built to carry a family or passengers over long distances with generous payload. Budget, inspection points and maintenance before you buy.

The Cherokee Six: a 6-seat family single

The Piper Cherokee Six (PA-32) was designed as a longer, wider version of the Cherokee family, with a clear goal: offer six seats and a payload well above that of a classic PA-28, while remaining a single-engine aircraft that's simple to operate. Its spacious cabin, large rear cargo door that makes loading easier, and stable handling in flight have made it a well-liked aircraft for family travel and light transport missions.

On the used market, the Cherokee Six remains sought after by owners who regularly need to carry several passengers and bags, without wanting to deal with the complexity and cost of a twin. It's a frequent choice for large families, flying clubs or professionals who use the aircraft as a transportation tool rather than a pure leisure aircraft.

Variants and engines: PA-32 and Saratoga

The Cherokee Six went through several evolutions during its production run, with different engine power ratings depending on the version. The lineage was later continued with the Piper Saratoga, which keeps the same six-seat cabin philosophy but with a modernized airframe and avionics, and in some versions a retractable landing gear.

Before buying, it's essential to identify precisely the version and the engine fitted to the aircraft you're considering, since performance, fuel burn and maintenance costs can differ significantly between early fixed-gear generations and later, better-equipped versions. The logbook should clearly document the history of any modifications and engine replacements.

  • Pin down the exact version (PA-32, Saratoga variant) before comparing listings.
  • Check the installed engine and its consistency with the logbook.
  • Compare advertised performance against the payload you actually need.

Budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used Cherokee Six depends mainly on its year of production, the number of engine hours remaining before overhaul, the avionics level and the general condition of the airframe. The oldest examples, with reduced engine potential and basic avionics, trade at a noticeably more accessible entry point than better-equipped or more recent versions.

Beyond the purchase price, factor extra costs into the overall budget: a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic, insurance, registration and transfer fees, and possibly an avionics upgrade if the aircraft needs one.

Points to check before buying

As with any used aircraft, a pre-purchase inspection carried out by an independent mechanic qualified on the type is essential before closing a deal. For a Cherokee Six, a few points deserve particular attention because of its large cabin and frequent use at high loads.

  • Complete engine history: hours since major overhaul (SMOH), compliance with the manufacturer's maintenance program.
  • Airframe condition and corrosion check, especially on the spars and cabin floor, which are subject to repeated loads.
  • Condition and seal of the large rear cargo/passenger door.
  • Consistency between the actual empty weight and the weighing report — essential on an aircraft regularly loaded close to its maximum weight.
  • Complete logbook and technical file, with no gaps in the aircraft's history.
Good to know — Always ask for the current weight-and-balance report: on an aircraft built to carry people and baggage, a bad center of gravity at full load is a real risk, not a paperwork formality.

Payload and real-world use: family, baggage, light cargo

The Cherokee Six's main strength remains its payload, clearly higher than a comparably powered classic PA-28. This capacity lets you carry several passengers with substantial baggage, or transport equipment for light professional use, without running into the maximum takeoff weight the moment the cabin is full.

Before buying, it's worth working out your actual intended use in detail — typical number of passengers, baggage weight, typical trip distances — to check that the configuration and engine of the aircraft you're considering actually match that need, since some older or less powerful versions can limit the usable payload once the tanks are full.

Keep the classic fuel-versus-payload trade-off in mind too: on an aircraft designed to fly full of people and bags, topping off the tanks eats directly into the payload left for occupants. Many Cherokee Six owners quickly learn to adjust fuel load to the mission — a short hop with six people and baggage, or a longer leg with fewer passengers — rather than filling the tanks before every departure as a matter of habit.

Maintenance and running costs

Running costs for a Cherokee Six remain comparable to those of a piston single of equivalent power, with a few specifics tied to its size and frequent use at high loads. The main cost items remain the mandatory annual inspection, insurance, hangarage — which can be more expensive given the aircraft's wingspan — and, over the longer term, the engine overhaul once hour potential is reached.

Having the aircraft maintained at an approved shop familiar with the type helps limit unpleasant surprises and preserve resale value. Thorough, well-documented maintenance records, especially on the airframe and landing gear that absorb repeated loads, are a strong argument at resale time.

Tip — Always ask for the last three maintenance invoices before making an offer: they give an accurate picture of how seriously the aircraft has been maintained, particularly on an aircraft regularly flown at high loads.

Resale and the used market

The Cherokee Six benefits from an active used market in Europe and the United States, supported by a large installed fleet and good parts availability thanks to its kinship with the rest of the PA-28/PA-32 family. A well-maintained aircraft, with a transparent history and demonstrated payload capability, generally resells more easily than one whose technical file has gaps.

Comparing several listings of similar models and model years remains the best way to assess a fair price, whether buying or preparing for a future resale. Price differences between two aircraft of the same model year are almost always explained by remaining engine hours and the documented quality of maintenance.

It's also worth looking at demand on each regional market: some regions place a particularly high value on the Cherokee Six's payload and versatility for agricultural, touring or light transport work, which can shorten resale time and support a stronger price compared with a plain four-seat single of comparable power.

Finally, a buyer planning to keep the aircraft for several years is better off budgeting for the eventual engine overhaul cost right from the purchase, rather than discovering it at resale time: a Cherokee Six with low remaining engine potential will inevitably trade at a discount reflecting that looming expense, which should be factored into the initial purchase negotiation.
