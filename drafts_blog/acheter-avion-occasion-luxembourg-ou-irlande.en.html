Buying a used aircraft in Luxembourg or Ireland: which country to choose

Market, prices, tax, registration and maintenance: a comparison between Luxembourg and Ireland to help you decide where to search for your used aircraft.

A landlocked continental country versus an English-speaking island

Buying a used aircraft in Luxembourg or Ireland puts the buyer in front of two very different market profiles, despite one strong point in common: both are EU and EASA member states with a light aviation fleet that is modest compared with their larger neighbours. Luxembourg is a tiny continental territory wedged between Belgium, France and Germany, which lets it permanently lean on the markets and maintenance networks of those three neighbours. Ireland, by contrast, is an island set apart from the continent: its light aviation market is larger and more self-contained, but geographically isolated, with a ferry or air journey required for any cross-border viewing, inspection or ferry flight.

For a French- or German-speaking buyer, Luxembourg offers the advantage of immediate proximity and technical documentation that is often available in three languages (French, German, English). Ireland, on the other hand, naturally appeals to an English-speaking buyer through the simplicity of the language and through an aviation ecosystem historically built around business aviation and international aircraft leasing: Dublin is one of the world's major hubs for aircraft finance and leasing, an industry that indirectly feeds the whole local aviation sector, including general aviation.

Both countries apply the same European certification rules, which simplifies a cross-border purchase on the technical side, but the geographic distance and the sea or air crossing needed to reach Ireland from the continent weigh far more heavily in the logistics equation than between Luxembourg and its direct land neighbours.

Size and activity of the used market

Luxembourg's used market stays limited by the sheer size of the country: few airfields, a limited number of aircraft based locally, but a regular international clientele who take advantage of the country's border position to compare listings in neighbouring countries before deciding. The relative scarcity of local supply is largely offset by easy access to the Belgian, French and German markets, often just a short drive away.

The Irish market is larger in absolute volume, supported by an active network of flying clubs around Dublin, Cork, Shannon and Waterford, as well as a well-established tradition of recreational light aviation (touring single-engine aircraft, microlights, gliders, aerobatics). The country's insularity, however, limits direct comparison with continental markets: an Irish buyer has to work with a more closed local supply, since ferrying an aircraft bought on the continent requires air or sea transport that is considerably more costly than a simple road trip.

In both cases, the volume of listings available remains far below that of Germany, the United Kingdom or France, which often justifies widening the search to several countries at once rather than focusing on a single national market.

Comparing the prices charged

Price levels in Ireland and Luxembourg remain broadly in line with European averages for a comparable model and maintenance condition. The differences observed owe more to the local scarcity of certain aircraft types than to a structural difference between the two countries. In Luxembourg, immediate proximity to several large neighbouring markets limits scarcity premiums: a buyer can always turn to the Belgian, French or German supply if the local market has nothing suitable. In Ireland, geographic isolation can instead push up the price of models that are poorly represented locally, for lack of an easily accessible alternative without sea or air transport.

A buyer specifically targeting one of these two small markets should set up alerts and move quickly once a listing matches their criteria, always subject to a satisfactory pre-purchase inspection.

Tax and purchase-related costs

A private sale involving an aircraft already in free circulation within the European Union generally does not trigger a new VAT charge, whether the sale takes place in Luxembourg or Ireland, since the tax was already paid when the aircraft was first put into circulation within the EU. The situation differs for a sale made by a VAT-registered professional, or for an aircraft imported from outside the EU, which can trigger import VAT calculated on its customs value, in either country, according to the rates and procedures specific to each national tax administration.

For a cross-border transaction between Luxembourg and Ireland, two countries that do not share a land border, it is worth checking precisely any intra-EU VAT formalities applicable between professionals, as well as the transport and insurance costs involved in ferrying the aircraft between the two territories. Using a professional familiar with this type of transaction (broker, lawyer specialised in aviation law) helps avoid procedural mistakes that can prove costly, particularly around customs documentation and the applicable VAT calculation.

Registration: Luxembourg's DAC versus Ireland's IAA

In Luxembourg, registration falls under the Direction de l'Aviation Civile (DAC), which issues the LX- prefix. In Ireland, the competent authority is the Irish Aviation Authority (IAA), which issues the EI- prefix. An aircraft bought in either country can be registered in the other, depending on the buyer's choice: keeping the registration of the purchase country is not mandatory.

  • Check the consistency between the logbook, the technical file and the aircraft's actual condition before any change of registration.
  • For an aircraft imported from another country, plan for its prior deregistration before the new registration can be issued.
  • Plan for administrative lead times, which can vary significantly between the DAC and the IAA.
  • Gather a valid Certificate of Airworthiness (CofA), the bill of sale and proof of liability insurance before the first flight under the new registration.
Good to know — Common EASA rules govern transfers between member states and make them simpler than an import from outside the European Union. Expect generally 2 to 8 weeks depending on how complete the file is, regardless of the destination country.

Maintenance network, logistics and language

Luxembourg, far too small a country to maintain a full network of approved workshops on its own territory, benefits directly from the immediate proximity of the Belgian, French and German maintenance networks: no competent workshop is ever very far away, and a missing part can often be delivered the same day from a neighbouring country. Ireland, in comparison, has its own network of approved maintenance organisations concentrated around the main airfields, but the country's insularity often lengthens delivery times for specific parts or rare engines, which frequently have to travel by ferry or by air from the United Kingdom or the continent.

For rarer models or specific engines, it remains useful to check in advance the availability of parts and the proximity of a specialised workshop before finalising the purchase, regardless of the aircraft's country of origin. Ireland's main advantage here is the language: technical documentation and dealings with parts suppliers are exclusively in English, which makes ordering from major British and North American suppliers easier, a welcome asset for the American-built models that are so common in light aviation.

In Luxembourg, the language barrier is generally limited: a large share of aviation professionals speak English alongside French or German, which also makes interactions easy for a buyer coming from abroad.

Which country to favour depending on your profile

For a buyer based on the European continent, used to quickly comparing several listings in neighbouring countries and arranging a pre-purchase inspection without any sea-transport constraint, the Luxembourg market remains the easiest to access, thanks to its position at the heart of several borders. For an English-speaking buyer, or one ready to tap into Ireland's dynamic aviation ecosystem (leasing, business aviation, an active community of pilots), the Irish market offers a larger local supply, at the cost of heavier cross-border logistics if the search is widened to the continent.

Good to know — In all cases, a pre-purchase inspection by a qualified independent mechanic remains essential, whether the aircraft is located in Luxembourg, Ireland or elsewhere in Europe.

Ultimately, the country of purchase matters less than the actual condition of the aircraft and the quality of its maintenance record. Comparing listings from both markets, while factoring in the real cost of ferrying the aircraft, gives you a stronger negotiating position and a fuller picture of the supply available across Europe.
