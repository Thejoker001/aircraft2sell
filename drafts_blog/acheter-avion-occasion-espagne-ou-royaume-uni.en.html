Buying a Used Aircraft in Spain or the UK: Which Country to Choose

Market, prices, registration, post-Brexit VAT and pitfalls to avoid: a complete comparison to help you decide where to buy your used light aircraft between Spain and the United Kingdom.

Two light aviation markets with different profiles

Spain and the United Kingdom offer two fairly different faces of light aviation in Europe. Spain benefits from a climate favourable to flying all year round, an active network of airfields and flying schools, particularly in the south of the country, and a used market driven in part by microlights, touring single-engine aircraft and gliders in certain regions. The United Kingdom is one of the largest historic general aviation markets in Europe, with a very dense network of airfields and a considerable installed base of touring single-engine aircraft, two-seaters and gliders.

Spain directly applies EASA airworthiness standards as an EU member state. Since Brexit, the United Kingdom has had its own civil aviation authority, independent of EASA, for registration and airworthiness, while largely retaining procedures inspired by the European framework for light aircraft. This regulatory difference is the key point to understand before comparing a Spanish listing with a British one.

The British market is also marked by airfield operations on the left-hand pattern and by widespread use of pounds sterling in listings, which adds a currency factor to take into account when comparing prices with Spain, in the eurozone.

Price comparison by aircraft type

Price ranges remain broadly comparable between the two countries for an aircraft of equivalent category and condition, with price level depending above all on engine history (hours remaining before TBO), airframe condition and installed avionics rather than the country of sale alone. The GBP/EUR exchange rate can, however, noticeably shift the displayed price of a British aircraft once converted, which is worth tracking when comparing two listings.

Registration: AESA versus CAA

In Spain, every aircraft based and operated in the country must be registered with the Agencia Estatal de Seguridad Aérea (AESA), under the EC- prefix. In the United Kingdom, the competent authority is the Civil Aviation Authority (CAA), which assigns the G- prefix. In both countries, a purchase between private individuals where the aircraft is already registered locally simply requires updating the registered owner, while an aircraft imported from the other country must first be deregistered from its register of origin and then newly registered in the destination country.

  • Valid certificate of airworthiness — issued under the EASA framework in Spain, under the CAA's own post-Brexit framework in the United Kingdom.
  • Bill of sale — dated, signed by both parties, stating the serial number, required by both AESA and the CAA.
  • Liability insurance certificate — required before first use under the new registration, in both Spain and the United Kingdom.
Good to know — A registration transfer between two EU member states, such as between Spain and another EU country, follows a common EASA framework that is generally fast. A transfer between Spain and the United Kingdom now involves dealing with two separate authorities (AESA and the CAA) applying their own procedures, which can lengthen timelines slightly compared with an intra-EU transfer: build in extra time and check the exact formalities with the CAA before committing.

VAT and customs: the effect of Brexit

This is the most important point of difference between the two countries since 2021. Spain, as an EU member, applies the common EU VAT regime: a sale between EU resident private individuals is generally VAT-exempt, while a sale by a professional or an import from outside the EU remains subject to import VAT.

The United Kingdom is no longer part of the European Union or its common customs regime following Brexit. An aircraft imported into the UK from the EU, or the reverse, may now be subject to customs formalities and to UK VAT (calculated on the value of the aircraft), except in specific situations that need to be confirmed case by case with the relevant authorities. It is essential to have your tax and customs situation confirmed before acquisition, particularly for an aircraft that has already changed country of registration several times since Brexit.

Pre-purchase inspection and documents

Whether the aircraft is Spanish or British, a pre-purchase inspection by a certified mechanic independent of the seller remains the best protection against unpleasant surprises. It covers the condition of the airframe, the consistency of the engine logbook, maintenance history and the compliance of the avionics with current requirements (transponder, 8.33 kHz radio) — requirements that remain broadly similar in both countries despite the post-Brexit regulatory split.

  • Up-to-date airframe, engine and propeller logbooks, with no chronological gaps, whether kept in Spain or the United Kingdom.
  • History of maintenance invoices from the last three years, from an approved workshop in the relevant country.
  • No undisclosed structural damage, verifiable via the maintenance file and a thorough visual inspection.
Point of caution — Never finalise a purchase, in Spain or in the United Kingdom, without having seen the original logbooks and having their consistency checked by a professional independent of the seller.

Which country to choose depending on your profile

The choice between Spain and the United Kingdom depends above all on your project and your tolerance for administrative steps. Spain appeals to buyers who value weather conditions favourable to regular flying and the administrative advantage of a simplified registration transfer within the European Union, with no customs formalities. The United Kingdom retains a historic and very active light aviation and gliding market, with a particularly abundant supply of gliders and touring single-engine aircraft, but since Brexit requires anticipating additional customs and tax formalities, particularly in the event of a cross-border import or export.

In all cases, the decisive factor remains the quality of maintenance and the transparency of the aircraft's history, far more than the country of registration alone. Comparing several listings in both countries, at an equivalent budget and category, and always converting British prices at the day's exchange rate, remains the best way to make an informed choice.

Tip — If you are considering an import between the two countries, have the post-Brexit customs and tax formalities confirmed in advance by a specialist professional: this avoids unpleasant surprises after signing.
