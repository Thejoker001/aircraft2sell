Landing gear: common failures to check before buying

The landing gear absorbs every take-off and every landing. Here are the most common failures and the points you must inspect before buying a used aircraft.

Landing gear: a component under heavy strain

The landing gear is one of the most heavily loaded components on the aircraft: it absorbs the aircraft's weight at every landing, soaks up the shock of uneven ground, and carries the full load of every ground manoeuvre, from taxiing to braking. Unlike the engine, whose condition is tracked through precise hour counts, landing gear wear is often less well documented in maintenance logbooks, which makes it a particular point of attention when buying a used aircraft.

A poorly maintained gear does not necessarily mean an immediate failure: corrosion and material fatigue progress silently, and a defect can remain invisible for several years before suddenly manifesting itself, sometimes during a slightly hard landing. That is why inspecting the landing gear deserves at least as much attention as the engine or the airframe.

The most common failures and wear patterns

Certain failures recur very regularly on used aircraft, whether a light single-engine, a helicopter or a heavier aircraft. Knowing them helps you know where to focus during a viewing or a pre-purchase inspection.

  • Corrosion of legs and fittings — encouraged by outdoor storage, a coastal environment or insufficient washing, it weakens structural attachment points.
  • Leaking or under-pressurised shock struts (oleo-pneumatic legs) — shows up as a gear that sags on the ground or a hard, jarring touchdown.
  • Worn brakes and discs — common on aircraft used intensively for flight training, checked via the remaining thickness of the linings.
  • Cracked, flat-spotted or under-inflated tires — often a telltale sign of routine maintenance neglected by the seller.
  • Excessive play in joints and bushings — causes vibration and accelerates wear of other gear components.
  • Retraction system failure (cables, actuators, electric motor, hydraulic pump) — specific to retractable gear, with the risk of a gear that fails to extend or retract fully.
  • Faulty position sensors — false "gear up/down" indications that complicate diagnosis both in flight and on the ground.
Word of caution — A hard landing or a runway excursion in the aircraft's history means you must check that a structural inspection of the gear and its attachment points was actually carried out and documented, even if the aircraft looks fine visually.

Fixed vs. retractable gear: different risks

Fixed gear is mechanically simpler: fewer parts, no retraction system, hence fewer potential failure points and generally lower maintenance costs. Failures mostly concern the shock struts, tires and brakes.

Retractable gear adds a significant layer of complexity: actuators, cables, position sensors, and sometimes a dedicated hydraulic pump or electric motor. Each additional component is a possible failure point, and the periodic inspections tied to the retraction system represent a recurring maintenance cost that should not be underestimated over the ownership period.

How to inspect the gear before buying

Inspecting the landing gear must be a full part of any pre-purchase inspection, not just a quick visual glance during the viewing.

  • Visual examination of the legs, fittings and attachment points for corrosion, cracks or deformation.
  • Checking the pressure and the absence of leaks on oleo-pneumatic shock struts.
  • Checking the condition and wear of tires, brakes and discs.
  • Retraction and extension test for retractable gear, ideally on jacks or during a test flight with an approved mechanic on board.
  • Careful review of the maintenance logbook to identify any past intervention on the gear, any reported hard landing, or any applicable airworthiness directive.
Tip — Always ask your independent mechanic to explicitly include the landing gear in the scope of the pre-purchase inspection; this is not automatic unless clearly requested.

Budget: repair and maintenance costs

The cost of landing gear work varies widely depending on the nature of the defect. Replacing tires or brake linings remains routine maintenance, modest compared to the price of the aircraft. On the other hand, overhauling an oleo-pneumatic leg, replacing a retraction actuator, or carrying out a structural repair linked to advanced corrosion can represent a significant expense, sometimes comparable to a minor engine intervention.

Before committing to an aircraft, ask an approved workshop for an indicative quote for any anomaly found during the inspection: this lets you calculate your negotiating margin precisely.

Regulation and airworthiness directives

Like the engine or the airframe, the landing gear may be subject to specific airworthiness directives (AD) for certain aircraft types, requiring periodic inspections or limiting the life of certain parts. Before buying, ask the seller for the complete list of applicable directives and their compliance status; if in doubt, your mechanic can check the manufacturer's and the relevant airworthiness authority's databases.

Impact on resale value

Well-maintained landing gear with a documented history and no reported incident is a strong resale argument: prospective buyers, or their mechanics, pay close attention to it. Conversely, an undocumented hard landing, corrosion treated too late, or a temperamental retraction system can significantly reduce the perceived value of the aircraft, even if the engine and avionics are flawless.

Keeping every invoice and work report related to the gear is therefore a direct investment in the aircraft's future resale value.

Frequently asked questions

What are the most common landing gear failures?

The most common are corrosion of the legs and fittings, worn shock struts (oil leaks, loss of nitrogen pressure), worn brakes and tires, excessive play in the linkages, and, on retractable gear, electrical or hydraulic failures of the retraction system.

How can I tell if the landing gear of a used aircraft is in good condition?

Visually inspect the legs, shock struts, tires and brakes, review the maintenance logbook for the gear's service history, and have a retraction/extension test performed (for retractable gear) by an approved mechanic during the pre-purchase inspection.

Is retractable landing gear more expensive to maintain than fixed gear?

Generally yes. Retractable gear adds mechanical, electrical or hydraulic components (actuators, cables, position sensors, sometimes a hydraulic pump) that increase the number of potential failure points and the cost of periodic inspections compared to fixed gear.
