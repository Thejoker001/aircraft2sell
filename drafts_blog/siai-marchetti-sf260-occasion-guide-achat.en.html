An aircraft built for military training and aerobatics

The SIAI-Marchetti SF260, designed by Italian engineer Stelio Frati, was originally conceived as a selection and training aircraft for air forces, before becoming widely available on the civilian market. Its low-wing metal airframe, honest handling qualities and capacity for advanced aerobatics make it a distinctive aircraft among used light aircraft, far more a "military trainer" in character than the typical touring single-engine of similar power class.

Used by several air forces around the world for initial pilot selection and training, the SF260 has earned a reputation as a demanding but honest aircraft, one that rewards precise flying and gives little margin for gross piloting errors. It is precisely this characteristic that now draws civilian buyers looking for a capable aerobatic aircraft, often at a more accessible purchase price than other aircraft purpose-built solely for that discipline.

What budget to plan for a used SF260

The price of a used SF260 depends heavily on its provenance: a demilitarised ex-military example registered in the civil category is generally not negotiated in the same range as an aircraft that has remained in civilian use since it left the factory, with a continuous civilian maintenance trail. The overall condition of the airframe, total airframe and engine hours, the presence or absence of a full aerobatic fit (reinforced harnesses, smoke system) and the quality of the installed avionics also strongly influence the final price.

As with any aircraft of this type, remaining engine potential before the major overhaul remains a central negotiating point, and this is even more sensitive on an aircraft used for aerobatics, where structural and engine stresses are generally higher than for ordinary touring use.

Good to know — Always ask whether the aircraft has had intensive military instructional use or more moderate civilian use: this information often weighs more heavily on real value than a simple total-hours figure.

Versions to know: F, M, W and the turboprop variant

The SF260 was produced in several versions over its production run, with engine and equipment changes along the way. The SF260M, oriented toward military training, and the SF260W, fitted with underwing hardpoints for advanced instructional use, are among the most common variants on the used market sourced from military surplus. There is also a turboprop variant, the SF260TP, rarer and intended for advanced training use, which must not be confused with the piston-engine versions when searching listings.

Before buying, it's essential to precisely identify the version and the aircraft's use history — civilian from the outset or demilitarised ex-military — since this directly affects parts availability, available technical documentation and, in some countries, the civil registration process that applies.

What to check before buying

As with any used aircraft, a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic qualified on the type is essential before finalising a transaction. For an SF260, a few points deserve particular attention, tied to its intensive and sometimes military usage profile.

  • Complete history of the aircraft: original civilian or military use, and demilitarisation paperwork if the aircraft came from surplus.
  • Logbook and traceability of airframe and engine hours, particularly if the aircraft has seen intensive aerobatic use.
  • Condition of the structure, especially wing attachment points and areas subject to high load factors during aerobatics.
  • Operation and overhaul schedule of the reinforced safety harness and smoke system, if fitted.
  • Compliance of the certificate of airworthiness (CofA) and any usage limitations specific to the version concerned.
Good to know — A demilitarised ex-military aircraft must have a complete demilitarisation file and accessible technical documentation: its absence makes civil registration and subsequent maintenance significantly harder.

The SF260 in aerobatics: demanding but rewarding

The SF260's reputation in aerobatics rests on generous power for its class, good handling and a structure designed from the outset to withstand significant load factors, well above those of an ordinary touring aircraft. It is commonly used by amateur aerobatic pilots as well as by some advanced instructional programmes, which appreciate its honest behaviour and its ability to perform a wide range of figures.

This aerobatic vocation does, however, come at a cost in terms of structural and engine wear: an aircraft used intensively for aerobatics deserves an even more thorough inspection than an example kept in touring use, particularly on components subject to repeated fatigue. A buyer planning to fly aerobatics regularly should also make sure their own qualification and insurance properly cover this type of use on this aircraft.

Maintenance and operating costs

The operating cost of an SF260 remains broadly comparable to that of a capable piston single-engine of equivalent power, with a few specifics tied to its aerobatic use and sometimes military origin. The main expense items remain the mandatory annual inspection, insurance — potentially higher for regular aerobatic use — parking and, in the longer term, engine overhaul once the hourly potential is reached.

Parts availability can vary significantly depending on the version and the aircraft's country of origin: it's advisable to contact a workshop already familiar with the type before buying, to check how easily parts specific to this airframe can be sourced.

Tip — Always ask for recent maintenance invoices and, if possible, a contact at a workshop already experienced with the SF260 before making an offer: parts availability and the mechanic's familiarity with the type weigh heavily on real maintenance cost.

Resale and the used market

The SF260 benefits from an active niche market in Europe, supported by a community of owners and aerobatic pilots attached to the model and by a reasonable supply of examples sourced from demilitarised military surplus. A well-maintained aircraft, with a transparent technical file and clear traceability of its past use, generally resells more easily than one whose history has grey areas.

Comparing several listings of similar versions, with comparable airframe and engine hours, remains the best way to assess a fair price for this niche model. Price differences between two examples of the same model year are most often explained by remaining engine potential, equipment quality, and the clarity of the demilitarisation file when the aircraft comes from military surplus.
