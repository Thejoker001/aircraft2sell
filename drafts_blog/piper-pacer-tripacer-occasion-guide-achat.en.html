Why the Pacer and Tri-Pacer still have fans

Launched by Piper in the early 1950s, the Pacer (PA-20) and Tri-Pacer (PA-22) share the same airframe: a steel-tube fuselage covered in fabric, four seats and an instantly recognisable stocky shape. Both aircraft were once Piper's family entry-level models, long before being commercially overtaken by the Cherokee generation, yet they never really left the ramps: their mechanical simplicity, ruggedness and short-field manners have kept them popular with owners who enjoy cross-country touring and operating from unpaved strips.

On the European used market, these aircraft remain scarcer than period Cessna 172s or PA-28s, which creates a limited but genuinely active community of owners, especially within vintage-aviation networks and type clubs. That relative rarity directly affects how long a search can take: finding an example that matches your exact wishlist sometimes requires patience.

Pacer or Tri-Pacer: which gear to choose

The fundamental difference between the two models is the landing gear. The Pacer kept the conventional (tailwheel) gear inherited directly from the Clipper that preceded it, while the Tri-Pacer adopted tricycle nosewheel gear, designed to appeal to private pilots less experienced on taildraggers.

  • The Pacer, more demanding on landing and taxi, is generally sought by pilots already trained on taildraggers and drawn to its short-field performance and versatility on rough strips.
  • The Tri-Pacer, easier to handle, suits classic family touring and remains the more common of the two on the used market.
  • Some Tri-Pacers have undergone a tailwheel conversion, a well-known modification in the type community that brings the converted aircraft's handling closer to an original Pacer: always check that the conversion is fully documented and properly approved.

The choice between the two therefore depends mainly on the pilot's background and intended use: family touring on paved strips for the Tri-Pacer, backcountry flying and rough strips for the Pacer.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used Pacer or Tri-Pacer depends above all on the condition of the structure (fabric and tubing), remaining engine potential, the quality of the restoration or upkeep accumulated over decades, and the avionics fitted. Since most of these aircraft date from the 1950s, their value hinges far more on overall condition and paper-trail quality than on the actual year of manufacture.

On top of the purchase price come the usual costs: a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic familiar with the type, registration and transfer fees, insurance, and possibly a restoration budget if the fabric or avionics need upgrading in the near term.

What to check before buying

The fabric-covered tube construction of these aircraft requires specific checks, different from those on an all-metal airframe. A pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic familiar with vintage aircraft of this type is essential.

  • Condition of the fabric covering: look for signs of aging, cracking or loss of tautness, and check the date of the last full recover and the process used.
  • Corrosion of the tube structure underneath the fabric, particularly at cable anchor points, longerons and hard-to-access areas where moisture can collect.
  • Complete engine history: hours since the last overhaul (SMOH), traceability of replaced parts and compliance with the maintenance program.
  • Condition of control cables, control-surface hinges and linkages, components that take extra wear on aircraft that have flown from rough strips.
  • Complete and consistent logbook and engine logbook, plus compliance with the airworthiness directives applicable to the type.
  • If the gear has been converted (tricycle to tailwheel or vice versa), check for proper technical documentation and approval.
Good to know — On a fabric aircraft of this age, the real condition of the structure under the covering can only be properly assessed with a fabric-tension test and, if needed, a targeted probe: never rely solely on how the paint looks from outside.

Maintenance and operating costs

The operating cost of a Pacer or Tri-Pacer is broadly comparable to that of a classic light single of similar power, with one added item: the "structure and fabric" line joins the usual costs (annual inspection, insurance, fuel, hangar). A full recover is a significant expense to plan for over the ownership period if the aircraft hasn't had one recently.

Having the aircraft maintained at a shop used to fabric-covered tube construction and vintage aircraft of this type helps avoid misdiagnosis and protects its value. Parts availability remains generally reasonable thanks to an active community of owners and specialist suppliers serving the restoration of these models.

Tip — Joining a type club or owners' forum before buying gives you real feedback on competent shops and the maintenance pitfalls specific to the Pacer and Tri-Pacer.

Typical uses: touring, STOL and grass strips

The Pacer and Tri-Pacer are above all four-seat family touring aircraft, valued for their generous cabin room relative to their size and for their short take-off and landing performance. These qualities make them popular companions for pilots who regularly use grass strips or unpaved runways, especially the taildragger Pacer, whose configuration copes better with uneven ground.

A fair share of the fleet has also been modified over time: engines more powerful than the original fit, larger tyres for rough-field flying, or upgraded avionics panels. When properly documented and approved, these modifications can be a genuine asset at purchase.

Resale and the used market

The Pacer and Tri-Pacer resale market remains a niche compared to light-aviation best-sellers, but it is supported by a loyal and enthusiastic base of owners. An aircraft with a transparent technical file, a recent recover and an engine with good remaining potential generally finds a buyer without excessive difficulty, especially through vintage-aviation networks.

Comparing several listings of similar configurations (gear type, fabric condition, engine hours) remains the best way to assess a fair price, in a market where limited supply can sometimes push prices up for the best-presented examples.
