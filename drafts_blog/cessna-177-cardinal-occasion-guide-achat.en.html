The Cardinal: a different kind of Cessna single

Launched in the late 1960s to succeed the popular Cessna 172, the 177 Cardinal stood apart from its siblings right away with a more modern look: a forward-mounted landing gear that opened up the front fuselage, no wing strut thanks to a reinforced spar, and cabin visibility well ahead of other Cessna singles of the era. This more ambitious design made it a pleasant aircraft to fly, but also one built in far smaller numbers than the 172 or the 182, which directly affects availability on the used market and the price of spare parts.

On the European market, the Cardinal remains a niche aircraft sought by owners who value cabin comfort, in-flight visibility and a distinctive look over maximum parts availability or the widest choice of listings. It suits family or friends travel well, with a decent useful load on the more powerful versions.

Versions to know: 177, 177A, 177B and the engine

The original 1968 model, fitted with a modest-power engine, quickly showed performance limits that led Cessna to revise the powerplant in the following model years. The 177A and especially the 177B, fitted with a more powerful engine and a modified wing, now dominate the used market and are generally recommended for purchase thanks to more reassuring flying characteristics, particularly on takeoff and climb at high weights.

A retractable-gear variant, the Cardinal RG, also exists and trades in a different price bracket, with higher maintenance costs tied to the retractable gear mechanism. Before committing to a listing, clearly identify the exact version and model year: the gap in performance and value between an original 177 and a late 177B is significant.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used Cardinal depends mainly on the version (177, 177A or 177B), total airframe and engine hours, the remaining potential before the next major overhaul, and the condition of the avionics and interior. The oldest airframes with low remaining engine potential trade at an accessible entry level, while a well-equipped, recently overhauled 177B represents a substantially higher budget.

Beyond the purchase price, factor in the usual ancillary costs: an independent mechanic's pre-purchase inspection, familiar with the type, registration and transfer fees, insurance, and a possible avionics upgrade if the aircraft isn't fully compliant with current requirements.

What to check before buying

As with any used aircraft, a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic qualified on the type is essential. For a Cardinal, a few points deserve particular attention because of its specific design.

  • Condition of the wing spar and load-bearing structure: the strutless design relies entirely on the spar, which should be carefully checked, especially on aircraft that have flown in humid or coastal environments.
  • Signs of corrosion on the airframe, particularly around the spar, gear attachments and hard-to-access areas.
  • Complete engine history: hours since major overhaul (SMOH), compliance with the manufacturer's maintenance program, traceability of replaced parts.
  • Operation of the flap system, a Cardinal characteristic, and the overall condition of the flight controls.
  • For the RG version: condition and maintenance of the retractable gear mechanism, a specific expense item to watch closely.
  • Complete logbook and technical file, with no grey areas in the aircraft's history.
Good to know — Never rely solely on the seller's statements about overall condition: a written independent inspection report, including a specific spar check, remains the best protection against a bad surprise after purchase.

Airworthiness directives and regulatory follow-up

Like most aircraft of its generation, the Cardinal is subject to airworthiness directives and manufacturer service bulletins that have evolved over the decades, notably around monitoring certain structural elements. It's essential to verify, with the help of an approved mechanic, that the aircraft you're considering is up to date on all applicable directives and that their follow-up is properly documented in the technical file.

Also ask for confirmation of a valid Certificate of Airworthiness (CofA) and that the registration matches the file presented by the seller — two basic items to check before any serious negotiation.

Maintenance and operating costs

The operating cost of a Cardinal remains broadly comparable to that of other Cessna singles in the same category, with one caveat: availability of certain model-specific parts can be more limited than for a 172 or a 182, built in far greater numbers. The main expense items remain the mandatory annual inspection, insurance, parking and, in the longer term, engine overhaul once the hourly potential is reached.

Having the aircraft maintained at a shop familiar with the type helps limit unpleasant surprises tied to the Cardinal's specifics (spar, flaps, retractable gear on the RG version) and preserve its resale value. Rigorous, well-documented maintenance records are a strong argument at a future resale.

Tip — Always ask for the last three maintenance invoices and the history of airworthiness directives addressed before making an offer: they give an accurate picture of how seriously the aircraft has been maintained.

Resale and the used market

The used Cardinal market in Europe is smaller than that of the 172 or the 182, with a lower volume of listings but a loyal buyer base drawn to the cabin comfort and distinctive looks of the model. A well-maintained aircraft, with a verified spar, a transparent history and up-to-date avionics, generally finds a buyer within a reasonable timeframe.

Comparing several listings of the same version remains the best way to assess a fair price, both for buying and for preparing a future resale. Price differences between two aircraft of the same model year are almost always explained by remaining engine potential, spar condition and the quality of the avionics fitted.

Frequently asked questions

What's the difference between the 177, 177A and 177B?

The original 1968 model was less powerful, which led Cessna to increase engine power from the following model years onward. The 177B, with a modified wing and a more powerful engine, is the most sought-after version and the best represented on today's used market.

Is the Cardinal RG more expensive to maintain than the fixed-gear model?

Yes, the retractable gear adds a specific maintenance item (mechanism, wiring, periodic checks) that doesn't exist on the fixed-gear versions, and which should be factored into the operating budget.

Should the wing spar be a particular concern when buying?

It's indeed one of the most important points to watch on this model, given its strutless design. A specialist inspection of the load-bearing structure by a mechanic familiar with the type is recommended before any commitment.
