Used Cessna 210 Centurion: buying guide

Why the 210 Centurion still appeals to owners

The Cessna 210 Centurion holds a special place in the Cessna lineup: it is the only cabin-class single-engine from this manufacturer to have been fitted with retractable landing gear, giving it noticeably superior cruise performance compared to fixed-gear Cessnas of similar size. Its spacious cabin, which can seat up to six occupants depending on the version, and its generous payload have made it a preferred choice for family or business travel over medium to long distances.

Produced over several decades and offered in numerous versions, the Centurion went through notable engine (naturally aspirated or turbocharged) and avionics changes, which today creates a varied used-market offering in terms of age, equipment and therefore price.

The main Centurion variants on the market

Before targeting a budget, it's worth clearly identifying the variant you're after. The earliest generations, with naturally aspirated engines, offer simpler operation and generally more moderate maintenance costs, but more limited high-altitude performance. The turbocharged versions (Turbo Centurion) allow you to fly higher and faster, particularly useful for crossing mountainous terrain or unfavourable weather, but involve an engine that is more demanding to monitor and more costly to overhaul.

Some pressurised versions were also offered during part of the production run, adding extra cabin comfort for high-altitude flight at the cost of greater complexity and more involved maintenance. Clearly identifying the exact version of any aircraft for sale is therefore essential before comparing prices.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used Cessna 210 depends above all on the year of manufacture, the version (naturally aspirated, turbocharged or pressurised), total airframe and engine hours, remaining engine potential before overhaul, and the level of avionics fitted. The oldest aircraft, with naturally aspirated engines and original avionics, trade at a noticeably more accessible entry level than recent, well-equipped turbocharged versions, which represent a far larger investment.

Beyond the purchase price, your budget should include ancillary costs: an independent mechanic's pre-purchase inspection, registration and transfer fees, insurance, and possibly an avionics or cabin interior refresh if the aircraft needs one.

What to check before buying

As with any used aircraft, a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic qualified on the type is essential before finalising a transaction. For a Cessna 210, a few points deserve particular attention.

  • Complete engine history: hours elapsed since the last overhaul (SMOH), compliance with the manufacturer's maintenance program, traceability of replaced parts, particularly on turbocharged versions which place greater demands on the powerplant.
  • Full operation of the retractable gear: actuators, cables, selector and position indicators, as well as the history of any gear-related incidents.
  • Presence and application of airworthiness directives specific to the model and version, to be confirmed with the maintenance shop.
  • Condition of the structure and any signs of corrosion, especially at the wing spars and gear attachment points on the oldest examples.
  • Complete logbook and technical file, with no grey areas in the aircraft's history or any previous ownership changes.
Good to know — Never rely solely on the seller's statements about overall condition: a written independent inspection report, carried out by a mechanic familiar with the type, remains the best protection against a bad surprise after the purchase.

The retractable gear: a performance asset to watch

The retractable gear is both the Centurion's main performance asset and its most specific point of attention compared with other fixed-gear Cessna single-engines. A well-maintained mechanism generally causes no particular issue, but an incomplete maintenance history or a poorly documented gear-related incident should be treated as a serious warning sign during a viewing.

It is recommended to always ask for the details of interventions carried out on the gear system (actuators, cables, periodic functional tests) and to verify that any airworthiness directives relating to this system have indeed been applied and documented in the aircraft's technical file.

Maintenance and operating costs

The operating cost of a Cessna 210 remains broadly comparable to that of other high-performance cabin-class single-engines in its category, with an additional item linked to maintaining the retractable gear compared with a fixed-gear Cessna such as the 172 or 182. On turbocharged versions, engine monitoring also requires extra attention, with the turbocharger and its associated components representing an expense to plan for.

Having the aircraft maintained at an approved shop familiar with the type helps limit unpleasant surprises and preserve its resale value. Rigorous, well-documented maintenance records, particularly for the retractable gear and the powerplant, are a strong argument at a future resale.

Tip — Always ask for the last three maintenance invoices before making an offer: they give an accurate picture of how seriously the aircraft has been maintained, particularly the retractable gear and engine.

Resale and the used market

The Cessna 210 benefits from an active used market in Europe as well as in the United States, supported by a large installed fleet and good overall parts availability, despite production of the model having ended decades ago. A well-maintained aircraft, with a transparent history of the retractable gear and engine, as well as up-to-date avionics, generally resells more easily and on better terms than one whose technical file has grey areas.

Comparing several listings of similar models, the same version and the same equipment level, remains the best way to assess a fair price, both for buying and for preparing a future resale.
