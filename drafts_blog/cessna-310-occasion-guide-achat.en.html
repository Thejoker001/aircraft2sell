Used Cessna 310: twin-engine buying guide

A historic travel twin, still active on the market

The Cessna 310 was produced for more than twenty years and remains one of the most common light piston twins on the used market, both in Europe and the United States. Originally designed for fast travel and business use, it has appealed to several generations of owner-pilots thanks to its high cruise speed for its category and its ability to comfortably carry four to six occupants depending on the version.

On the used market, the Cessna 310 comes in many successive versions, with changes in engines, fuel capacity and avionics. This diversity of model years explains a wide price range, which makes it essential to define your budget and expectations clearly before starting your search.

Budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used Cessna 310 depends above all on the age and version of the aircraft, total airframe hours, the status of both engines (remaining potential before major overhaul) and the level of avionics equipment. The oldest examples, with reduced remaining potential on one engine or the other, trade at a markedly more accessible entry level than later, well-equipped versions with up-to-date avionics.

Aircraft age and versionLater, well-equipped versions sell for considerably more

Beyond the purchase price, your budget should factor in additional costs: a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic familiar with the type, registration and transfer fees, insurance generally higher than for an equivalent single-engine aircraft, and possibly an avionics upgrade if the aircraft needs one.

Points to check before buying

As with any used aircraft, a pre-purchase inspection carried out by an independent mechanic qualified on the type is essential before finalising a transaction. For a Cessna 310, certain points deserve particular attention given its twin-engine configuration and often advanced age.

  • Complete history of both engines: hours since the last major overhaul on each engine, compliance with the manufacturer's maintenance programme, traceability of replaced parts.
  • Condition of the metal structure: check for corrosion, particularly around the wingtip fuel tanks and areas exposed to moisture.
  • Consistency of flight hours between the two engines, which should have been operated and maintained in a balanced way.
  • Condition and updates of the avionics, as well as compliance with current regulatory requirements (transponder, traffic surveillance).
  • Complete logbook and technical file, with no grey areas in the aircraft's history or its Certificate of Airworthiness (CofA).
Good to know — Never rely solely on the seller's statements about overall condition: a written independent inspection report, including a thorough check of both engines, remains the best protection against an unpleasant surprise after purchase.

Engines, fuel and twin-engine specifics

The Cessna 310 is fitted with two piston engines whose exact specifications vary depending on the version and model year. Managing a twin requires more vigilance than a single-engine aircraft: each engine must be tracked separately, and a marked imbalance between the hours or condition of the two powerplants can signal significant costs in the short to medium term.

Fuel consumption on a twin is naturally higher than on an equivalent single-engine aircraft, a parameter to factor into the real cost per flight hour, alongside maintenance, insurance and parking.

Maintenance and operating costs

The operating cost of a Cessna 310 is structurally higher than that of a comparable single-engine aircraft, since every routine intervention, every propeller replacement and every engine overhaul is now calculated for two complete powerplants. The main cost items remain the mandatory annual inspection, insurance, parking and, over the longer term, the major overhaul of each engine once its hour potential is reached.

Having the aircraft maintained at an approved shop experienced with the type helps limit unpleasant surprises and preserve its resale value. Rigorous, well-documented maintenance tracking, for both engines, is a strong argument for a future resale.

Tip — Always request the latest maintenance invoices for both engines before making an offer: they give an accurate picture of how seriously the aircraft has been maintained.

When the Cessna 310 still makes sense

The Cessna 310 particularly suits owner-pilots looking for a fast travel twin for regular medium- and long-distance trips, with greater payload than a typical single-engine aircraft. Its engine redundancy is also a safety argument for flights over mountains, sea or areas difficult to reach in the event of a failure.

Conversely, a pilot primarily looking for minimal operating costs, or just starting out in aircraft ownership, will more naturally turn to a single-engine aircraft, since the extra maintenance cost of a twin of this age is only justified by regular use and a genuine need for engine redundancy.

Resale and the used market

The Cessna 310 benefits from an active used market, supported by a large installed base and good parts availability, particularly in the United States. A well-maintained aircraft, with a transparent history on both engines and up-to-date avionics, generally resells more easily and on better terms than one whose technical file has grey areas.

Comparing several listings of similar versions and model years remains the best way to assess a fair price, both when buying and when preparing a future resale. Price gaps between two aircraft of the same model year are almost always explained by the remaining engine potential on each engine and the quality of the avionics.
