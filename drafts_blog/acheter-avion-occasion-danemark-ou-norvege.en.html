Buying a used aircraft in Denmark or Norway: which country to choose

Market, prices, tax, registration and maintenance: a comparison between Denmark and Norway to help you decide where to look for your used aircraft.

Two Scandinavian markets, two geographic realities

Denmark and Norway are Scandinavian neighbours sharing a strong aviation culture, but their geographic realities differ considerably. Denmark, a flat and compact country, offers a territory that is easy to cover with a tightly knit airfield network. Norway, by contrast, stretches over a considerable length and has mountainous terrain that shapes light aviation very differently, with much larger distances between home bases.

For a buyer planning to operate an aircraft over long distances or in demanding terrain, Norway imposes specific operational constraints that the more uniform Danish market does not have. This geographic factor should be weighed before even comparing prices or taxation between the two countries.

Size and activity of the used market

The Danish used-aircraft market remains modest in volume but active, driven by a well-structured network of flying clubs where maintenance history is often rigorously documented. Supply is concentrated on a handful of common models, with good availability of microlights and light touring single-engines.

The Norwegian market, comparable in listing volume, shows a markedly higher share of aircraft equipped for short-field or winter operations, notably low-pressure tyres or ski-equipped types. This specialisation reflects local needs tied to terrain and climate, and is a selection criterion in its own right for a buyer planning to operate under similar conditions.

Comparing prices

Price levels remain broadly comparable between the two markets for a standard model in good maintenance condition, with no systematic gap in either direction. Aircraft specifically equipped for Norwegian conditions (skis, low-pressure tyres) can however carry a premium linked to this specialised equipment, regardless of which country they are listed for sale in.

Tax and purchase-related costs

As with any transaction in Europe, the applicable taxation depends above all on the buyer's status (private individual or professional), the country of tax residence and the country in which the aircraft will be registered after the sale. A sale between private individuals is generally not subject to VAT in either country, while a sale by a professional or a company may be subject to it under the applicable national rules. Since Norway is not an EU member state, particular attention should be paid to the customs formalities applicable to a cross-border transfer with an EU member state such as Denmark.

For a transaction between Denmark and Norway, it is worth checking the customs and VAT formalities specific to each situation, as well as the administrative fees specific to each destination country. Using a professional experienced in cross-border transactions reduces the risk of procedural errors, particularly given Norway's special status outside the European Union.

Registration: the Trafikstyrelsen versus the Luftfartstilsynet

In Denmark, aircraft registration is handled by the Trafikstyrelsen (the Danish transport authority), under the OY- prefix. In Norway, the competent authority is the Luftfartstilsynet (the Norwegian Civil Aviation Authority), which manages the register under the LN- prefix. Norway takes part in the EASA area on the same footing as EU member states, which considerably simplifies registration transfer between the two countries despite Norway's special status outside the EU.

  • Valid certificate of airworthiness and ARC — a precondition for any flight under the new registration, whichever destination country is chosen.
  • Deregistration from the register of origin — required before the new registration if the aircraft changes country.
  • Complete technical file — airframe, engine and propeller logbooks to be submitted to the new authority.
Good to know — Generally allow 2 to 8 weeks for a registration transfer between Denmark and Norway depending on how complete the file is, a timeline comparable to that observed for any transfer within the EASA area.

Maintenance network and parts availability

Denmark has a compact but well-structured maintenance network, often attached directly to flying clubs, with good parts availability for the most common models. Norway has a maintenance network spread over a considerably larger territory, with operators experienced in equipment specific to winter conditions and short-field operations, but sometimes longer lead times for certain parts due to the remoteness of some bases.

For an aircraft equipped with skis or low-pressure tyres, it is advisable to check that the chosen workshop has the experience and approval needed for this specialised equipment, more common in Norway than in Denmark. Conversely, a Danish workshop is often the simpler choice for a classic touring aircraft without any particular equipment.

Which country to favour depending on your profile

For a buyer looking for a classic touring aircraft intended for operation on standard paved or grass runways, the Danish market offers a more uniform setting and an easily accessible network of flying clubs. For a buyer planning operations on short fields, in mountainous terrain, or in demanding winter conditions, the Norwegian market deserves particular attention thanks to its higher share of aircraft already equipped and proven for this kind of use.

A pilot planning to remain based in Denmark will find a practical benefit in registering their aircraft locally with the Trafikstyrelsen, if only to simplify day-to-day administrative procedures. A pilot based in Norway, particularly in more remote regions, will often favour an aircraft already equipped for local conditions rather than a standard model that would then need adapting, which represents a significant extra cost.

Overall ownership costs should also factor in ferrying: distances in Norway can be considerably greater than in Denmark to reach a home base, adding cost and delay before an aircraft spotted far from its intended destination can first be used. This often-underestimated factor is worth quantifying before settling on one listing over another.

Good to know — In all cases, a pre-purchase inspection by a qualified independent mechanic remains essential, whether the aircraft is located in Denmark, Norway or elsewhere in Europe.

It is also worth factoring in seasonality before finalising a choice. In Denmark, light aviation activity remains fairly consistent year-round, aside from typical Northern European weather constraints. In Norway, by contrast, some regions have a markedly shorter operating season, with winter conditions that can limit the use of an aircraft not equipped accordingly for several months. This factor is worth anticipating from the very start of the search, particularly if the goal is regular year-round operation.

Ultimately, the choice between the two countries depends less on overall budget, which remains broadly comparable, than on the intended operating profile: a uniform and accessible setting in Denmark, or a more specialised market for demanding operations in Norway.
