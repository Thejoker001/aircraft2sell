Why the TBM 700 appeals to owner-pilots

The Daher/Socata TBM 700 was one of the first pressurised single-engine turboprops designed to be flown by a single owner-pilot, sitting halfway between a light piston aircraft and a business jet. With its pressurised cabin and PT6A turbine engine, it can climb to altitudes well above most piston singles, clearing turbulence and poor weather while still delivering high cruise speeds for its category.

This positioning explains its success with owner-pilots who want to step up beyond a conventional piston single without taking on the complexity and cost of a business jet. On the used market, the TBM 700 is often a pilot's first contact with turbine flying, which calls for particular preparation before buying.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used TBM 700 depends mainly on the age and generation of the aircraft, total airframe and engine hours, the engine's status relative to its overhaul program, and the level of avionics fitted. The earliest, older examples trade at a noticeably more accessible level than later, better-equipped versions, but the cost of a future engine shop visit must be factored into the decision from the outset.

Beyond the purchase price, a TBM 700 budget should include a specialist turbine pre-purchase inspection, registration and transfer fees, insurance suited to a turboprop, and, where relevant, a reserve for a future engine overhaul.

What to check before buying

The pre-purchase inspection of a TBM 700 must be carried out by a shop or mechanic qualified on turboprops and, ideally, experienced on this specific type. A few points deserve particular attention.

  • Complete history of the PT6A engine: hours elapsed, compliance with the engine manufacturer's maintenance program, traceability of major maintenance events.
  • Condition and sealing of the cabin pressurisation system, tested under real conditions during the inspection if possible.
  • Condition and updates of the avionics, in particular compliance with current navigation and traffic-monitoring equipment requirements.
  • Complete logbook and technical file, with no grey areas in ownership history or aircraft use.
  • General condition of the airframe and landing gear, bearing in mind that some examples may have seen intensive professional use.
Good to know — On a turboprop, a pre-purchase inspection cannot be limited to a visual check: it must include a thorough review of the engine maintenance program's documentation, since its upcoming milestones strongly determine the real cost of ownership over the medium term.

The PT6A turboprop and pressurisation: what to know

The PT6A turbine engine fitted to the TBM 700 is well known for its reliability, but its operation and monitoring differ considerably from those of a piston engine. Its maintenance program relies on rigorous tracking of engine parameters flight after flight and on maintenance tasks scheduled according to the engine manufacturer's recommendations, with ongoing monitoring rather than a single periodic overhaul as on a piston engine.

The pressurised cabin, which allows comfortable flight at high altitude, is itself a system that needs monitoring: door seals, control valves and cabin structure all deserve careful inspection, since a pressurisation failure can have serious consequences for flight safety.

Type training and required qualification

Moving from a piston aircraft to a turboprop such as the TBM 700 requires specific transition training, beyond simply holding an appropriate pilot licence. This training covers turbine engine management, high-altitude flight with a pressurised cabin, and type-specific procedures, and should ideally be taken at a recognised training centre experienced with the TBM.

A buyer who has never flown a turboprop should factor the cost, duration, and sometimes the insurance requirements tied to this step up into their overall financing plan, rather than discovering them after the purchase.

Maintenance and operating costs

Operating a TBM 700 involves noticeably higher costs than a piston single, driven in particular by the turbine's fuel burn, scheduled engine maintenance tasks, and insurance, which is typically higher than for an equivalent piston aircraft. These costs should be anticipated at the annual operating budget stage, not just at the point of purchase.

Having the aircraft maintained at an approved shop specialising in the type and the PT6A engine helps preserve its value and avoid unpleasant surprises at a future major inspection. A rigorous, well-documented maintenance history remains one of the most decisive arguments at resale.

Tip — Always ask for the detailed engine monitoring program and the likely date of the next major milestone: this information directly determines the real cost of ownership over the coming years.

Resale and the used market

The TBM 700 benefits from an active used market in Europe and worldwide, supported by a large installed fleet and the PT6A engine's lasting reputation. A well-maintained aircraft, with a transparent history, a complete engine file and up-to-date avionics, generally resells more easily and on better terms than one whose records leave room for doubt.

Comparing listings across different generations and model years remains the best way to assess a fair price, both for buying and for preparing a future resale. In this segment, the price gap between two comparable aircraft is almost always explained by proximity to a major engine milestone and the quality of the installed avionics.
