Buying a Used Aircraft in Germany or the Netherlands: Which Country to Choose

Market, prices, tax, registration and maintenance: a comparison between Germany and the Netherlands to help you decide where to look for your used aircraft.

Two neighbouring markets, two very different airspaces

Germany and the Netherlands are two neighbouring light aviation markets, but of very different size. Germany has one of the densest networks of airfields in Europe and a particularly strong general aviation tradition, while the Netherlands has to work with a small territory and airspace that is heavily constrained by commercial traffic around Schiphol and numerous military zones. This structural difference directly affects the size of the used-aircraft supply available in each country.

For a buyer based near the German-Dutch border, both markets remain easily accessible in parallel, which often makes it possible to widen the search without any major distance constraint.

Market size and dynamism of the used-aircraft trade

Germany has a substantial fleet of light aircraft, supported by a large number of flying clubs and a widespread practice of touring aviation. The used-aircraft supply there is abundant, particularly for the most common light single-engine types (Cessna 172, Piper PA-28, Diamond DA40), which makes it easier to compare several similar aircraft before deciding.

In the Netherlands, the used-aircraft market remains more modest in size, concentrated around airfields such as Lelystad Airport, Teuge, Hilversum or Seppe (Breda International). The number of listings available at any given time is naturally more limited than in Germany, which leads many Dutch buyers to naturally widen their search to German, Belgian and British listings, these markets being closely interconnected in terms of flying clubs and cross-border approved workshops.

Price comparison

Price levels between the two markets remain broadly comparable for the same model and the same maintenance condition, Western Europe forming a relatively homogeneous price area for light aviation. The gap observed on a given listing has more to do with local supply and demand at a given time, and with the quality of the technical file, than with any structural difference between the two countries.

In both cases, comparing several listings of similar models before committing remains the best way to assess a fair price, especially in the Netherlands where the number of comparable listings at a given time can be limited.

Seasonality also plays a role: certain times of the year see more listings published in one country than the other, depending on local selling habits (end of the flying season, club fleet renewal). Widening the search to both countries often smooths out these variations, particularly on the Dutch side where local supply is more limited.

Tax and purchase-related costs

Applicable taxation depends above all on the buyer's status (private individual or professional), their country of tax residence and the country where the aircraft will be registered after the transaction, more than on the single country where the aircraft happens to be at the time of purchase. A sale between EU-resident private individuals involving an aircraft already in free circulation within the EU generally does not trigger new VAT, whether in Germany (Umsatzsteuer) or the Netherlands (BTW). The situation is different for a sale carried out by a professional, or for an aircraft imported from a country outside the EU, which can trigger import VAT in either country.

In a cross-border transaction between Germany and the Netherlands, it is worth checking precisely the intra-EU VAT formalities that may apply between professionals, as well as the ownership transfer fees specific to each country. Using a professional experienced in cross-border transactions (broker, specialist lawyer) helps avoid procedural mistakes that can prove costly.

Registration and administrative steps

An aircraft based in Germany is registered with the Luftfahrt-Bundesamt (LBA) under the D- prefix, while an aircraft based in the Netherlands falls under the Inspectie Leefomgeving en Transport (ILT) under the PH- prefix. An aircraft bought in either country can be re-registered in the other, depending on the buyer's choice: there is no obligation to keep the registration of the country of purchase. This change involves prior deregistration from the register of origin, then a new registration with the authority of the destination country, with the full technical file to be submitted and the certificate of airworthiness checked for compliance.

  • Check the consistency between the logbook, the technical file and the actual condition of the aircraft before any registration change.
  • Allow for administrative timelines, which can vary between the German LBA and the Dutch ILT depending on how complete the file is.
  • Check any equipment requirements specific to the destination country.
  • Budget for ferry flight costs if an inspection or compliance work is needed before the transfer.
Good to know — Common EASA rules govern the transfer between member states and make it simpler than an import from outside the European Union, regardless of the direction of transfer between Germany and the Netherlands.

Maintenance network and parts availability

Germany has a particularly dense network of approved maintenance workshops, covering almost all common light aviation types. The Netherlands also offers a network of competent workshops, especially around the airfields concentrating leisure and training activity, but for rarer models or specific engines it can be worth checking in advance the availability of parts and the proximity of a specialist workshop, including in neighbouring Germany or Belgium.

The geographic proximity between the two countries strongly limits any distance constraint for routine maintenance: a Dutch owner can easily have their aircraft maintained in Germany, and vice versa, provided the chosen workshop is properly approved for the type of aircraft concerned.

The language barrier is generally less pronounced than with other European markets, as technical English is commonly used in workshops in both countries, which makes communication easier during a cross-border transaction or maintenance follow-up.

Which country to prioritise depending on your profile

For a buyer looking above all for a wide choice of similar aircraft to compare, Germany generally offers a larger volume of listings. For a buyer based in or near the Netherlands, searching locally often remains practical for visits and inspection, but widening the search to Germany frequently provides access to a larger supply and to technical files that are reputed to be rigorous.

Good to know — In all cases, a pre-purchase inspection by a qualified independent mechanic remains essential, whether the aircraft is located in Germany, the Netherlands or elsewhere in Europe.

Ultimately, the country of purchase matters less than the actual condition of the aircraft and how well it has been maintained. Comparing listings from both markets widens your options and gives you a stronger negotiating position, whether on price or on the terms of the transaction.
