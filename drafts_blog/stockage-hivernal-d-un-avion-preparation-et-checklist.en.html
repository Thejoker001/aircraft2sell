Winter aircraft storage: preparation and checklist

Engine, airframe, tires, battery, hangar choice: everything you need to do before putting an aircraft to rest for winter, and how to bring it back into service without a nasty surprise in spring.

Why preparing your aircraft for winter matters

An aircraft left standing for several weeks or months faces very different stresses from one in regular operation. Trapped moisture encourages corrosion, engine oil becomes loaded with combustion acids that attack internal surfaces while idle, tires develop permanent flat spots under prolonged static weight, and a neglected battery discharges until it sulfates beyond recovery. A poorly prepared winter lay-up often costs more to put right than a full season of normal flying.

This preparation applies just as much to the owner of a light single-engine as to a turboprop or a helicopter left idle for several months — only the durations and a few specific tasks (rotor blade de-icing, hydraulic systems) vary by aircraft type.

Before storage: oil change and engine preservation

The engine is the component most sensitive to a prolonged shutdown. An oil change right before lay-up removes the acidic combustion residues in used oil, which would otherwise keep corroding cylinder walls for the whole idle period. For storage longer than 30 days, most manufacturers and maintenance shops recommend a dedicated preservation oil, richer in corrosion inhibitors than normal running oil.

  • Full oil change with a preservation oil suited to the engine type.
  • Plugging air intakes (static port, cooling inlets, exhaust) with dedicated caps, never plain tape that leaves residue.
  • Spraying anti-corrosion compound into the cylinders for storage beyond 90 days, following the manufacturer's procedure.
  • A dated entry in the logbook noting the treatment performed and the engine hours at the time of shutdown.
Word of caution — Never run the engine "to warm it up" during lay-up without a full flight afterwards: a short run that doesn't clear out condensation moisture is worse than not running it at all.

Protecting the airframe and exterior surfaces

The airframe should be cleaned before storage, not afterwards: dust, insect residue and fuel stains trap moisture against the skin for weeks if left in place. A thorough wash followed by careful drying significantly reduces corrosion starting points.

  • Full exterior wash and drying before lay-up, especially under the wings and around control-surface hinges.
  • A breathable cover over the cockpit and glass surfaces, never an airtight plastic tarp that traps condensation.
  • Checking and treating existing corrosion spots before they progress during lay-up.
  • Covering probes, static ports and antennas with dedicated caps, removed and counted before every subsequent flight.

Tires, brakes and landing gear

A tire that carries the aircraft's weight on the same contact patch for months develops a permanent flat spot. Brake discs can also corrode in contact with the pads when there is no movement.

  • Inflating tires to the maximum recommended pressure to reduce static deformation.
  • Placing the aircraft on jack stands for long-term storage to fully unload tires and shock struts.
  • Releasing the brakes rather than leaving them set, to avoid pads bonding to the discs.
  • Greasing the landing gear pivot points before covering the aircraft.

Interior, avionics and battery

The ambient humidity of an unheated hangar or an outdoor shelter also affects the interior: mould on seats, oxidised electrical contacts, and above all a gradual battery discharge which, if not monitored, sulfates and permanently loses capacity.

  • Disconnect or remove the battery and store it in a dry place, with periodic maintenance charging (a suitable float charger).
  • Desiccant bags in the cockpit to limit residual moisture.
  • Occasional cabin ventilation during inspection visits to avoid musty odours.
  • Checking onboard fire extinguishers and expiry dates before returning to service.

Choosing the right storage location: hangar or outdoors

A closed hangar remains the safest option: it shelters from the weather, limits temperature swings and reduces corrosion risk. Prolonged outdoor storage, even under a quality cover, exposes the aircraft more to frost, snow and the humidity-drying cycles that encourage corrosion.

Whichever option you choose, a regular check visit — at least monthly — remains essential to catch any issue (leak, animal nest, cover blown off by wind) before it worsens.

Spring return-to-service checklist

Returning an aircraft to service after winter lay-up should never be a simple cover removal followed by a take-off. It follows a precise protocol, often documented in the aircraft's maintenance manual.

  • A full visual inspection of the airframe, checking for corrosion, moisture traces or insect/rodent nests.
  • Removal of every protective cap and cover, counted off a list so none is left behind.
  • Checking the recharged battery, fluid levels and tire pressure.
  • A ground engine run before any flight, monitoring parameters (oil pressure and temperature) and checking for leaks.
  • A local check flight before resuming normal activity, especially after a lay-up of several months.
  • Updating the logbook with the return-to-service date and the findings.
Tip — Also check the validity of the certificate of airworthiness (CofA) and its airworthiness review before the first flight: a long lay-up sometimes spans an expiry date that went unnoticed.

Cost and maintenance frequency during storage

The initial preparation (oil change, engine treatment, washing, covers) accounts for most of the cost, but it must be followed by regular monitoring throughout the storage period: battery top-up charging, a monthly visual check, and for multi-month storage, an interim check of the preservation oil's condition. This follow-up remains far cheaper than an early major overhaul made necessary by internal corrosion discovered too late.

If you plan to sell the aircraft once winter is over, keep every invoice and logbook entry related to this lay-up: a complete, well-documented maintenance file is a strong trust signal for a potential buyer.
