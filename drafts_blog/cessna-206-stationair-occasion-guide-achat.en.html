Used Cessna 206 Stationair: buying guide

A utility single built for versatility

The Cessna 206 Stationair is a fixed-gear single-engine aircraft produced since the 1960s, designed from the outset to carry people and bulky loads rather than to prioritise cruise performance. With six seats, a large cargo hold accessible through wide cargo doors, and a rugged airframe, it has long occupied a particular niche: the aircraft "that works", favoured by skydiving operations, bush operators, floatplane operators, and by private owners looking for a family aircraft able to carry heavy loads out of rough strips.

On the used market, this versatility translates into a wide variety of individual aircraft: some have had an exclusively private, well-cared-for life, while others have been flown intensively in commercial use or under demanding conditions (unpaved strips, floats, skydiving operations). Understanding a specific aircraft's operational history is therefore one of the first steps before any viewing.

Variants and derivatives worth knowing

The 206 has gone through several developments over its production run, with direct consequences for price and the uses an aircraft can be put to.

  • Normally aspirated and turbocharged (Turbo Stationair) versions, the latter offering better high-altitude performance but a more heavily stressed engine and generally higher maintenance costs.
  • Cargo configuration with a large side door, highly sought after for skydiving operations, freight carrying or utility missions.
  • Floatplane versions, popular in Scandinavia and other lake-rich regions, which require a specific rating and extra vigilance regarding corrosion.
  • Turbine conversions (such as Soloy or Supervan on related airframes), less common in Europe but present on the market, which radically change the aircraft's maintenance profile and value.

Before comparing listings, it is therefore essential to identify the exact variant, the STC modifications installed (cargo door, floats, STOL kit, avionics) and their associated documentation, as two "Cessna 206" aircraft can have very different use profiles and values.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used 206 varies significantly depending on the year of manufacture, airframe and engine hours, the presence of STC modifications (cargo door, floats, turbocharger, STOL kit) and the quality of the avionics. The oldest and least-equipped examples trade at a relatively accessible entry level for a six-seater, while recent, turbocharged examples fitted with floats or up-to-date digital avionics can represent a substantially larger investment.

Beyond the purchase price, budget for a thorough pre-purchase inspection, registration and transfer fees, insurance — potentially higher for floatplane or skydiving use — and a possible avionics or interior refresh.

What to check before buying

Beyond the standard checks applicable to any used aircraft, a few points deserve particular attention on a 206, given its frequent use in demanding conditions.

  • Airframe corrosion, especially on aircraft that have operated on floats or in humid/salty environments: inspect the floor, spars and gear attachment points carefully.
  • Condition of the hinges and structure around the large cargo door, a significant stress area on utility or skydiving aircraft.
  • Complete engine history: hours elapsed since the last overhaul (SMOH), compliance with the manufacturer's maintenance program, traceability of replaced parts.
  • Compliance and documentation of installed STC modifications (floats, STOL kit, cargo door, turbocharger): each modification should be traceable in the technical file.
  • Condition of the cargo floor and tie-down points, often marked by frequent loading and unloading of equipment.
  • Complete logbook and technical file, with no grey areas in the aircraft's history, particularly if it has seen intensive commercial use abroad.
Good to know — A pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic familiar with the type, including a specific check for corrosion and STC modifications, remains the best protection against a bad surprise after the purchase.

Regulations and specific uses

Some uses of the 206 involve particular regulatory requirements that should be anticipated before buying. A floatplane version requires a specific seaplane rating for the pilot and extra attention to the airworthiness of the float installation. An aircraft configured for skydiving operations may have undergone specific modifications and maintenance tracking, which should be verified against the registry authority and the technical file. More generally, any STC modification should be properly documented and linked to the aircraft's Certificate of Airworthiness (CofA); if in doubt about the compliance of an installation, further verification with an approved maintenance shop is recommended before committing.

Maintenance and operating costs

The operating cost of a 206 remains broadly comparable to other high-capacity piston singles, with a few specific items tied to its utility use: additional wear on the structure under frequent loading, extra maintenance for floats or the cargo door, and higher fuel consumption on turbocharged versions.

Having the aircraft maintained at an approved shop familiar with the type helps limit unpleasant surprises and preserve its resale value, particularly where STC documentation needs to remain impeccable. Rigorous maintenance tracking is a strong argument at a future resale, especially for an aircraft that has seen commercial use.

Tip — Always ask for recent maintenance invoices and the complete documentation of any STC modifications before making an offer: they give an accurate picture of how well the aircraft has been maintained and of its compliance.

Resale and the used market

The 206 benefits from an active used market in Europe and internationally, driven by steady demand from utility operators, skydiving schools and floatplane enthusiasts, in addition to private buyers. A well-maintained aircraft, with a transparent technical file, well-documented STC modifications and no corrosion, generally resells more easily and on better terms than one whose history has grey areas.

Comparing several listings of comparable configurations (normally aspirated or turbo, presence of floats or a cargo door, hours and remaining engine potential) remains the best way to assess a fair price, both for buying and for preparing a future resale.
