Two cousins from the Beechcraft Musketeer family

The Beechcraft Sundowner (model C23) and the Beechcraft Sierra (model C24R, later A24R) are both descendants of the Musketeer family, launched by Beechcraft in the 1960s and produced in several variants through the early 1980s. Both are four-seat, low-wing, all-metal aircraft designed for very similar missions: advanced flight training, short and medium family trips, and regular club or private ownership use.

The two models share the same basic airframe and the same flying qualities, known for being stable and forgiving, which has made them popular with pilots building experience as well as owners who want a straightforward four-seater that's easy to maintain. Their main point of difference lies in the landing gear and the powerplant, which noticeably changes the usage profile, the maintenance budget and the used price.

On the European market, the Sundowner and Sierra fleet is much smaller than that of contemporary Cessna 172s or Piper PA-28s. That relative scarcity means a more limited choice of listings and sometimes a longer search, but it also tends to translate into more attractive used prices for a comparable level of equipment.

Sierra or Sundowner: what are the technical differences

The Sundowner is fitted with fixed landing gear and a Lycoming engine driving a fixed-pitch propeller, making it the mechanically simplest option in the lineup. That simplicity translates into more predictable routine maintenance and generally lower operating costs, at the expense of a more modest cruise speed.

The Sierra, by contrast, builds on a very similar airframe but adds retractable landing gear and a more powerful engine paired with a constant-speed propeller. That added mechanical complexity delivers a faster cruise speed and better overall performance, but also demands more exacting maintenance and higher ownership costs, particularly tied to upkeep of the retractable gear.

The choice between the two comes down mainly to intended use: a Sundowner suits regular local flying or a flight school looking for simplicity very well, while a Sierra will appeal more to an owner who wants to travel farther and faster, accepting slightly more technical maintenance in return.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used Sundowner or Sierra depends above all on the age of the aircraft, accumulated airframe and engine hours, remaining potential before the next major overhaul, the overall condition of the aluminum airframe, and the level of avionics fitted. For a Sierra, the documented condition of the retractable gear and the constant-speed propeller also weighs heavily on resale value.

Beyond the purchase price, your budget should include ancillary costs: an independent mechanic's pre-purchase inspection, registration and transfer fees, insurance, and possibly an avionics refresh if the aircraft needs one. The limited fleet of these models in Europe may also mean allowing extra time to source certain spare parts.

What to check before buying

As with any used aircraft, a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic qualified on the type is essential before finalising a transaction. For a Sundowner or Sierra, a few points deserve particular attention.

  • Condition of the aluminum airframe: look for any signs of corrosion, particularly in areas where moisture can accumulate and around the main spars.
  • Complete engine history: hours elapsed since the last major overhaul (SMOH), compliance with the manufacturer's maintenance program, traceability of replaced parts.
  • Complete logbook and technical file, with no grey areas in the aircraft's history and no chronological gaps.
  • Condition and updates of the avionics, as well as compliance with current regulatory requirements.
  • Validity of the certificate of airworthiness (CofA) and associated documents, to confirm with the seller before any negotiation.
Good to know — Never rely solely on the seller's statements about overall condition: a written independent inspection report remains the best protection against a bad surprise after purchase, especially on a model with a limited fleet where spare parts can take longer to source.

The Sierra's retractable gear: a specific point to watch

The Sierra's retractable landing gear is the feature that most clearly sets this model apart from the Sundowner, and it's also the point that deserves the most rigorous check before buying. Have several full gear retraction and extension cycles carried out in the presence of a qualified mechanic, to confirm there are no hydraulic leaks, that the safety switches work correctly, and that the tires and gear legs are in good condition.

As with any retractable mechanism on an aircraft with a limited fleet, the availability of certain parts specific to the gear and the constant-speed propeller is worth checking ahead of purchase, ideally with a shop approved and experienced on the type. Well-documented maintenance of this system is a strong argument for reassuring a future buyer at resale.

Maintenance and operating costs

The operating cost of a Sundowner remains comparable to other simple four-seaters in its category, with predictable routine maintenance thanks to the absence of retractable gear and a constant-speed propeller. The Sierra, being mechanically more complex, generally involves a somewhat higher maintenance budget, mainly tied to periodic upkeep of the gear and propeller.

For both models, finding a mechanic experienced on the type can be harder in Europe than for more common models like the Cessna 172 or Piper PA-28, which is worth planning for ahead of purchase by identifying an approved shop near your future base.

Tip — Always ask for the last three maintenance invoices before making an offer: they give an accurate picture of how seriously the aircraft has been maintained and of real parts availability for this type.

Resale and the used market

The used market for the Sundowner and Sierra remains a niche market in Europe, supported by a base of enthusiasts and clubs who appreciate the robustness and forgiving handling of these four-seaters. A well-maintained aircraft, with a transparent logbook and a corrosion-free airframe, generally resells more easily and on better terms than one whose technical file has grey areas.

Comparing several listings of similar models remains the best way to assess a fair price, both for buying and for preparing a future resale. Price differences between two aircraft of the same model year are almost always explained by remaining engine hours, airframe condition and, for a Sierra, the documented quality of retractable-gear maintenance.
