Used Daher TBM 930: Buying Guide

Why the TBM 930 appeals to owner-pilots

The Daher TBM 930 is a pressurised single-engine turboprop designed for owner-pilots who want to step up in speed and altitude without the added complexity of a twin-engine aircraft or a business jet. Introduced by Daher as an evolution of the TBM family, the 930 stands out above all for its touchscreen Garmin G3000 avionics suite and its autothrottle, a first for this type of aircraft at the time, which meaningfully reduces workload during cruise and approach.

On the used market, the TBM 930 targets a buyer already experienced on turboprops or transitioning from a high-end piston single, drawn by the high cruise speed, the pressurised cabin and the ability to operate from relatively short runways. This versatility makes it a sought-after aircraft for both private use and certain light business missions.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used TBM 930 depends above all on the year of build, total airframe and engine hours, engine status (remaining potential before a major overhaul) and the level of avionics and cabin options fitted. A recent aircraft with substantial remaining engine potential trades at a noticeably higher price than an older example close to its overhaul deadline, for which the buyer will need to factor the cost of that intervention into the negotiation.

Beyond the purchase price, your budget should include ancillary costs: a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic qualified on the type, registration and transfer fees, insurance (often higher than for a piston single due to the aircraft's speed and power), and additional training for the pilot if not already type-qualified on the TBM.

The PT6A engine: engine hours and SMOH

The TBM 930 is powered by a Pratt & Whitney Canada PT6A-series turboprop engine, a widely used powerplant well regarded for its robustness across general and regional aviation. On a listing, the engine hours quoted usually refer to total hours since new, while SMOH (hours elapsed since the last major overhaul) indicates the remaining potential before the next major intervention set out by the engine manufacturer's maintenance program.

The cost of a major overhaul on this type of turboprop is a significant expense, which is why a low SMOH is an important value driver at purchase. It is advisable to always request the detail of the engine maintenance program followed (periodic inspections, replacement of life-limited components) rather than relying solely on the total hours figure shown in the listing.

Tip — Always have the exact engine status confirmed by a shop approved on the PT6A before making an offer: the hourly tracking program and intermediate inspections vary depending on the aircraft's operating history.

What to check before buying

As with any used turboprop, a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic qualified on the type is essential before finalising a transaction. For a TBM 930, a few points deserve particular attention.

  • Complete PT6A engine history: total hours, SMOH, compliance with the manufacturer's maintenance program and traceability of replaced life-limited components.
  • Operation and sealing of the cabin pressurisation system, often a costly item in the event of a fault.
  • Condition and updates of the Garmin G3000 avionics suite and autothrottle, as well as compliance with current regulatory requirements (transponder, traffic-monitoring systems).
  • Condition of the landing gear, brakes and tyres, which are put under greater stress by the aircraft's high performance.
  • Complete logbook and technical file, with no grey areas in the aircraft's operating history.
Good to know — Never rely solely on the seller's statements about a turboprop's overall condition: a written independent inspection report from a shop familiar with the type remains the best protection against a bad surprise after the purchase.

Certificate of airworthiness, logbook and registration

Before signing anything, check the validity of the certificate of airworthiness (CofA) and its associated airworthiness review certificate, which determine the first flight under the new owner's name. The logbook should record every intervention on the airframe, engine and avionics since the aircraft entered service, with no chronological gaps.

For an aircraft imported from another register, deregistration in the country of origin is required before the new registration, as with any registration transfer in Europe. Common EASA rules govern this transfer between member states and make it simpler than an import from outside the European Union.

Maintenance and operating costs

The operating cost of a TBM 930 is notably higher than that of a piston single, due to jet fuel consumption, the hourly cost of turbine maintenance and higher insurance premiums linked to the aircraft's speed and power. The main expense items remain the mandatory annual inspection, insurance, parking at a suitable airfield and, in the longer term, the engine major overhaul once the hourly potential is reached.

Having the aircraft maintained at an approved shop familiar with the TBM family helps limit unpleasant surprises and preserve its resale value. Rigorous, well-documented maintenance records, with a thorough logbook, are a strong argument at a future resale.

Tip — Always ask for the last three maintenance invoices and the detail of intermediate engine inspections before making an offer: they give an accurate picture of how seriously the aircraft has been maintained.

Resale and the used market

The TBM 930 benefits from an active used market in Europe, supported by the TBM family's reputation for reliability and good availability of PT6A parts. A well-maintained aircraft, with a transparent logbook, low SMOH and up-to-date avionics, generally resells more easily and on better terms than one whose technical file has grey areas.

Comparing several listings of similar TBM family aircraft remains the best way to assess a fair price, both for buying and for preparing a future resale. Price differences between two aircraft of the same model year are almost always explained by remaining engine potential and the quality of the avionics fitted.
