Used Beechcraft Bonanza 36: buying guide (six seats)

Why choose a Bonanza 36 over a V-tail

The Beechcraft Bonanza 36, often called the Model 36 or A36 depending on the generation, is the conventional-tail, stretched-fuselage version of the famous Bonanza family. Unlike the V-tail (Model 33/35), the 36 offers a lengthened cabin seating up to six, with a rear cargo door that makes loading passengers or bulky baggage much easier. This configuration has made it a highly sought-after used aircraft for owner-pilot families and professionals who need more useful load than a standard four-seater provides.

In the light aviation market, both in Europe and North America, the Bonanza 36 occupies a distinctive niche: it combines the ruggedness and performance reputation of the Bonanza line with cabin versatility close to that of a Cessna 206 or a Piper Cherokee Six, while retaining retractable gear and a better aerodynamic efficiency than either of those two models.

The main variants of the Model 36

The Model 36 has gone through several evolutions since its launch in the late 1960s. The earliest versions carry a more modest engine, while the later A36 and eventually the turbocharged B36TC offer better high-altitude performance and increased useful load. It's essential to identify the exact variant before comparing listings, because the price and operating-cost gap between an early non-turbo model and a recent B36TC can be very significant.

Original Model 36Six-seat cabin, non-turbocharged engine, analog cockpit

Beyond the generation, it's also worth checking whether the aircraft has received a modernised avionics suite (digital cockpit) compared with its original configuration, which heavily influences its value and day-to-day comfort.

What budget to plan for at purchase

The price of a used Bonanza 36 depends above all on the age of the aircraft, accumulated airframe and engine hours, engine status (remaining potential before the major overhaul) and the level of avionics fitted. Older examples with an engine close to its limit trade at a noticeably more accessible entry level than well-equipped recent B36TCs, which represent a far larger investment.

Beyond the purchase price, your budget should include ancillary costs: a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic qualified on the type, registration and transfer fees, insurance suited to a six-seat cabin, and possibly an avionics or interior refresh if the aircraft needs one.

What to check before buying

As with any used aircraft, a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic familiar with the type is essential before finalising a transaction. For a Bonanza 36, a few points deserve particular attention.

  • Corrosion and overall structural condition, especially on examples that spent time in humid or coastal environments.
  • Retractable landing gear operation: actuators, wiring, the manual backup system, and the history of work done on this costly item.
  • Complete engine history: hours elapsed since the last major overhaul (SMOH), compliance with the manufacturer's maintenance program, traceability of replaced parts.
  • Condition and updates of the avionics, as well as compliance with current regulatory requirements (transponder, traffic-monitoring systems).
  • Complete logbook and technical file, with no grey areas in the aircraft's history, and a valid certificate of airworthiness (CofA).
Good to know — Never rely solely on the seller's statements about overall condition: a written independent inspection report remains the best protection against a bad surprise after the purchase.

Engine, SMOH and major overhaul

Depending on the version, the Bonanza 36 is fitted with high-displacement flat engines whose remaining potential before the major overhaul is one of the most decisive factors in resale price. SMOH, meaning the hours elapsed since the last major overhaul, should be compared against the total potential published by the engine manufacturer to estimate the hours remaining before the next major intervention.

An engine close to its potential limit means budgeting for a future major overhaul, which represents a significant expense. Conversely, a recently overhauled engine with low SMOH and good traceability of the work performed is a meaningful value argument both for the buyer and for a future resale.

Maintenance and operating costs

The operating cost of a Bonanza 36 is comparable to that of a high-performance, retractable-gear single-engine aircraft in its category, with the main expense items being the mandatory annual inspection, insurance, parking and, in the longer term, the engine's major overhaul once its hourly potential is reached. The retractable gear adds a specific maintenance item compared with a fixed-gear aircraft.

Having the aircraft maintained at an approved shop familiar with the type helps limit unpleasant surprises and preserve its resale value. Rigorous, well-documented maintenance records in the logbook are a strong argument at a future resale.

Tip — Always ask for the last three maintenance invoices before making an offer: they give an accurate picture of how seriously the aircraft has been maintained.

Typical use: six-seat family travel

The main advantage of the Bonanza 36 over the rest of the Bonanza family is its load-carrying capacity. With six seats and a rear door that eases loading, it particularly suits owner-pilot families travelling with bulky baggage, professionals carrying equipment, or owners who regularly want to take several passengers over medium to long distances.

Keep in mind, however, that filling all six seats and the baggage compartment reduces available fuel range: always check the weight-and-balance sheet of the specific aircraft to make sure it matches your intended use.

Resale and the used market

The Bonanza 36 benefits from an active used market, supported by a large installed fleet and good parts availability, particularly in the United States where the model is very common. A well-maintained aircraft, with a transparent logbook and up-to-date avionics, generally resells more easily and on better terms than one whose technical file has grey areas.

Comparing several listings of similar models remains the best way to assess a fair price, both for buying and for preparing a future resale. Price differences between two aircraft of the same model year are almost always explained by remaining engine potential (SMOH) and the quality of the avionics fitted.
