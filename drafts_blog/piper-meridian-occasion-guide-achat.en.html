Used Piper Meridian: Buying Guide

Why owner-pilots are drawn to the Meridian

The Piper Meridian, designated PA-46-500TP by the manufacturer, is the turboprop version of the PA-46 family that Piper launched to give owner-pilots the performance of a single-engine turbine aircraft without the burdens of an airline-style crew. Powered by a Pratt & Whitney Canada PT6A turbine, it combines a pressurised cabin, solid cruise speed and a range well suited to medium-distance business or family travel across Europe.

On the used market, the Meridian attracts a specific type of buyer: a pilot already experienced on an advanced piston single (such as a Piper Malibu Mirage or Cirrus SR22) looking to step up to turbine power without moving straight to a business jet. This transition involves an acquisition and operating budget considerably higher than that of a piston single, which must be anticipated precisely before getting started.

What budget to plan for

The price of a used Meridian depends mainly on the production year, total airframe and turbine hours, the engine's status relative to its scheduled overhaul interval, and the level of avionics fitted (original analogue cockpit or glass-cockpit retrofit). The oldest examples, with reduced remaining turbine potential, trade at a notably more accessible entry level than recent, well-equipped versions.

Beyond the purchase price, factor in additional costs: a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic qualified on turboprops, registration and transfer fees, insurance (notably higher than for a piston single), and, where needed, an avionics upgrade.

The PT6A turbine: the key inspection point

The Pratt & Whitney Canada PT6A turbine fitted to the Meridian is known for its ruggedness, but its documented history is the central element of any transaction on this type of aircraft. The engine's status relative to its scheduled overhaul interval, strict adherence to the maintenance programme defined by the engine manufacturer, and complete traceability of life-limited parts must be checked with the utmost rigour.

  • Hours and cycles elapsed since the last major turbine intervention, compared against the manufacturer's scheduled interval.
  • Complete turbine history: engine logbook, service bulletins applied, traceability of life-limited parts.
  • Whether an hourly engine maintenance programme with the engine manufacturer is in place, which secures the cost of a future major intervention.
Good to know — An hourly engine maintenance programme, when transferable to the new owner, is a valuable point to check before making an offer: it greatly limits the financial risk of an unplanned major intervention.

Other points to check before buying

Beyond the turbine, a pre-purchase inspection carried out by an independent mechanic qualified on the type is essential before finalising any transaction. On a Meridian, certain structural items and systems deserve particular attention.

  • Cabin pressurisation system sealing and operation, often a significant expense item if a leak is left untreated.
  • Landing gear condition and hydraulic actuators, stressed on every flight cycle.
  • Operation and currency of the fitted avionics, as well as compliance with current regulatory requirements.
  • Complete flight log and technical file, with no gaps in the aircraft's history.
Good to know — Never rely solely on the seller's statements about overall condition: a written independent inspection report remains the best protection against unpleasant surprises after purchase.

Pressurised cabin: a benefit and an operating constraint

The Meridian's pressurised cabin allows cruising at higher altitudes, above most turbulence and piston-powered traffic, improving passenger comfort on longer trips. In return, this feature requires specific maintenance of the pressurisation system and its seals, and calls for extra attention during the pre-purchase inspection regarding airframe sealing.

Before buying, it is worth asking the seller for the history of work done on the pressurisation system, to anticipate this expense item in the financing plan if an intervention is approaching.

Maintenance and operating costs

Operating costs for a Meridian are considerably higher than for a piston single, due to the turbine's fuel burn, the hourly maintenance rate, and the higher insurance premiums associated with turboprops. The main expense items remain the mandatory annual inspection, insurance, parking, and, over the longer term, the major turbine intervention once the remaining hours are consumed.

Having the aircraft maintained at an approved shop familiar with the type helps avoid unpleasant surprises and preserve resale value. Rigorous, well-documented maintenance tracking is a strong argument when the time comes to sell.

Tip — Always ask for the last three maintenance invoices and the latest turbine inspection report before making an offer: they give an accurate picture of how seriously the aircraft has been maintained.

Type training and qualification

Moving from a piston single to a turboprop like the Meridian requires specific transition training, covering turbine management, cabin pressurisation and the procedures associated with higher speeds and altitudes. Insurers generally require proof of this training, delivered by organisations specialised on the type, before covering a new owner-pilot.

It is advisable to factor the cost and duration of this training into the turbine-transition plan ahead of the purchase, so as to be operational as soon as the aircraft is taken into possession.

Resale and the used market

The Meridian benefits from an active but narrower used market than the most common piston singles, with an installed base concentrated in a handful of major European markets. A well-maintained aircraft, with a transparent turbine history and a transferable engine maintenance programme, generally resells more easily and on better terms than one with gaps in its technical file.

Comparing several listings of similar model years remains the best way to assess a fair price, whether buying now or preparing a future resale. Price differences between two aircraft of the same model year almost always come down to turbine status and the quality of the avionics fitted.
