Best aircraft for a pilot who mostly flies solo

You don't need four seats or a twin when you almost always fly without a passenger. An overview of aircraft families suited to a mostly solo mission, by budget and purpose.

Why the "solo pilot" profile changes the choice of aircraft

A pilot who flies alone most of the time, with the occasional passenger, doesn't face the same constraints as someone who routinely carries family or colleagues. Payload capacity, often the first criterion highlighted in listings, matters less than other priorities: cost per flight hour, ease of maintenance, flying enjoyment and resale ease.

This profile opens the door to categories of aircraft that a "family" purchase often rules out from the start: microlights, light two-seaters, or older singles with simplified upkeep. The budget saved on unused seats can be reinvested in performance, engine quality, or how often you actually get to fly.

Microlights and very light aircraft: deliberate minimalism

For a pilot who flies exclusively or almost exclusively solo, a microlight or a very light two-seat aircraft is often the most economical option, both to buy and to maintain. Reduced fuel burn, lighter fixed costs and taxes depending on the category and country, a simple structure: this segment lets you fly a lot on a contained annual budget.

The trade-off is more limited versatility: modest cruise speed, restricted baggage capacity, and in some models, greater sensitivity to wind and weather. For a pilot who values flying often on short or medium trips over touring performance, this trade-off is usually very favourable. Our guide on microlight vs. light aircraft for beginners covers the decision criteria for this category in more detail.

Classic two or four-seat singles

A classic four-seat single, even if used in practice solo most of the time, remains a sensible choice for a solo pilot who wants to keep some flexibility: taking a passenger occasionally, carrying gear, or simply benefiting from a wider secondhand market and better resale liquidity.

Models like the Cessna 172 remain a benchmark for this profile: ruggedness, spare parts available throughout Europe, and maintenance costs well documented by a large owner community. The downside is a higher cost per flight hour than a microlight, for a use that in practice doesn't always make use of the rear seats.

  • A secondhand four-seat single still makes sense if you intend to occasionally bring a passenger or bulky luggage.
  • The resale market is generally more liquid for widespread models, even for a mostly solo use.
  • Fixed costs (insurance, hangar, taxes) are often similar whether you fly solo or with three others, which weighs more heavily on a solo pilot's budget.

Fast aircraft for solo touring

For a solo pilot who favours long trips over local circuits, a faster single with more capable avionics can be a better investment than a classic trainer, even accepting a higher purchase and maintenance budget. Cruise speed and range matter more here than payload capacity.

A composite aircraft like the Diamond DA40 or a high-performance single like the Cirrus SR22 lets you cover long distances in shorter flight times, with a level of comfort and modern avionics that's genuinely appreciated solo on long trips. See our Diamond DA40 vs DA42 comparison for the distinction between single and twin on this kind of mission.

Good to know — For exclusively solo use, the extra cost of a twin over a fast, well-equipped single is rarely justified, unless a specific route requirement applies (repeated flights over water or mountains).

Budget comparison by profile

Annual budget varies significantly by aircraft category, far more than by the number of seats actually used. The table below gives a relative order of magnitude across the three profiles described above.

Fast / touring singleHigherHigherSpeed, range, comfort solo on long trips

Beyond these broad figures, the real cost always depends on the aircraft's age, remaining engine potential (engine hours, SMOH) and how often you intend to fly over the year.

Buying used for solo use: points to watch

A mostly solo use doesn't reduce the checks needed before a secondhand purchase. The logbook, engine history and certificate of airworthiness (CofA) must be reviewed with the same rigour as for any other buyer profile — see our complete guide on pre-purchase aircraft inspection.

  • Check engine hours and the time remaining before the next scheduled major overhaul.
  • For a microlight, check the maintenance intervals specific to the category and parts availability for the model.
  • Don't buy an oversized aircraft "just in case" if your real use will stay solo 90% of the time: the extra operating cost is permanent, not occasional.

Which aircraft to choose for your mission

For a pilot who flies alone most of the time, the best approach is to start from the real mission rather than the number of available seats. Local leisure flying with regular use in mind: a microlight or a light two-seater. Occasional flexibility and a wide resale market: a classic four-seat single. Priority on long solo trips with a good level of comfort: a fast, well-equipped single.

Another factor worth considering is how often you can realistically fly over a year. A solo pilot who only flies occasionally often gets more out of a simple aircraft that's cheap to keep on the ground (taxes, hangar, insurance) and easy to bring back into service after a period of inactivity, rather than a more capable aircraft that demands more regular upkeep. Conversely, a solo pilot who flies very frequently more easily absorbs the fixed cost of a more sophisticated aircraft, since that cost is spread over a larger number of annual flight hours.

Shared ownership is also worth mentioning for this profile: sharing an aircraft with one or two other solo pilots can meaningfully cut fixed costs while still giving regular access to a better-equipped aircraft than an individual budget alone would allow. This option suits occasional or irregular use particularly well, provided the usage schedule and maintenance cost-sharing are clearly organised.

Finally, don't underestimate how much the choice of aircraft affects day-to-day flying enjoyment. A solo pilot often values flying feel and simplicity more than a "family" buyer who prioritises passenger comfort. A nimble, responsive and economical aircraft that you fly often tends to deliver more satisfaction over time than a more expensive aircraft that's heavier to manage administratively, with rear seats that stay empty most of the time.

In all cases, comparing several listings of similar models remains the best way to assess a fair price and spot a well-maintained aircraft suited to your real use rather than a theoretical one that's rarely exploited.
