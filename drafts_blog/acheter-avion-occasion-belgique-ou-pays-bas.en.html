Buying a Used Aircraft in Belgium or the Netherlands: Which Country to Choose

Market, prices, VAT, registration and maintenance: a comparison between Belgium and the Netherlands to help you decide where to look for your used aircraft.

Two small countries, two closely linked markets

Belgium and the Netherlands are two neighbouring markets of modest size by European standards, sharing a common border and fairly similar geography: a small territory, airspace heavily occupied by commercial traffic (Brussels National and Schiphol in particular) and numerous military zones to work around. In both countries, light aviation operates within constrained airspace, which means pilots and buyers need a good grasp of local procedures before getting started.

For a buyer based in either country, the other market remains within easy reach: the distance between the main Belgian and Dutch airfields is generally less than an hour's drive, which makes it easy to widen the search across the border.

Market size and dynamism of the used-aircraft trade

The Belgian used-aircraft market is supported by a network of active airfields such as Antwerp (Deurne), Grimbergen, Saint-Hubert or Kortrijk-Wevelgem, with supply concentrated on classic light single-engine types (Cessna 172, Piper PA-28, Robin DR400) alongside a well-established microlight and glider scene. The used-aircraft supply nonetheless remains limited in volume, the country being notably smaller than its German or French neighbours.

In the Netherlands, the market is of comparable size, concentrated around airfields such as Lelystad Airport, Teuge, Hilversum or Seppe (Breda International). The number of listings available at any given time is similarly limited, which leads many Belgian and Dutch buyers to naturally widen their search across the border, as well as towards Germany and France, these markets being closely interconnected in terms of flying clubs and cross-border approved workshops.

Price comparison

Price levels between the two markets remain broadly comparable for the same model and the same maintenance condition, Western Europe forming a relatively homogeneous price area for light aviation. The gap observed on a given listing has more to do with local supply and demand at a given time, and with the quality of the technical file, than with any structural difference between the two countries.

In both cases, since the available supply at any given time is limited, comparing several listings of similar models before committing remains the best way to assess a fair price. Widening the search to both countries, or even to the neighbouring German and French markets, often gives access to a wider choice without any significant distance constraint.

Seasonality also plays a role: certain times of the year see more listings published in one country than the other, depending on local selling habits (end of the flying season, club fleet renewal). Following both markets in parallel often helps smooth out these variations.

Tax and purchase-related costs

Applicable taxation depends above all on the buyer's status (private individual or professional), their country of tax residence and the country where the aircraft will be registered after the transaction, more than on the single country where the aircraft happens to be at the time of purchase. A sale between EU-resident private individuals involving an aircraft already in free circulation within the EU generally does not trigger new VAT, whether in Belgium or the Netherlands (BTW). The situation is different for a sale carried out by a professional, or for an aircraft imported from a country outside the EU, which can trigger import VAT in either country.

In a cross-border transaction between Belgium and the Netherlands, it is worth checking precisely the intra-EU VAT formalities that may apply between professionals, as well as the ownership transfer fees specific to each country. Using a professional experienced in cross-border transactions (broker, specialist lawyer) helps avoid procedural mistakes that can prove costly.

Registration and administrative steps

An aircraft based in Belgium is registered with the Directorate-General for Air Transport (DGTA/BCAA) under the OO- prefix, while an aircraft based in the Netherlands falls under the Inspectie Leefomgeving en Transport (ILT) under the PH- prefix. An aircraft bought in either country can be re-registered in the other, depending on the buyer's choice: there is no obligation to keep the registration of the country of purchase. This change involves prior deregistration from the register of origin, then a new registration with the authority of the destination country, with the full technical file to be submitted and the certificate of airworthiness checked for compliance.

  • Check the consistency between the logbook, the technical file and the actual condition of the aircraft before any registration change.
  • Allow for administrative timelines, which can vary between the Belgian DGTA/BCAA and the Dutch ILT depending on how complete the file is.
  • Check any equipment requirements specific to the destination country.
  • Budget for ferry flight costs if an inspection or compliance work is needed before the transfer.
Good to know — Common EASA rules govern the transfer between member states and make it simpler than an import from outside the European Union, regardless of the direction of transfer between Belgium and the Netherlands.

Maintenance network and parts availability

Belgium has a dense network of approved maintenance workshops for a country of its size, covering most common light aviation and microlight types. The Netherlands also offers a network of competent workshops, especially around the airfields concentrating leisure and training activity, but for rarer models or specific engines it can be worth checking in advance the availability of parts and the proximity of a specialist workshop, including in neighbouring Germany, France or Belgium.

The geographic proximity between the two countries strongly limits any distance constraint for routine maintenance: a Dutch owner can easily have their aircraft maintained in Belgium, and vice versa, provided the chosen workshop is properly approved for the type of aircraft concerned.

The language barrier is generally minor between the two countries: Dutch is spoken across a large part of Belgium (Flanders), which makes direct communication with Dutch workshops and clubs easier, while technical English remains commonly used across workshops in both countries.

Which country to prioritise depending on your profile

For a French-speaking buyer based in Belgium, the search often naturally covers Wallonia and Flanders, with a complementary look at the Netherlands to widen the choice on a specific model. For a Dutch-speaking buyer or one based in the Netherlands, Flemish Belgian listings remain just as accessible, language generally not being an obstacle. In both cases, widening the search to the neighbouring country often doubles the number of comparable listings available at a given time, each market taken on its own being modest in size.

Good to know — In all cases, a pre-purchase inspection by a qualified independent mechanic remains essential, whether the aircraft is located in Belgium, the Netherlands or elsewhere in Europe.

Ultimately, the country of purchase matters less than the actual condition of the aircraft and how well it has been maintained. Comparing listings from both markets widens your options and gives you a stronger negotiating position, whether on price or on the terms of the transaction.
