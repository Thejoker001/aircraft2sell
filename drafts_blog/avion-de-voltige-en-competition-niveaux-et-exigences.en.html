Competition Aerobatic Aircraft: Levels and Requirements

From regional levels to international championships: competition categories, the technical requirements placed on the aircraft, and the qualification needed to fly in competitive aerobatics.

Competition levels in aerobatic flying

Competitive aerobatics is organised into progressive tiers, from introductory levels up to the highest international standard. National aerobatics federations, affiliated to the International Aerobatic Commission (CIVA) of the Fédération Aéronautique Internationale (FAI), generally structure competitions into several categories of increasing difficulty: an introductory or Sportsman level, an Intermediate level, an Advanced level, and finally the Unlimited level, reserved for the most accomplished pilots and aircraft.

Each level requires a known compulsory programme and a free programme, whose technical difficulty and load-factor demands increase significantly from one tier to the next. Competitions run at club level, then national championship level, and finally continental and world level for pilots flying in the Unlimited category, at events held under the authority of CIVA.

Moving from one level to the next depends both on the pilot's accumulated experience and on the structural and aerodynamic capabilities of the aircraft used: an aircraft suited to introductory competition is not necessarily able to safely fly the programme required at Unlimited level.

Figures, the Aresti catalogue and flight programmes

Aerobatic figures are described and codified using the Aresti system, an internationally recognised graphical notation that unambiguously represents the flight path of a figure (loop, roll, spin, combined figures) regardless of the pilot's or judge's native language. Each figure in the Aresti catalogue carries a difficulty coefficient, used to calculate the overall score of a flight programme.

Known programmes are identical for all competitors in the same category at a given competition, allowing direct comparison of execution accuracy. Free programmes, on the other hand, are composed by each pilot within the rules of their category, which leaves room for creativity while respecting the technical limits of the aircraft used.

Good to know — Scoring in an aerobatic competition relies on the judgement of qualified judges, who assess the geometric accuracy of each figure (axes, symmetry, consistency) rather than its theoretical difficulty alone.

The aerobatic aircraft: technical requirements

An aircraft intended for aerobatic competition must meet structural requirements far beyond those of a standard touring aircraft. Its airframe is designed to withstand very high positive and negative load factors, generated by sequences of violent manoeuvres, with a structural safety margin suited to repeated, intensive use.

Aircraft used in top-level competition (Extra 300, Zivko Edge 540, MXS, CAP 232, among other well-known references) share common characteristics: a very favourable power-to-weight ratio, direct, low-leverage flight controls for precise handling, a fuel tank and system designed for extended inverted flight, and an engine and propeller optimised to run in any attitude.

  • High structural strength — designed to absorb positive and negative load factors far higher than those of a conventional touring aircraft.
  • Fuel and lubrication systems suited to inverted flight — essential to keep the engine supplied regardless of the aircraft's attitude.
  • Direct, responsive flight controls — for precise execution of figures scored by judges.
  • Reinforced seats and safety systems — seats, multi-point harnesses and an emergency parachute suited to aerobatic flying.
Point of caution — The competition level you are aiming for directly determines the aircraft you need: an aircraft designed for introductory categories does not have the structural margin required for an Unlimited programme. Always check the aircraft's technical data sheet and its competition history before buying.

Pilot qualification and training

Beyond a basic pilot licence, flying in aerobatic competition requires specific, progressive, supervised training with a qualified aerobatics instructor, covering knowledge of the aircraft's structural limits, management of spatial disorientation, incident recovery procedures (spin, dynamic stall) and correct use of the emergency parachute.

Progression between competition categories is generally accompanied by experience and technical validation requirements set by the relevant national federation, which may require a check flight or sponsorship by an experienced pilot before allowing entry at a higher level.

Tip — Never try to progress faster than your training allows: most incidents in competitive aerobatics occur when attempting figures beyond the level the pilot has actually mastered.

Preparing and maintaining a competition aircraft

An aerobatic aircraft used in competition is subjected to mechanical stresses far greater than touring use, requiring reinforced maintenance monitoring: regular structural inspections, particular attention to flight-control attachment points, checks for play in joints, and rigorous tracking of engine hours (SMOH) given the demanding speeds and attitudes flown.

  • Logbook detailing hours flown specifically in aerobatics, distinct from cruise flight hours.
  • History of structural inspections and the condition of control attachment points after each competition season.
  • Tracking of the service life limit of the emergency parachute and other life-limited safety equipment.

When buying a used aerobatic aircraft for competition, a pre-purchase inspection by a mechanic specialised in this type of aircraft is even more critical than for a conventional touring aircraft, given the specific structural demands of this discipline.

Budget and the cost of entering competition

The cost of entering aerobatic competition varies significantly depending on the level targeted and the type of aircraft. A used aircraft suited to introductory categories represents a far more accessible budget than a specialised Unlimited-category aircraft, whose price and maintenance cost remain close to those of the most capable aircraft on the market. Recurring costs specific to the discipline add up as well: ongoing training with a qualified instructor, competition licences through the federation, entry fees for events, and reinforced aircraft maintenance.

Tip — Many pilots start competing by renting or co-owning an aircraft suited to the introductory level, before considering buying a personal aircraft once their progression towards higher levels is confirmed.

Pitfalls to avoid when buying

The most common mistakes when buying a used aerobatic aircraft for competition are underestimating structural wear from intensive use, neglecting to verify the precise history of aerobatic hours relative to total hours, or acquiring an aircraft that is over-specified for the level actually targeted, leading to unnecessary extra maintenance costs.

Always compare several aircraft in the same category before deciding, and have a professional independent of the seller confirm the consistency between the maintenance logbook, the declared competition history and the actual condition found during inspection.

Tip — Always ask the seller or the relevant federation for the aircraft's competition history (events flown, level reached): it usefully complements the maintenance logbook when assessing the aircraft's real use.
