Twin-Turbine Helicopter Training: Path and Cost

Prerequisites, the type rating path, a realistic timeline and the budget needed to fly a twin-turbine helicopter.

Why move up to a twin-turbine

Stepping up from a single-engine turbine helicopter to a twin-turbine is a major milestone for both private owner-pilots and career aviators. The main driver is engine redundancy: if one engine fails, a twin-turbine can usually continue controlled flight to a suitable landing area, whereas a single-engine failure forces an immediate autorotation. That redundancy opens the door to missions a single-engine aircraft simply cannot fly, such as operations over congested or built-up areas, offshore work, air ambulance missions, or any operation where the applicable performance category requires an engine-out safety margin.

Beyond the safety case, twin-turbine helicopters also tend to be heavier, faster and more heavily equipped, which changes the workload in the cockpit: managing two engines, redundant systems, type-specific failure procedures, and typically denser avionics. Twin-turbine training is therefore not just about learning a new airframe — it is about adopting a more disciplined approach to flight management overall.

Prerequisites before you start

Before enrolling in a twin-turbine type rating, a pilot already needs a valid helicopter licence appropriate to the intended use (private or commercial), plus meaningful experience logged on single-engine turbines. Training providers and insurers generally require a substantial amount of prior helicopter flight time before accepting a candidate into a twin-turbine course, though the exact threshold varies by aircraft type, intended mission, and the specific school or insurer involved.

Many providers also look for recent experience — hours flown in the months leading up to the course — rather than simply a large total logged years earlier. The fundamentals of helicopter handling need to be fresh before adding the complexity of a second engine and additional redundant systems on top.

Good to know — Exact minimum hours and experience requirements depend on the applicable regulation, the specific aircraft type, and the insurer: always confirm them directly with the relevant authority and the training provider before planning your career path.

The type rating path

A twin-turbine type rating is generally built from several complementary building blocks. Ground school covers the systems specific to the airframe: powerplant architecture, electrical and hydraulic systems, fuel system, performance limitations, and the engine-failure procedures unique to the type. This phase ends with a theoretical exam that validates systems knowledge before moving to flight training.

Practical training most often starts in a full-flight simulator when the school has access to one for the type, which allows engine failures, emergency procedures and systems management to be drilled intensively without the cost or risk of doing so in the real aircraft. Time on the actual helicopter then consolidates normal procedures and validates competence in real operating conditions, under the supervision of an instructor qualified on that specific type.

  • Ground school covering the systems specific to the target twin-turbine type.
  • Simulator sessions focused on engine failures and emergency procedures.
  • Flight training on the real aircraft to build operational proficiency.
  • A final skill test with an examiner qualified on the type.

The path concludes with a skill test, conducted with an examiner, which confirms the pilot's ability to operate the type autonomously and safely.

How long training actually takes

Training duration depends heavily on the aircraft type, the pilot's prior experience, and how readily the school's simulator slots are available. A pilot already comfortable with modern integrated avionics and experienced on a single-engine turbine will typically progress faster than someone learning both a twin-turbine airframe and a dense glass cockpit at the same time.

In practice, an intensive course often runs over several weeks, but the timeline can stretch considerably longer when training is spread around a full-time job or when simulator availability is limited. It is better to plan in some scheduling flexibility than to aim for an overly ambitious fixed date that ends up compromising the quality of the learning.

The real cost of twin-turbine training

A twin-turbine type rating costs noticeably more than training on a single-engine turbine, for several reasons: the hourly rate of flying a twin-turbine is well above that of a single-engine machine, simulator time is itself a significant expense, and instruction from an instructor qualified on that specific type also carries a premium compared with standard training aircraft.

Cost itemWhat it covers
Ground schoolClassroom instruction on type-specific systems, materials, theory exam
Simulator sessionsEngine failures and emergency procedures practiced without risk or airframe wear
Flight hours on the real aircraftThe largest single cost item, billed at a far higher rate than a single-engine turbine
Type-rated instructorSpecialist instruction, usually billed separately from flight time
Final skill testSession with an examiner to confirm the rating

Altogether, the total budget for a twin-turbine type rating typically runs into the tens of thousands of euros, depending on the target airframe, the chosen school, and the number of hours needed to reach the required standard. Always ask for an itemised quote before committing — hourly rates and simulator access vary significantly between providers.

Careers and typical uses of a twin-turbine

For a career pilot, a twin-turbine rating opens the door to higher value-added missions: air ambulance work, offshore operations, executive and VIP transport, and any operation that legally requires engine redundancy over congested or maritime areas. It is also often a required step toward captaincy on heavier types.

For a private owner-pilot, a twin-turbine is more often a choice driven by comfort, performance and safety margin for demanding personal or business travel, particularly for routes that regularly cross terrain offering few safe landing options in the event of a failure.

Choosing the right training provider

The choice of training provider has a major impact on both the quality and the final cost of the rating. Check the real availability of a simulator dedicated to the target type, the instructors' actual experience on that specific twin-turbine, and the school's reputation among former students or prospective employers if the goal is a professional career.

  • Real, bookable availability of a simulator dedicated to the target type.
  • Instructors with genuine experience on that specific twin-turbine.
  • An itemised quote with no hidden costs discovered mid-course.
  • A school reputation that holds up with employers if the goal is a professional career.

Keeping your rating current

A twin-turbine type rating is not a one-time achievement: it has to be maintained through regular flying and periodic skill tests, with frequency depending on the applicable regulation and how the aircraft is used. A pilot who flies infrequently on their twin-turbine type risks losing sharpness, particularly in handling engine failures, which remains the central challenge of this kind of rating.

Scheduling recurrent training, including simulator sessions wherever possible, keeps proficiency high and builds the confidence needed to handle a degraded situation that, by nature, happens rarely but has to be handled flawlessly when it does.
