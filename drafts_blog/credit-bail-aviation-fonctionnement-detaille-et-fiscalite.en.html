Aviation Finance Lease: How It Works and the Tax Rules

How an aviation finance lease (crédit-bail) actually works to finance an aircraft or business jet: legal mechanism, purchase option, accounting treatment, tax rules, and how it compares to a traditional loan.

Aviation finance lease: the general principle

A finance lease, often called crédit-bail in French-speaking markets, is a financing structure in which a specialized leasing company (the lessor) buys the aircraft chosen by the operator and then makes it available in exchange for periodic rental payments over a set term. At the end of the contract, the operator holds a purchase option allowing them to acquire the aircraft for a residual value fixed at signature.

This structure differs from a traditional loan on one key point: the operator is not the legal owner of the aircraft during the contract, even though they have exclusive use of it, insure it and are responsible for its maintenance. It allows a light aircraft, a turboprop or a business jet to be financed without immediately tying up the cash needed for an outright purchase, while keeping the option to become the owner later.

Legal mechanism: who owns the aircraft during the contract

Legally, the lessor remains the owner of the aircraft for the entire duration of the finance lease. Depending on the jurisdiction, the lessor may appear as the registered owner on the certificate of registration, while the operator holds a contractual right of use. This separation between legal ownership and economic use has direct practical consequences: the operator generally needs the lessor's approval for any significant modification to the aircraft, and the contract spells out maintenance and insurance obligations, as well as return conditions if the purchase option is not exercised.

The contract also sets the rental payments (usually monthly or quarterly), any indexation, the total term of the commitment (commonly 5 to 10 years for a business jet), and the residual value that will determine the final purchase option price.

The purchase option at the end of the contract

At the end of the rental period, the operator has three possible outcomes: exercise the purchase option and become the owner of the aircraft for the residual value set in the contract, return the aircraft to the lessor at no extra cost if the contract allows it, or in some structures, renew the lease for a new period.

  • The residual value is fixed at signature, not negotiated at the end of the lease.
  • Exercising the option transfers full legal ownership to the operator, who can then resell or re-register the aircraft freely.
  • Not exercising the option means returning the aircraft in the condition defined in the contract (engine hours, SMOH, logbook condition).

This choice largely depends on the aircraft's actual market value at maturity compared with the contractual residual value: if the residual value is lower than the estimated resale value, exercising the option is generally advantageous.

Accounting treatment: off-balance-sheet or restated

The accounting treatment of a finance lease depends on the framework applied by the operating company. Under traditional local accounting standards in some countries, finance lease payments are recorded as operating expenses and the commitment appears only in the notes, without the asset or the corresponding financial liability showing on the balance sheet — this is referred to as off-balance-sheet treatment.

Under IFRS (IFRS 16) or for companies required to restate leases analytically, the leased aircraft must instead be restated: it is recognized as an asset representing a right of use, with a corresponding financial liability equal to the present value of the remaining lease payments. This restatement changes important financial ratios (leverage, return on assets) and should be anticipated by any company that may be consolidated or audited under these standards.

Good to know — The exact accounting treatment depends on the framework applicable to your structure (local standards, IFRS, company size): have the structure validated by your accountant before signing, as rules differ meaningfully between jurisdictions.

Tax rules for an aviation finance lease

From a tax perspective, the main advantage usually sought in a finance lease is the deductibility of lease payments from the operator's taxable income, provided the aircraft's use is linked to a business activity (transport, flight training, leasing to third parties, demonstrable business use). This deductibility may apply in full or be capped under local tax rules on luxury goods or touring aircraft, with some jurisdictions limiting the deduction when business use is not clearly demonstrated.

VAT also follows a specific regime under a finance lease: it generally applies to each rental payment rather than to the full purchase price at once, which can represent a cash-flow advantage for the operator compared with an outright purchase. The treatment of the final purchase option (often subject to VAT on the residual value) and the recovery rules vary considerably depending on the country of registration, the operator's VAT status, and the aircraft's actual use.

We deliberately do not quote precise rates or caps here: these parameters change regularly and differ by tax jurisdiction. Specialized aviation tax advice remains essential before finalizing a structure.

Finance lease or a traditional loan: which to choose

The choice between a finance lease and a traditional bank loan mainly depends on the operator's financial structure and the intended use of the aircraft. A traditional loan makes the buyer the immediate owner: the aircraft appears on the balance sheet from the purchase date, and generally only the loan interest, not the principal, is actually deductible.

For an operator planning to refresh their fleet regularly or who wants to preserve borrowing capacity for other investments, a finance lease offers more flexibility. For an owner planning to keep the aircraft long-term and who values immediate full ownership, a traditional loan is often simpler to structure.

Points to watch before signing

Before committing to an aviation finance lease, several clauses deserve close attention: the exact return conditions (expected condition, maximum tolerated engine hours and SMOH, up-to-date logbook documentation), early termination penalties, how maintenance and airworthiness obligations (certificate of airworthiness, scheduled major overhaul) are split between lessor and operator, and residual value guarantee clauses in case of a weaker market at maturity.

  • Check who bears the cost of a major overhaul or a significant engine replacement occurring during the lease term.
  • Compare several aviation finance lease providers: residual value and term conditions vary significantly.
  • Have a traditional loan quoted in parallel to compare the real total cost over the term, not just the displayed monthly payment.

An aviation finance lease remains a relevant tool for preserving cash flow and keeping flexibility, provided the legal, accounting and tax mechanics are clearly understood before committing for several years.
