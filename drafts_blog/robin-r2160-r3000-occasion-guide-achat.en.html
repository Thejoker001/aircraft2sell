The R2160 and the R3000, two variants of the same family

The Robin R2160 and the Robin R3000 are two light single-engine aircraft built by the French manufacturer Robin, carrying on a long tradition of aircraft combining wood-and-fabric construction with a metal airframe, a hallmark of the brand. The R2160, often known commercially as the "Acrobin" in some of its versions, was designed to withstand higher load factors and allow light aerobatics, in addition to classic touring and training use. The R3000, a more recent design within the Robin range, follows a similar philosophy while positioning itself more as a versatile touring and training aircraft.

Both models share a low wing, good cabin visibility and a reputation for ruggedness typical of Robin aircraft, making them safe bets on the French and broader European used market, particularly for flying clubs and individual owners looking for an economical aircraft to operate.

What are the differences between the R2160 and the R3000

The main difference between the two models lies in their original purpose. The R2160 was certified to allow certain light aerobatic maneuvers, which implies a reinforced structure and load-factor limits suited to that use. The R3000, on the other hand, is primarily designed as a touring and training aircraft, with no stated aerobatic purpose, resulting in an airframe optimized for that role rather than for resisting aerobatic stresses.

For a buyer, this distinction matters: a pilot who wants to practice light aerobatics in full regulatory compliance should look toward an R2160 whose technical file confirms this capability, while a pilot simply looking for an economical touring or training aircraft can be satisfied with either model depending on market availability.

What budget to plan for

The price of a used R2160 or R3000 depends above all on the age of the airframe, the total airframe and engine hours, the remaining engine potential before the next major overhaul, and the level of avionics on board. The oldest examples, with reduced engine potential and basic avionics, trade at a noticeably more accessible entry level than recently overhauled and re-equipped aircraft.

Beyond the purchase price, your budget should also include additional costs: a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic familiar with the Robin brand, registration and transfer fees, insurance, and potentially an avionics or cabin interior refresh.

Points to check before buying

As with any used aircraft combining wood-and-fabric construction, a pre-purchase inspection carried out by an independent mechanic qualified on the type is essential before finalizing a transaction. For an R2160 or an R3000, certain points deserve particular attention.

  • Condition of the wood structure and fabric covering: look for any trace of moisture, rot or previous undocumented repairs.
  • Complete engine history: hours elapsed since the last major overhaul (SMOH), compliance with the manufacturer's maintenance program, traceability of replaced parts.
  • For an R2160, consistency between the declared use (aerobatic or not) and the structural condition found during the inspection.
  • Validity of the certificate of airworthiness (CofA) and compliance with airworthiness directives applicable to the type.
  • Complete logbook and technical file, with no grey areas in the aircraft's history.
Good to know — Never rely solely on the seller's statements about the general condition of a wood-and-fabric structure: a written independent inspection report, including a check of the fabric covering and the spars, remains the best protection against an unpleasant surprise after the purchase.

Training, light aerobatics and touring: which aircraft for which use

The choice between the R2160 and the R3000 should first be based on the intended real use. For a pilot or a flying club wishing to offer light aerobatic introduction alongside classic training, the R2160 remains the natural reference within the Robin range, provided the specific example has the right configuration and supporting documentation.

For use mainly oriented toward personal touring or private pilot training with no aerobatic purpose, the R3000 offers a relevant balance of operating cost, simplicity and comfort, following the same economic logic as other light single-engine aircraft on the used market.

Maintenance and operating costs

The operating cost of an R2160 or an R3000 remains broadly comparable to that of other light single-engine aircraft in the same category, with one specific point tied to the wood-and-fabric structure: regular maintenance of the fabric covering and careful monitoring of the wood's condition are necessary to preserve the aircraft's airworthiness over time. The main expense items remain the mandatory annual inspection, insurance, parking, and in the longer term, the major engine overhaul once the hourly potential is reached.

Having the aircraft maintained at an approved workshop familiar with Robin structures helps limit unpleasant surprises and preserves its resale value. Rigorous, well-documented maintenance tracking is a strong argument at a future resale.

Tip — Always ask for the last three maintenance invoices and the latest fabric-covering inspection report before making an offer: they give an accurate picture of how seriously the aircraft has been maintained.

Resale and the used market

The R2160 and the R3000 benefit from an active used market in France and more broadly across Europe, supported by a significant installed base among flying clubs and good parts availability through the maintenance network familiar with the Robin brand. A well-maintained aircraft, with a transparent history and fabric covering in good condition, generally resells more easily and under better conditions than one whose technical file has grey areas.

Comparing several listings of similar models remains the best way to assess a fair price, both for buying and for preparing a future resale. Price gaps between two aircraft of the same model year are almost always explained by the remaining engine potential and the actual condition of the wood-and-fabric structure.
