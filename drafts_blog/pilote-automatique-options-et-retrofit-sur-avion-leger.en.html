Autopilot: Options and Retrofit for Light Aircraft

Two-axis or three-axis autopilot, integration with a GPS/EFIS, retrofit costs and compatibility with your aircraft: what you need to know before equipping or buying a light aircraft with an autopilot.

Why fit a light aircraft with an autopilot

Long reserved for airliners and business jets, the autopilot has become widely available on light aircraft over the past fifteen years. On a touring single-engine or a light twin, it significantly reduces pilot workload during cruise phases, particularly on long flights, IFR flights or night flights, by holding heading and altitude steady.

Beyond comfort, the safety benefit is real: a reliable autopilot lets the pilot focus on navigation, traffic awareness and radio communication rather than pure hand-flying, which reduces mental workload during high-information phases of flight. It is also a meaningful resale argument — an aircraft fitted with a modern autopilot generally commands a better price than a comparable aircraft without one.

Two-axis or three-axis autopilot: what's the difference

A two-axis autopilot (roll and pitch) controls the ailerons and elevator: it holds a heading and an altitude, or a climb/descent rate. This is the most common configuration on retrofitted light single-engines, and it is sufficient for the vast majority of touring missions.

A three-axis autopilot adds control of the rudder (yaw), which improves turn coordination and stability, particularly on twins where asymmetric yaw in the event of an engine failure can be partially managed by the system. This configuration is more complex and more costly to install, but it brings a genuine gain in comfort and safety on aircraft suited to it.

Good to know — The choice between two-axis and three-axis depends above all on your mission profile and your aircraft: a modern two-axis system coupled to GPS already covers the needs of a typical touring single-engine very well.

The main options on the market

The light aircraft autopilot market has settled around a few product families, from entry-level systems for microlights and light aircraft to high-end integrated systems for touring single-engines and twins.

  • Entry-level systems — designed for microlights and recreational light aircraft, with basic functions (heading and altitude hold) and simplified integration.
  • All-in-one retrofit systems — designed specifically for installation on an aircraft already in service, with dedicated autopilot servers and controls integrated into the existing instrument panel.
  • Systems integrated into modern avionics suites — the autopilot is driven directly from the multi-function display (EFIS) and natively coupled to the GPS flight plan, approach guidance and altitude management.

In every case, the choice of system depends heavily on the avionics already fitted in the aircraft: a modern autopilot makes full use of a modern GPS/EFIS, whereas an aircraft that has remained on analogue avionics will often need a parallel upgrade to unlock the advanced functions (coupled navigation, automated approach guidance).

Retrofitting an existing aircraft

Installing an autopilot on an aircraft that did not originally have one is one of the most sought-after avionics retrofits on the used aircraft market, as it clearly increases both the usability and the resale value of the aircraft without touching the airframe or the engine.

The retrofit generally involves installing servo motors on the relevant flight controls (ailerons, elevator, and rudder for a three-axis system), one or more computers, and a control interface in the instrument panel. The work must be carried out by an approved workshop, under an approved modification (a Supplemental Type Certificate or equivalent depending on the aircraft and jurisdiction), with the corresponding update to the aircraft's airworthiness records.

  • Confirm that an approved modification exists for your specific aircraft/retrofit kit combination before committing any funds.
  • Plan for the aircraft to be grounded during installation, typically from several days to a few weeks depending on system complexity.
  • Request a detailed quote covering labour, wiring and any structural work needed for the servo installations.
Point of caution — Never commit to an autopilot retrofit without first confirming with the approved workshop that an approved modification exists for your exact aircraft type: not every aircraft/system combination is certified.

Compatibility with your existing avionics

Compatibility is the point buyers most often underestimate. A modern autopilot communicates constantly with the navigation GPS to follow a flight plan, with the heading indicator or EFIS for attitude reference, and sometimes with the transponder or a traffic awareness system. An aircraft fitted with older instruments or a non-compatible GPS can severely limit the functions that are actually usable, even with a recent, capable autopilot.

Before any retrofit project, it is advisable to have the approved workshop draw up a precise list of compatible equipment (the exact GPS model, attitude indicator, and EFIS where relevant) and identify any upgrades needed to get a fully functional system rather than a basic heading-hold function only.

Budget and costs to expect

The cost of an autopilot varies considerably depending on the type of aircraft, the level of integration sought and the avionics already installed. An entry-level system for a microlight or a simple light aircraft represents the lowest investment, while a fully integrated three-axis system on a modern avionics suite in a touring single-engine or a twin sits in a noticeably higher bracket, installation included.

Beyond the equipment price, the budget needs to include the approved workshop's labour, the aircraft's downtime, and any upgrade to related equipment (GPS, attitude indicator) needed to make full use of the new system. Getting several detailed quotes from approved workshops remains the best way to assess the real budget for your aircraft.

Tip — Always weigh the cost of a full retrofit against the expected gain in resale value: on an aircraft you plan to keep for several years, a modern autopilot often pays for itself over the ownership period.

Buying an aircraft already fitted with an autopilot

When buying a used aircraft already fitted with an autopilot, several points deserve specific attention during the pre-purchase inspection: proper operation of every mode (heading hold, altitude hold, coupled navigation), the absence of recurring fault messages at start-up, and the presence in the airworthiness records of documents justifying the original installation or the retrofit (modification certification, installation invoices, maintenance history of the system).

  • Request an in-flight test of every autopilot mode, not just a ground demonstration.
  • Check consistency between the installed system and the modification documents present in the aircraft's file.
  • Ask the seller about any history of faults or repairs to the system, and cross-check the answer against the maintenance logbook.

How to choose: our recommendations

Choosing an autopilot, whether buying an aircraft already fitted with one or planning a retrofit, should start with your mission profile: short local flights, regular IFR touring, or operating a twin or a turboprop do not all call for the same level of sophistication. For regular touring use, a modern GPS-coupled two-axis system is often the best compromise between cost, reliability and real workload reduction.

In every case, get a detailed quote from an approved workshop, confirm that an approved modification exists for your exact aircraft, and check compatibility with the rest of your avionics before committing, whether for a retrofit or for buying an aircraft already fitted with an autopilot.

Tip — If you are buying an aircraft already fitted with an autopilot, insist on a full in-flight test before signing: it is the best way to confirm the system actually works properly in the flight conditions relevant to you.
