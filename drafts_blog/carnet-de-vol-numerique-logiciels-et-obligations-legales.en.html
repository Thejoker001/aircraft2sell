Digital Flight Logbooks: Software and Legal Requirements

Available software, legal standing with the authorities, migrating from a paper logbook and backup best practices: everything you need to know before switching to a digital flight logbook.

Why switch to a digital logbook

Digital flight logbooks are winning over a growing number of private and professional pilots, for practical reasons as much as regulatory ones. They remove the risk of loss or physical damage, automatically total hours by aircraft type, by function (PIC, co-pilot, instruction) and by regulatory category (night, IFR, and so on), and generate the summaries needed for a licence or rating renewal in a few clicks.

For an aircraft owner tracking several pilots, or a pilot flying several different machines, centralising flight hours digitally also makes it easier to cross-check against the aircraft's technical logbook and engine hours — a classic point of scrutiny when reselling a used aircraft.

Legal framework: what the rules actually say

European regulation (Part-FCL, Annex I to Regulation (EU) No 1178/2011) requires every pilot to record their flights in a personal flight logbook, but does not mandate a specific medium: nothing prevents a digital format, provided the data it contains remains complete, reliable, and can be produced in a readable form if requested by the competent authority or an examiner.

In practice, most national civil aviation authorities accept a flight logbook kept exclusively in electronic form, provided it remains accessible at all times (including offline) and can be printed or exported when needed — for instance during a ramp check or when submitting a file for a rating. Some airlines and flight schools, however, still require a paper register or a regularly archived export in addition, particularly for student pilots.

Good to know — Exact requirements can vary between national authorities and change over time: check with your civil aviation authority or training organisation on the current position applicable to your licence before switching fully to digital.

Overview of digital logbook software

The market for digital flight logbook software has grown considerably in recent years, ranging from a simple personal spreadsheet to full platforms combining flight planning, weather and regulatory tracking.

  • Apps built into an EFB (such as ForeFlight, SkyDemon) — the logbook is partly filled in automatically from GPS flight data, with a ready-to-present PDF export.
  • Dedicated logbook software (such as LogTen, MyFlightbook) — focused on detailed tracking of hours, ratings, medical deadlines and regulatory duties, with automatic totals by category.
  • Fleet and club-oriented solutions — designed for flying clubs and operators managing several pilots and aircraft, with automatic cross-checking between the pilot logbook and the aircraft's technical logbook.
  • Spreadsheets and home-made solutions — flexible and free, but with no guarantee of data integrity or automatic backup; best reserved for occasional use or as a supplement to another tool.

The right choice depends above all on how you fly: an occasional private pilot will favour simplicity and integration with their usual navigation app, while a professional pilot or instructor will need finer tracking of regulatory deadlines and exports compatible with the formats required by their employer or authority.

Legal standing and acceptance by authorities

A digital flight logbook only has evidentiary value if it guarantees the integrity of the data entered: reliable timestamps, traceability of any edits, and ideally a signature or validation by the instructor or examiner for the relevant flights (dual instruction, proficiency checks). Most serious products on the market build in these features, but not all to the same standard.

  • Check that the app keeps an edit history and does not allow silent rewriting of past entries.
  • Favour a dated, structured PDF export that an authority or employer can accept directly without reformatting.
  • For flights requiring a signature (instruction, check rides), verify the electronic validation feature offered by the app.
Tip — If you are unsure whether a digital format will be accepted for a specific process (licence renewal, job application), get written confirmation from the relevant body before relying solely on your digital logbook.

Migrating from paper to digital

Migrating from a paper logbook to a digital solution requires care so as not to lose the history built up over the years — something that can matter a great deal when reselling an aircraft or applying for a flying job.

  • Scan or photograph every page of the original paper logbook in high resolution before entering anything, and keep this copy independently of whichever app you choose.
  • Enter cumulative totals by aircraft type and category as of the switchover date, rather than re-entering every historical flight if the volume is large.
  • Keep the original paper logbook: it remains the reference document in the event of a dispute or an audit covering a period before the switchover.

For a pilot with several hundred or thousand hours, this step can take several hours of work, but it avoids gaps in the history that would otherwise complicate any regulatory process or the sale of a co-owned aircraft, where cross-checking each pilot's hours is closely scrutinised.

Backup, security and data longevity

A digital flight logbook is only useful if it survives a broken phone, a discontinued online service or a change of device. Good backup practices matter here just as much as the choice of software itself.

  • Favour a solution with automatic cloud synchronisation rather than local storage only.
  • Export a full PDF or spreadsheet backup regularly (at least once a year), stored in a location independent of the software vendor.
  • Check the data recovery terms in case the vendor shuts down or changes its business model.
Point of caution — Never rely on a single copy on a single device: a lost flight logbook represents a significant rebuilding effort, sometimes impossible to reconstruct accurately after several years.

Best practices and pitfalls to avoid

The most common mistakes with a digital flight logbook relate more to entry discipline than to the technology itself: flights not logged the same day and later forgotten, inconsistencies between the pilot logbook and the aircraft's technical logbook, or no backup export taken for many months.

Another common pitfall is switching to a new app without first checking whether a full, structured data export is possible, which can lead to losing part of the history during migration to a new tool.

Tip — Get into the habit of logging each flight the same day, and set a quarterly reminder to check the consistency between your digital logbook and the technical logbook of the aircraft you've been flying.
